# Battle Cards & War Gaming

Competitive intelligence applied to specific deals. Battle cards arm you for the room; war gaming prepares you for what the competition will do.

---

## Battle Cards

One-page cheat sheets per named competitor. 57% of B2B deals are competitive; 44% of buyers arrive having already identified solutions.

### Types

| Type | When to use | Content focus |
|------|------------|---------------|
| **Discovery/Early-Stage** | First meetings, qualification | Probing questions that expose competitor weaknesses, early framing of "us vs them" |
| **Late-Stage/Depositioning** | Final evaluation, shortlist | Specific talk tracks to counter competitor strengths, proof points |
| **Product/Feature Comparison** | Technical deep-dive | Detailed technical differentiators, architecture comparison |
| **Competitor Overview** | Executive conversations | High-level positioning, strategic differentiation |

### Battle Card Template

```

Last Updated: [Date] | Confidence: [H/M/L]

COMPETITOR SNAPSHOT
  What they do: [1-2 sentences]
  Their positioning: [How they describe themselves]
  Key customers: [Named references in our verticals]
  Pricing model: [What we know]
  Recent moves: [Launches, acquisitions, wins, losses]

OUR DIFFERENTIATORS (ranked by defensibility)
  1. [Differentiator] — [Proof point / evidence]
  2. [Differentiator] — [Proof point / evidence]
  3. [Differentiator] — [Proof point / evidence]

WHAT THEY CANNOT DO (structural — see defensive-positioning.md)
  [One sentence: the limitation they can't fix without becoming a different company]
  Structural reason: [Architecture / Business model / Scope / Maturity / Ecosystem]

WHAT THEY DO WELL (honest — don't pretend they're bad)
  -
  -

LANDMINES TO PLANT EARLY
  Questions to ask the buyer that expose competitor weaknesses:
  - "[Question that surfaces a gap they have]"
  - "[Question about a capability they claim but can't demo]"

WHEN THEY COME UP IN THE ROOM
  If buyer says "We're also talking to [Competitor]":
    "[Prepared response — acknowledge, then pivot to structural differentiator]"

  If buyer says "[Competitor] can do that too":
    "[Prepared response — specificity beats generality]"

  If buyer says "[Competitor] is cheaper":
    "[Prepared response — reframe to TCO or value, not price defense]"

THEIR LIKELY ATTACK ON US
  What they'll say about us: [Known FUD or competitive messaging]
  Our counter: [Specific, evidence-backed response]

SOURCES
  - [Where this intel came from, with dates]
```

### Maintenance

Battle cards are living documents. Refresh when:
- Competitor launches a new product or feature
- Competitor changes pricing
- You lose a deal to this competitor (add what you learned)
- Competitor wins a deal in your target vertical
- Analyst reports update competitive rankings

## War Gaming for Presale

The single most underused presale technique. Simulate what the competitor will say about you in the client's evaluation.

### When to war game

- Deal above $100K with a named competitor in the evaluation
- Entering a new vertical where competitor is entrenched
- Competitor just won a deal in the same account or industry
- You're the challenger trying to unseat an incumbent

### How to run a presale war game (lightweight version)

**Time:** 60-90 minutes. **People:** 3-5 (someone plays us, someone plays the competitor, someone plays the client).

**Structure:**

1. **Brief (10 min):** Share what you know about the deal, the client, and the competitor. Give the "competitor team" their brief — position, pricing, known strengths, likely messaging.

2. **Round 1 — Initial pitch (15 min each):**
   - "Our team" presents their positioning to "the client"
   - "Competitor team" presents theirs
   - "Client" asks questions of both

3. **Round 2 — Counter-positioning (15 min):**
   - "Competitor team" attacks our positioning — what FUD will they spread? What claims will they challenge?
   - "Our team" responds
   - "Client" judges credibility

4. **Debrief (20 min):**
   - Where were we strong? Where were we weak?
   - What did the competitor say that we didn't anticipate?
   - What counter-positioning do we need to prepare?
   - What claims of ours were vulnerable under pressure?

**Outputs:**
- Updated battle card for this competitor
- Prepared counter-positioning for specific attacks
- Landmine questions to plant early in the process
- Identified weak claims to soften or remove
- Updated Honest Q&A Table entries

### War Game Rules

1. **The competitor team must play to WIN.** No soft-pedaling. Their job is to find every vulnerability in your positioning and exploit it.
2. **Use real competitor messaging.** Pull from their website, case studies, analyst reports, and any intelligence from the field.
3. **The client team must be realistic.** They ask the questions a real evaluation committee would ask — technical, commercial, and political.
4. **No sacred cows.** If a claim crumbles under simulated pressure, it will crumble in the real room. Better to find out now.

## Competitive Displacement

When you're trying to unseat an incumbent.

### The Four-Part Framework

1. **Change Trigger** — What has changed that makes the status quo insufficient?
   - New technology requirements the incumbent can't meet
   - New leadership with different vendor preferences
   - Failed project or delivery breakdown by incumbent
   - New regulatory requirements
   - Strategic shift requiring new capabilities

2. **Gap Evidence** — Concrete proof the incumbent can't meet new requirements
   - Their architecture doesn't support [X]
   - They don't have [capability] and haven't announced it
   - They failed to deliver [project] on time/budget

3. **Risk of Inaction** — What it costs to stay with the current vendor
   - Quantify: "Staying with [incumbent] for another year costs [$X] in [missed capability / technical debt / competitive disadvantage]"

4. **Decision Architecture** — Make the switching decision feel structured and low-risk
   - Phased transition plan
   - Pilot alongside incumbent (reduce risk)
   - Clear success criteria at each stage
   - Reversibility guarantee (if pilot fails, no harm done)

### Never attack the incumbent directly

Shift the frame from "who is better" to "what has changed." The client chose the incumbent for a reason. Attacking that choice attacks the client's judgment.

**Good:** "Your requirements have evolved since you selected [incumbent]. The question is whether their platform has evolved with you."

**Bad:** "[Incumbent] is outdated and overpriced."

### Vulnerability Windows

| Signal | What it means | Action |
|--------|--------------|--------|
| Contract renewal date approaching | Client will naturally re-evaluate | Engage 6-9 months before renewal |
| New CIO/CTO hired externally | Brings different vendor preferences | Reach out within 30 days of appointment |
| Failed project by incumbent | Trust eroded, evaluation triggered | Offer limited-scope pilot as alternative |
| New strategic initiative | May require capabilities incumbent lacks | Position on the initiative, not against incumbent |
| Competitor acquisition/merger | Uncertainty about product roadmap, support | Offer stability and continuity narrative |

## CI Discipline Integration

### Source Grading (Admiralty Code)

Every piece of competitive intelligence gets a confidence rating.

**Source Reliability:** A (Completely reliable) → F (Cannot be judged)
**Information Credibility:** 1 (Confirmed by independent sources) → 6 (Cannot be judged)

| Example | Rating | How to use |
|---------|--------|-----------|
| Analyst report states competitor pricing | B2 | High confidence — use in battle card |
| Client mentions competitor's demo failed | C3 | Medium — note but verify with second source |
| LinkedIn post implies competitor layoffs | D4 | Low — monitor, don't act on yet |
| Reddit commenter says competitor is "terrible" | E5 | Noise — ignore |

### Key Intelligence Topics (KITs)

Every competitive research effort should be framed as a specific question tied to a deal decision:

**Good KIT:** "What will [Competitor] demo to [Client] in the final evaluation, and where will they claim superiority?"

**Bad KIT:** "Tell me everything about [Competitor]."

KITs keep research focused and actionable. Open-ended competitive research produces noise.
