# U02 · Graphic design for beginners — working notes

Source: `references/course/U02-L07*.md` through `U02-L21*.md` (5642 · U2). No `BLOCKED.md` exists for this unit — all 15 lessons were read in full: L07–L21.

Colour theory itself (wheel, hue/saturation/brightness, CMYK/RGB, monochromatic/analogous/complementary palette schemes) belongs to `color-theory`. It is flagged in **Overlaps** rather than expanded here; colour is kept in the sections below only where it is doing composition work (visual weight, eye direction, legibility contrast).

## 1. Method steps

The unit teaches one project end to end (nine Instagram templates for the studio "patio"), and the lesson order is the working order.

1. **Read an image literally, then subjectively.** List everything literally present in a source image, then re-read it for personal, less-obvious associations, to decide where a design should sit between literal and subjective. (5642 · U2 · L07 · 06:53–09:36)
2. **Choose a position on the literal–subjective spectrum from the brief**, not from habit — a brief can call for using every familiar cue, or for finding one and exploring it. (5642 · U2 · L07 · 03:57–05:07)
3. **Choose a style position from figurative toward abstract**, using geometry (circle, square, triangle) as the basic vocabulary once the concept is set. (5642 · U2 · L08 · 00:00–02:12)
4. **Practise representing a concept in geometry alone**: circle, square, triangle only, no colour, no stretching; repeat, resize, rotate, and arrange them instead. (5642 · U2 · L08 · 04:21–05:56)
5. **Check the arrangement against Gestalt laws** (pregnance, closure, proximity, figure/ground) before trusting that a viewer will read it as intended. (5642 · U2 · L09 · 00:00–01:56)
6. **Build a mark by working figure and ground together**, not the figure alone — draw a letter and look at what the surrounding negative space suggests. (5642 · U2 · L09 · 04:50–08:17)
7. **Define the project's real output format and pick a tool** before designing — here, nine 1080×1080 Instagram artboards in Figma (Canva offered as a simpler alternative). (5642 · U2 · L10 · 00:00–01:30)
8. **Collect visual references, including non-literal ones**, to find a graphic element and a colour direction rather than only literal matches for the brief. (5642 · U2 · L10 · 03:11–06:57)
9. **Build the artboards at final export size** (Instagram post preset, 1080 × 1080) before placing content. (5642 · U2 · L10 · 06:57–08:18)
10. **Explore the chosen graphic element loosely** — different treatments of the same shape (single, rounded rectangle, overlapped ovals, repeated confetti) — before deciding sizes or placement. (5642 · U2 · L10 · 08:18–10:45)
11. **Choose two typefaces that look different but share proportions** (pairing), and assign each a role — one for headlines, one for body — rather than mixing faces without a reason. (5642 · U2 · L13 · 00:00–02:18; applied at 5642 · U2 · L14 · 00:00–02:56)
12. **Build a typographic scale**: set headline size, then letter spacing, then line spacing, on an artboard at true output size; then repeat for body copy, keeping a strong size difference between the two. (5642 · U2 · L14 · 02:56–04:58, 04:58–06:57)
13. **Check typography for the common mistakes ("crimes")** before moving on: redundant image/text, too many typefaces, stretched or squeezed letterforms. (5642 · U2 · L12 · 00:00–02:12, 02:12–03:02, 03:02–04:44)
14. **Set alignment and line length for the reading direction and type size**, and adjust tracking/leading/kerning for the size in use, rather than trusting a typeface's defaults. (5642 · U2 · L13 · 02:18–03:41, 03:41–05:19)
15. **Build a palette from the same references used for form**, choosing hues that support the chosen typeface and shape rather than defaulting to saturated, obvious colour. (5642 · U2 · L18 · 00:00–04:18)
16. **Test the palette directly on the real canvases**, against the real type and shapes, not in isolation — check legibility of text on every background colour actually in use. (5642 · U2 · L18 · 04:18–06:01, 06:01–08:38)
17. **Check composition for ambiguity first**: an element that is almost-but-not-quite centred or aligned reads as a mistake; make placement clearly deliberate. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 00:00–02:16)
18. **Balance visual weight** (size, position on the page, colour darkness) rather than only lining elements up. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 02:16–04:28)
19. **Build a grid for the set** (columns, gutter, margin) so repeated layouts stay consistent, then align every element to it. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 04:28–06:59; applied at 5642 · U2 · L21 · 00:00–02:16, 06:40–14:00)
20. **Choose a compositional scheme per piece**: one high-contrast focal element, a diagonal for movement, or the rule of thirds — and simplify by removing anything that does not communicate. (5642 · U2 · L20 · 00:00–01:56, 01:56–03:37, 03:37–04:36)
21. **Review the finished set with the grid hidden**, checking the whole feed's rhythm (some posts colour fields, some white space, some text) rather than judging pieces in isolation. (5642 · U2 · L21 · 14:00–17:46)

## 2. Principles and claims

- Graphic design is giving form to an idea; it is not defined by which program is used. (5642 · U2 · L07 · 00:00–02:04)
- Image and text combined can create a meaning neither carries alone ("one plus one becomes three"). (5642 · U2 · L07 · 00:00–02:04)
- There is no inherently good or bad position between literalness and subjectivity; the brief decides. (5642 · U2 · L07 · 03:57–05:07)
- Using every familiar cue for a subject at once makes a design overly literal and redundant. (5642 · U2 · L07 · 03:57–05:07)
- Style runs from figurative to abstract with no fixed end point; the same subject can be represented many ways. (5642 · U2 · L08 · 00:00–02:12)
- A symbol's meaning depends on what the viewer has already learned (a dove reads as peace, a smiley as a face, only by learned convention). (5642 · U2 · L08 · 00:00–02:12)
- Geometric forms give a basic vocabulary for building images without unnecessary ornament. (5642 · U2 · L08 · 02:12–03:30)
- The Bauhaus reduced decorative detail partly to make production cheaper and easier, not only for style. (5642 · U2 · L08 · 02:12–03:30)
- Basic shapes carry felt qualities before they represent anything: a circle reads as kind/rounded, a triangle as sharper/pointed. (5642 · U2 · L08 · 03:30–04:21)
- Gestalt treats an image as many small parts, but changing one part changes how the whole is read. (5642 · U2 · L09 · 00:00–01:56)
- Law of pregnance (Prägnanz): images with fewer, simpler elements are easier to remember than complex ones. (5642 · U2 · L09 · 00:00–01:56)
- Law of closure: the mind completes incomplete forms and fills in what is not actually shown. (5642 · U2 · L09 · 01:56–02:53)
- Law of proximity: elements placed close together read as one group; this is a frequent source of design mistakes (e.g. wayfinding). (5642 · U2 · L09 · 02:53–03:45)
- Law of figure/ground: figure and ground cannot both read as dominant at once, but which one is figure can switch. (5642 · U2 · L09 · 03:45–04:50)
- Typography is almost as central to communicating an idea as the image itself. (5642 · U2 · L11 · 00:00–02:18)
- The broadest typeface split is serif vs. sans-serif; both can be equally legible today, so other qualities (tone, era, context) now distinguish them. (5642 · U2 · L11 · 02:18–03:21)
- Serif type tends to read as elegant, academic, or professional (editorial, fashion use); sans-serif tends to read as modern (its early "grotesque" tag, Bauhaus adoption). (5642 · U2 · L11 · 03:21–04:49)
- Display and script faces carry strong personality and should be used sparingly (headlines, isolated details), not throughout a design. (5642 · U2 · L11 · 04:49–06:11)
- Letterform, placement, and weight can make a word's visual form echo its own meaning (a calligram). (5642 · U2 · L11 · 06:11–07:57)
- The most common typography mistake is redundancy: letting image and text repeat the same message rather than letting them work together. (5642 · U2 · L12 · 00:00–02:12)
- Adding a second typeface needs a clear reason; a single family's own weights (light/bold) can carry emphasis without a new face. (5642 · U2 · L12 · 02:12–03:02)
- Never stretch or squeeze a typeface to fit a space; use a condensed or extended variant of the family, or rotate the text, instead. (5642 · U2 · L12 · 03:02–04:44)
- Pairing works when two visibly different typefaces still share underlying proportions (e.g. similar crossbar heights, similar O shapes). (5642 · U2 · L13 · 00:00–02:18)
- Left alignment is easiest to read for left-to-right readers; centred or right-aligned text is harder to follow in long paragraphs but can work in short, narrow contexts (e.g. a phone-width post). (5642 · U2 · L13 · 02:18–03:41)
- Line length should match type size: not too long for small type, not too short for large type. (5642 · U2 · L13 · 02:18–03:41)
- Very large type needs tighter letter and line spacing than default; very small type needs slightly looser spacing than default. (5642 · U2 · L13 · 03:41–05:19)
- Kerning adjusts spacing for a specific pair of letters beyond the font's general default spacing. (5642 · U2 · L13 · 03:41–05:19)
- A downloaded typeface should be checked for consistent letter weight and full language/kerning support before use. (5642 · U2 · L13 · 05:19–06:09)
- Ambiguity is the worst problem in a composition: an element that is nearly-but-not-quite centred or aligned unsettles the eye more than a clearly deliberate off-centre placement. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 00:00–02:16)
- Balance is a play of weight, size, and position (and colour), not just lining elements up. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 02:16–04:28)
- Elements placed higher on a page, or in a darker colour, carry more visual weight and can be countered by lighter or lower-placed elements. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 02:16–04:28)
- A grid gives repeated designs a shared structure to align against and keeps a series consistent. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 04:28–06:59)
- Building more grid columns than seem necessary gives flexibility — content can span two, three, or all of them. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 04:28–06:59)
- A grid also keeps a set of icons or panel drawings visually consistent with each other. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 04:28–06:59)
- One large, high-contrast focal element can make a composition impossible to miss, without needing a literal illustration of the subject. (5642 · U2 · L20 · 00:00–01:56)
- A diagonal introduces movement into a composition that would otherwise read as only horizontal/vertical. (5642 · U2 · L20 · 01:56–03:37)
- The rule of thirds places key elements at the intersections of a frame divided into thirds; a facing subject should have empty space on the side it looks toward. (5642 · U2 · L20 · 03:37–04:36)
- Simplifying — removing elements that do not communicate anything — is not itself a composition rule, but supports whichever rule is in use. (5642 · U2 · L20 · 03:37–04:36)
- A colour choice does not have to follow an object's literal real-world colour (a tree need not be green). (5642 · U2 · L17 · 00:00–01:19) — colour-theory territory; see Overlaps.
- Colour perception is relative to neighbouring colour, including neighbouring black/white/grey. (5642 · U2 · L15 · 00:00–01:41) — colour-theory territory; see Overlaps.

## 3. Checks the teacher uses

- **Literal vs. subjective list.** Write the literal contents of an image, then a second list of personal associations; a longer/harder-to-produce second list is expected and fine. Needs judgement by eye — the associations are personal, not measurable. (5642 · U2 · L07 · 06:53–09:36)
- **Groups-or-units test for proximity.** Ask whether a set of elements reads as separate groups or as individual units. Could be partly measured from a rendered image: compare the gap between elements within an intended group to the gap between groups (proximity ratio). (5642 · U2 · L09 · 02:53–03:45)
- **Figure/ground read.** Ask which of two interlocking shapes reads as the figure and which as the ground (Carrefour arrow, FedEx arrow). Needs judgement by eye — the answer is meant to be ambiguous/switchable, not fixed. (5642 · U2 · L09 · 03:45–04:50)
- **Redundancy check.** Ask whether the image is showing exactly what the text already says. Needs judgement (reading meaning across two channels), not a pixel measurement. (5642 · U2 · L12 · 00:00–02:12)
- **Distortion check.** Look at a typeface for stretching/squeezing rather than a true condensed or extended cut. Could be measured from a rendered image: compare the letterform's width-to-height ratio against the typeface's own natural proportions. (5642 · U2 · L12 · 03:02–04:44)
- **Centring check with the tool's own guide.** In Figma, use the centre-alignment control and its guide line to confirm an element is actually centred rather than merely close. Measurable directly from the file: element centre coordinate vs. artboard centre coordinate. (5642 · U2 · L21 · 06:40–14:00)
- **Off-centre/ambiguity check.** Look at whether a line or element sits clearly to one side or exactly centred, versus stuck ambiguously in between. Measurable from a rendered image: offset from true centre as a proportion of the available width. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 00:00–02:16)
- **"Does it fall to one side?" balance check.** After placing elements, judge whether the composition pulls toward one edge and needs a counterweight elsewhere. Needs judgement by eye — weight combines size, colour darkness, and position, which the course does not reduce to a formula. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 02:16–04:28)
- **Text-on-background legibility check.** Place the actual headline/body text on each candidate background colour and judge contrast (dark text on blue/green/red judged poor; yellow on blue judged good). Could be measured from a rendered image: contrast between text colour and background colour values. This check is colour-theory territory in substance; see Overlaps. (5642 · U2 · L18 · 04:18–06:01)
- **Font-quality check before use.** Inspect a downloaded font for uneven letter weight (e.g. a heavy O beside a light A) and for missing kerning-pair adjustments. Needs judgement by eye across the full character set; not reducible to one measurement. (5642 · U2 · L13 · 05:19–06:09)

## 6. Tool-specific steps (Figma)

- Open Figma; from the recent-files view, choose **New → Design file** to start a project. (5642 · U2 · L10 · 01:30–03:11)
- A Figma page is an infinite canvas; add pages via the plus sign and name them (e.g. "Posts", "Formas", "Tipografías", "Color"), keeping the project organized by page. (5642 · U2 · L10 · 01:30–03:11; 5642 · U2 · L18 · 00:00–04:18)
- Use the frame/artboard tool and the **Social media → Instagram post** preset (1080 × 1080) to create export-ready artboards. (5642 · U2 · L10 · 06:57–08:18)
- Drawing a shape adds it as a new entry in the layer list; hold **Option** while dragging to duplicate a selected shape (copy/paste also works). (5642 · U2 · L10 · 08:18–10:45)
- To install a new font while Figma is open: close Figma first, double-click the font file and install it at the OS level, then reopen Figma so the face appears in its font list. (5642 · U2 · L14 · 00:00–02:56)
- Set type size, then letter spacing (tracking), then line spacing (leading) as separate, sequential adjustments in the properties panel, judged on an artboard at true output size. (5642 · U2 · L14 · 02:56–04:58, 04:58–06:57)
- In Figma's colour picker, dragging left/right adjusts saturation (toward/away from grey) and dragging toward the corners adjusts brightness (toward white or black); Figma reports the colour as RGB and hex, and keeps a "recently used colours" row. (5642 · U2 · L18 · 00:00–04:18)
- Select an artboard and use its **Fill** property to set a background colour, so text/shape colour combinations can be tested on the actual canvas. (5642 · U2 · L18 · 04:18–06:01)
- Use the corner-radius control (plus arrow keys for fine adjustment) to fix a rounded rectangle's corners after resizing. (5642 · U2 · L18 · 06:01–08:38)
- Use the **Layout Grid** control in the right-hand panel to add a column guide to selected artboards; choose column count, gutter, margin, and grid colour/opacity. (5642 · U2 · L21 · 00:00–02:16)
- Reorder layers (move an image layer below a text layer) to control whether an image sits in front of or behind overlapping text. (5642 · U2 · L21 · 06:40–14:00)
- Use the centre-alignment control; a guide line confirms when a selection is exactly centred. (5642 · U2 · L21 · 06:40–14:00)
- Toggle the layout-grid guides off to review a finished design (or set) without them. (5642 · U2 · L21 · 14:00–17:46)

## Derived

- The 21-step ordering in **Method steps** is my synthesis of the sequence the teacher works through lesson-to-lesson (concept → style/geometry → perception → project setup → typography → typography pitfalls/fixes → colour application → composition/balance → grid → composition schemes → final ordering). No single lesson states "these are the steps of the method" as a list; this is inferred from the lesson order and each lesson's closing transition to the next.
- The **Overlaps** judgement of exactly which sentences in L15–L18 "serve composition" versus belong wholesale to `color-theory` is an editorial line I drew per the brief's instruction to keep colour material out of this skill except where it serves composition. A different reader could draw that line slightly differently (e.g. treat the L20 Bernhard/Amargo "colour directs the eye" examples as colour-theory rather than composition); I kept them above because the teacher introduces them explicitly under composition schemes ("Schemes that work"), not under a colour lesson.
- In **Checks**, whether each check "could be measured from a rendered image" is my assessment, not something the teacher states explicitly as a metric; the teacher only ever judges by eye in the videos. Where I say a check is measurable, I mean it is measurable in principle from pixel/colour/coordinate data, not that the course specifies a threshold or tool to do so.

All 15 lessons of this unit (L07–L21) were read in full; nothing was left unreached.
