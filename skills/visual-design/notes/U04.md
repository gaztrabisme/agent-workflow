# U04 · Composition techniques for graphic design — working notes

Source: `references/course/U04-L44*.md` through `U04-L64*.md` (5642 · U4). No `BLOCKED.md` exists for this unit — all 21 lessons were read in full: L44–L64.

Colour theory itself (hue/saturation/value, primary/secondary/tertiary, warm/cool as a colour-wheel property, Adobe Color harmony rules, relative value) belongs to `color-theory`. It is flagged in **Overlaps** rather than expanded here; colour is kept in the sections below only where it is doing composition work (proportion of emphasis, contrast for legibility, depth cues, hierarchy).

## 1. Method steps

The unit teaches composition theory (L44–L47), then works through the elements of a composition (shape, negative space, colour, typography, iconography, L48–L52), then applies them to two running projects — a magazine ("Revista Yvy") and a photographic portfolio piece ("Barcelona, bordes irregulares") — carried through simplification, balance, focal point, contrast, repetition, symmetry, the rule of thirds, proportional scales and grids (L53–L64). The steps below follow that working order.

1. **Judge a composition by its five-quality checklist** — clarity, balance, contrast, unity, salience (*pregnancia*) — before doing anything else with it. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 00:09–01:37)
2. **Choose a compositional register to match the intended effect**: contrast, hierarchy and diagonal movement for dynamism; symmetry and tighter balance for reflection. (5642 · U4 · L44 · 00:49–01:35)
3. **Start from the message's core** ("DNA") and let the content dictate the visual treatment, not the reverse. (5642 · U4 · L46 · 00:00–01:20)
4. **Build a project mood board on Google Images**: search generically, then by a known reference designer, then by uploading an image to find visually similar ones, saving chosen finds locally. (5642 · U4 · L48 · 09:03–12:04)
5. **Extend the mood board on Pinterest**: create a secret board, save and re-find related pins, then download chosen images into the project's own folder structure. (5642 · U4 · L49 · 06:43–10:01)
6. **Read the project's own material for a colour direction instead of imposing a fixed palette**: extract a theme from a mood-board photo in Adobe Color, compare "all colours," "lighter" and "muted" extraction modes, then name and save the refined palette to a library. (5642 · U4 · L50 · 07:32–08:49, 08:49–11:35)
7. **Choose typefaces by fixing the workhorse text face first**, then testing display candidates against it — matching x-heights and looking for a shared underlying skeleton beneath a contrasting style. (5642 · U4 · L51 · 06:24–07:56, 07:56–11:18)
8. **Analyse the available photographic material's iconography before laying it out**: classify it as descriptive or narrative, locate its motif, and decide whether to read a structure out of the material (when its own axes are strong) or impose one on it (when they are not). (5642 · U4 · L52 · 05:25–07:57, 07:57–08:56)
9. **Simplify a layout by sketching many very small (about 3×1.5 cm) thumbnails**, so the size itself forces a reduction to a plain shape and a fixed place for text before any detail is considered. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 02:56–05:07)
10. **Refine a sketched spread's position and hierarchy**: remove anything unneeded (e.g. an extra subtitle), and push an ambiguously placed element clearly to the edge or to one side. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 05:07–06:29)
11. **Group elements into as few clusters as possible, preferring an odd number**, so gaps read as deliberate breathing room rather than leftover space. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 06:29–07:11)
12. **Align and connect a spread's remaining elements**, duplicating or adding a piece if needed to give the layout a visual centre. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 07:11–08:40)
13. **Test more than one balance/movement treatment for a spread** (diagonal, spiral, left-to-right) and reject any option that hides the material's key motif or exaggerates its counter-shapes. (5642 · U4 · L54 · 04:11–05:01, 05:01–06:10)
14. **Build a visual centre of interest deliberately**, enlarging the dominant cluster or photo and pairing it with a secondary element (e.g. a drop cap) that receives the eye next. (5642 · U4 · L55 · 03:52–06:37)
15. **Iterate a spread's focal point across several passes** — title first, then colour, then resizing a competing element — until the entry point and reading order are unambiguous. (5642 · U4 · L55 · 06:37–13:01)
16. **Sharpen contrast once a focal point exists**: simplify anything still competing with it (a busy map, an oversized headline) and fix technical details (margins, bilingual text alignment, a credit's own size/weight) that were quietly undermining it. (5642 · U4 · L56 · 02:40–05:01, 05:01–06:31, 06:31–08:05)
17. **Check and adjust contrast at the page-sequence (macro) level**, not only within one spread — reorder spreads and add a colour accent to change where visual weight rises and falls. (5642 · U4 · L56 · 08:05–10:31)
18. **Build a repeating graphic module** (a styled text block plus a framed, tinted image) with paragraph and object styles, then duplicate the whole module for further sections rather than rebuilding it by hand. (5642 · U4 · L57 · 02:32–05:19, 05:19–07:31, 07:31–11:39)
19. **Fix a repeated module's small style details once, in the style definition**, so every instance updates together. (5642 · U4 · L57 · 12:43–14:26)
20. **Choose deliberately between symmetry (stillness, contemplation) and asymmetry (content-forward, dynamic reading)**, and let the two coexist rather than picking one dogmatically. (5642 · U4 · L58 · 04:37–05:18)
21. **Apply the rule of thirds to a format**: set margins first, divide the page into three rows and three columns of guides, and place each element (logo, image, cover lines, barcode, folio) against the resulting intersections and thirds. (5642 · U4 · L59 · 02:14–03:52, 03:52–04:58, 04:58–06:24, 06:24–08:27)
22. **Identify the format's own governing proportions** (e.g. a golden 1.618 width, a self-folded 1.25 height) and build a separate Fibonacci or fixed-ratio type scale from each, always matching x-heights before scaling. (5642 · U4 · L60 · 07:58–09:46, 09:46–13:38; 5642 · U4 · L61 · 07:08–07:58, 07:58–09:47)
23. **Derive page margins mathematically from the same governing ratio**, testing candidate proportions against a reference square before committing to one. (5642 · U4 · L62 · 02:12–04:56, 04:56–05:49)
24. **Build the grid on the master pages**: choose a column count that is the lowest common multiple of the layouts needed (12, to allow both 3- and 4-column spreads), then fix the number of text lines wanted in a column and let that number set the exact leading, rather than the other way round. (5642 · U4 · L64 · 01:20–02:55, 02:55–04:48, 04:48–06:48)
25. **Build the baseline grid from that computed leading**, lock body styles to it, adjust gutters by the same governing ratio, generate fields and flow lines with a guide-creation tool, then fit real content to the finished structure. (5642 · U4 · L64 · 06:48–07:19, 07:19–09:29, 09:29–10:24, 10:24–12:22)

## 2. Principles and claims

- Composition is a tool for honoring content; the message dictates how a design should look, not the reverse. (5642 · U4 · L44 · 00:00–00:48)
- A well-achieved composition can hold attention and produce aesthetic pleasure at once. (5642 · U4 · L44 · 00:00–00:48)
- Composition means organizing parts of a language into a whole; the same idea of unity recurs across writing (semantics/syntax), music (harmony/melody/rhythm), the visual arts (unity, weight, rhythm, movement, balance, contrast) and film (framing, lighting, rhythm). (5642 · U4 · L45 · 01:08–01:47, 01:48–02:35, 02:36–03:22, 03:23–04:22)
- A composition achieves unity when varied elements still read as parts of one coherent set. (5642 · U4 · L45 · 04:23–05:49)
- The layout is right only when it honors and emphasizes the content's core ("DNA"). (5642 · U4 · L46 · 00:00–01:20)
- Clarity means a composition's centering, orthogonality and size differences read unambiguously; a hesitant composition reads as insecure. (5642 · U4 · L46 · 01:40–02:56)
- Balance is the compensation of perceptual, not physical, forces; it can happen at rest or in motion, symmetrically or not. (5642 · U4 · L46 · 02:57–04:35)
- Contrast drives rhythm, variety and hierarchy, and enriches a design by distinguishing categories. (5642 · U4 · L46 · 04:36–06:14)
- Contrast produces the unity and salience that together capture and hold a viewer's attention. (5642 · U4 · L46 · 06:15–07:07, 07:08–07:39)
- A bad composition is disorganized, ambiguous, unbalanced, boring, or inconsequential. (5642 · U4 · L46 · 07:40–08:48)
- Every area of design applies the same five qualities (clarity, balance, contrast, unity, salience) differently, according to its medium and context. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 00:09–01:37)
- Logotypes rely on extreme simplicity for visual impact and reliable reproduction. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 01:37–02:44)
- A brand identity repeats a logotype's compositional traits — colour, space, typography, a distinctive mark — across every piece of a system. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 02:45–03:35)
- Posters rely on scale and contrast to force a clear reading hierarchy from a distance. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 03:35–04:53)
- Magazine covers manage dense information through contrast and hierarchy; book covers, free of newsstand urgency, allow more playful, expressive composition. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 04:53–05:55, 05:56–06:29)
- Editorial interiors compose a sequence of pages like film editing: rhythm, dosage, explosion and silence. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 06:29–07:15)
- Web design adds movement, interactivity and responsiveness to the same compositional parameters used in print. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 07:15–08:42)
- A shape is read through two dominant Gestalt principles: totality (the whole outweighs its parts) and simplicity (the mind seeks the plainest reading available). (5642 · U4 · L48 · 00:07–01:51)
- Both symmetry and asymmetry simplify a shape, by making its relation to the vertical/horizontal axes unambiguous — the Gestalt law of Prägnanz. (5642 · U4 · L48 · 02:05–03:15)
- Bertin's six visual variables — shape, size, colour, value, texture, orientation — plus position in space, are the attributes a designer can alter. (frame, confirmed) (5642 · U4 · L48 · 03:16–04:56)
- Five Gestalt grouping principles organize shape in a composition: similarity, proximity, continuity, closure, and figure/ground. (5642 · U4 · L48 · 04:57–06:27, 06:28–07:55, 07:56–08:37)
- A whole composition behaves as a single global shape, governed by the same perceptual principles as any smaller one. (5642 · U4 · L48 · 08:38–09:02)
- No figure exists without a ground, and no ground without figure. (5642 · U4 · L49 · 00:09–01:48)
- Notan (light/dark balance without colour or texture) is a core East Asian compositional concept that shaped European Japonisme's flat colour, off-centre subjects and diagonal axes. (5642 · U4 · L49 · 00:09–01:48)
- Negative space is dynamic, not leftover void: giving it shape, size, orientation and position is a deliberate decision. (5642 · U4 · L49 · 02:06–03:47)
- A figure separates from its ground through contour, value contrast, smaller surface area, convexity, or symmetry. (frame, confirmed) (5642 · U4 · L49 · 03:48–04:52)
- Negative space organizes a piece: it groups or separates elements, builds hierarchy, and paces the sequence in which it is read. (5642 · U4 · L49 · 04:52–06:43)
- Warm colours (red-violet to yellow) advance and expand shapes; cool colours (yellow-green to violet through blue) recede and contract them. (5642 · U4 · L50 · 02:01–02:44)
- The 60-30-10 proportion keeps a palette's emphasis clear, whether applied to three flat colours or to a whole image's extracted palette. (5642 · U4 · L50 · 02:44–03:33)
- Converting a palette to grayscale is a quick way to check whether it has enough contrast, which matters for accessibility, above all between figure and background. (5642 · U4 · L50 · 06:10–07:32)
- Colour alone rarely creates hierarchy; another attribute, such as size or position, usually has to do that work. (5642 · U4 · L50 · 06:10–07:32)
- A colour's identity depends on context and relation, not on the colour in isolation. (5642 · U4 · L50 · 07:32–08:49)
- A letterform is built from positive and negative space together; the negative space (counterform) is as much a design decision as the black shape. (5642 · U4 · L51 · 00:00–00:56)
- Counter size, weight and letterspacing move together: bigger counters read lighter and want looser spacing; smaller counters read heavier and want tighter spacing. (5642 · U4 · L51 · 01:37–02:27)
- Typographic colour is the gray value a text block reads as from a distance, driven by letter, word and line spacing rather than by the letterforms themselves. (5642 · U4 · L51 · 01:37–02:27)
- A type family's variables move along four axes — weight, construction/posture, width, and, less commonly, optical size. (5642 · U4 · L51 · 02:27–04:21)
- Hierarchy can come from size, weight, or both, using either one family's built-in range or a size/weight ladder within it. (5642 · U4 · L51 · 05:01–05:55, 05:55–06:24)
- When combining typefaces, match x-heights, keep the faces easy to tell apart, and look for a shared underlying skeleton beneath a contrasting style. (5642 · U4 · L51 · 06:24–07:56)
- Images are not secondary in a composition — they are read first, and carry direct, fast, emotional weight of their own. (5642 · U4 · L52 · 00:00–00:57)
- An image reduced to very few elements can read as complex too, simply by being unusual. (5642 · U4 · L52 · 00:57–01:43)
- Iconicity places images on a scale from realistic photograph to pure typographic abstraction; high-iconicity images demand the rest of a composition defer to their realism, while abstract images need size or contrast to read at all. (5642 · U4 · L52 · 01:43–02:29)
- Images sort as descriptive (topographic or portrait, often built around a defining motif) or narrative (implying a before and an after). (5642 · U4 · L52 · 02:29–03:27)
- Closing a shot raises abstraction; a downward camera tilt suits the lower part of a frame, an upward tilt the upper part. (5642 · U4 · L52 · 03:27–04:55)
- Bleeding an image past the frame's edge crops toward its key parts and adds a sense of scale. (5642 · U4 · L52 · 04:55–05:25)
- A simple composition captures attention and is transparent, but oversimplifying can make a design too obvious or boring. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 00:09–01:09)
- Reducing a colour palette (60-30-10, and capping colour-based categorisation at five or six colours) is one of the main techniques for simplifying a design. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 01:09–02:56)
- Balance is not the same as stillness: rest and tension are two different states, and both can be balanced. (5642 · U4 · L54 · 00:07–01:16)
- Sharper angles and tighter curves generate more visual force; an oblique position generates directed movement and depth. (5642 · U4 · L54 · 01:16–02:02)
- A small shape near a larger one absorbs its movement; overlapping shapes read as moving into depth. (5642 · U4 · L54 · 01:16–02:02)
- Weight, from size, position, or colour intensity, pulls the rest of a composition toward it. (5642 · U4 · L54 · 02:02–03:12)
- A composition reads clearly when one figure is made dominant and the rest are subordinated to it; trying to make everything equally important makes nothing read clearly. (5642 · U4 · L55 · 00:08–01:27)
- Visual weight comes from difference: size, contrast against the background, warm colour, and position all add weight. (5642 · U4 · L55 · 01:27–02:33)
- Position can reverse the usual reading direction: a focal point placed low, or alternated in position, pulls the eye's path with it. (5642 · U4 · L55 · 02:33–03:52)
- Contrast is a comparison mechanism: it can come from adding attributes to what should stand out, or from removing attributes from everything around it. (5642 · U4 · L56 · 00:00–02:00)
- Composition has opposites beyond the standard visual attributes: style, full vs. empty, horizontal vs. vertical vs. diagonal, image vs. text. (5642 · U4 · L56 · 00:00–02:00)
- Hierarchy is contrast of degree: extreme values plus intermediate nuances, each differing progressively from its neighbours so the whole sequence stays legible. (5642 · U4 · L56 · 02:00–02:40)
- Repetition works through similarity: it clarifies a message by revealing structure once and letting the reader concentrate on content instead of decoding layout. (5642 · U4 · L57 · 00:00–02:32)
- A graphic module is a complete, self-contained pattern; repeating it unifies a design but risks monotony if overused. (5642 · U4 · L57 · 00:00–02:32)
- Repetition operates at more than one level at once: macro (a section's structure), module (a repeating unit), and micro (a colour, a rule line, a weight). (5642 · U4 · L57 · 12:43–14:26)
- Levelling keeps an object on its geometric centre and horizontal/vertical relationship; sharpening shifts it while still working through those same axes; ambiguity is what happens when neither is clearly chosen. (5642 · U4 · L58 · 00:00–01:45)
- Symmetry reads as simple, stable and contemplative, but narrows the room available for emphasis. (5642 · U4 · L58 · 01:45–02:58)
- Asymmetry favours the prominence of content, negative space and dynamic relationships over fixed proportion, but risks inconsistency. (5642 · U4 · L58 · 02:58–03:45)
- Composition carries meaning on its own, apart from any symbol or data in the piece: asymmetry's avant-garde history can read as reflective or sophisticated. (5642 · U4 · L58 · 03:45–04:37)
- The rule of thirds rebalances a composition that is too calm (centred) or too free (unstructured asymmetry), using two vertical and two horizontal guides to create nine zones and four strong intersection points. (5642 · U4 · L59 · 00:00–00:32, 00:32–02:14)
- The rule of thirds is a flexible guide, not a rule demanding exact precision. (5642 · U4 · L59 · 00:32–02:14)
- The golden rectangle's long side is 1.618 (phi) times its short side; phi is irrational, and multiplying any measurement by phi produces a golden-ratio relationship. (frame, confirmed) (5642 · U4 · L60 · 01:16–02:14)
- The Fibonacci sequence, where each term is the sum of the previous two, is proportional to the golden rectangle and can be applied to any numeric value, not only a physical dimension. (5642 · U4 · L60 · 02:58–04:14)
- A Fibonacci type scale requires matching x-heights first, then computing each term as the base size times phi (first term) or the sum of the two previous sizes (later terms). (5642 · U4 · L60 · 07:58–09:46, 09:46–13:38)
- A proportional scale's starting point can be any measurement, such as a printer's required margin, but it can never be zero; once a scale is generated, only some of its intervals need to be used, not all. (5642 · U4 · L61 · 00:30–01:16, 02:13–02:42)
- Every proportioned rectangle traces back to a square folded and subdivided in different ways. (5642 · U4 · L61 · 02:42–03:44)
- The root-2 rectangle (1.4142…), the basis of the DIN paper formats, is the only self-folding proportion: halving it always reproduces the same proportion at the smaller size. (5642 · U4 · L61 · 06:18–07:08)
- A design can carry two useful proportions at once, such as a golden-ratio width paired with a self-folded height, and each can seed its own type scale. (5642 · U4 · L61 · 07:08–07:58, 07:58–09:47)
- Setting the gutter equal to the leading makes the gutter-and-leading gap form a perfect square, a small self-consistency check on the grid. (5642 · U4 · L62 · 07:06–08:23)
- A fixed column structure suits a book, which needs less flexibility; a magazine benefits more from a grid system that can flex per spread. (5642 · U4 · L62 · 09:54–11:27)
- Rabatment, rotating a square within the format, preserves the same proportion at any distance. (5642 · U4 · L62 · 16:43–17:50)
- A grid is a diagram of guides that organizes content and lets it be handled with more control, or more looseness, depending on the job. (5642 · U4 · L63 · 00:00–00:47)
- There are four grid types: manuscript (one column, set by margins), multicolumn (subdivided horizontally), modular (equal modules assigned to content), and hierarchical (built around areas of interest, more flexible but more fragile). (5642 · U4 · L63 · 00:47–03:02)
- A grid builds up from one minimum unit, usually the text's leading: leading gives columns, columns plus gutters (which double as flow lines) give modules, and modules combine into fields. (5642 · U4 · L63 · 04:04–06:37)
- A working grid should still carry its own deliberate exception — the one line that is allowed to break it. (5642 · U4 · L63 · 06:37–07:05)
- A grid is planned from the general to the particular, but built from the particular to the general. (5642 · U4 · L64 · 00:00–01:20)
- Twelve columns is the practical choice when a layout needs to alternate between three and four columns, since 12 is the lowest common multiple of both. (5642 · U4 · L64 · 01:20–02:55)
- When a format's own proportion won't divide evenly by the "natural" leading, it is better to fix the number of lines wanted and let the space dictate the leading instead. (5642 · U4 · L64 · 02:55–04:48)

## 3. Checks the teacher uses

- **Five-quality checklist.** Judge a piece against clarity, balance, contrast, unity and salience before working on it further. Needs judgement by eye; the course presents it as a checklist, not a metric. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 00:09–01:37)
- **Precision check.** Are elements clearly centred or not, are lines clearly orthogonal or not, are size differences clear? Could be measured from a rendered image: element-centre vs. canvas-centre coordinates, line-angle deviation from vertical/horizontal, size ratios between elements. (5642 · U4 · L46 · 01:40–02:56)
- **Levelling/sharpening ambiguity check.** Is an object clearly on-axis (levelled) or clearly shifted through the axis (sharpened), or stuck ambiguously between the two? Could be measured: offset from the geometric centre as a proportion of the frame. (5642 · U4 · L58 · 00:00–01:45)
- **Grayscale-conversion check for colour contrast.** Convert a palette to grayscale to see how much a hue needs to be pushed up or down, especially between figure and background. Measurable from a rendered image: luminance difference after desaturation. This check is colour-theory territory in substance; see Overlaps. (5642 · U4 · L50 · 06:10–07:32)
- **Vibration check.** Do two complementary colours at full saturation clash or vibrate? Needs judgement by eye, though the underlying condition (near-complementary hue at similar saturation/value) could be flagged from colour values. Colour-theory territory; see Overlaps. (5642 · U4 · L50 · 05:15–06:10)
- **Counter/spacing mismatch check.** Does a heavier weight's tight counters sit next to letterspacing that is too wide (or a lighter weight's open counters next to spacing that is too tight)? Measurable from a rendered image: ratio of counter area to inter-letter gap. (5642 · U4 · L51 · 01:37–02:27)
- **x-height match check.** When combining typefaces, are the two faces' x-heights level on the page? Directly measurable: x-height in points or pixels for each face at its chosen size. (5642 · U4 · L51 · 06:24–07:56)
- **Iconicity-match check.** Does a supporting graphic's realism (e.g. a map placed beside photographs) sit too close, in style, to the photographic material it should stay subordinate to? Needs judgement by eye. (5642 · U4 · L52 · 05:25–07:57)
- **Ambiguous-position check.** Does an element sit clearly to one side or the edge, or is it stuck in an undecided middle position? Measurable from a rendered image: offset from true centre or edge as a proportion of the available space. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 05:07–06:29)
- **Odd-number-of-groups check.** When grouping images, is the result an odd number of clusters? Directly measurable: a count. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 06:29–07:11)
- **Motif-visibility check.** Is the key motif of an image (e.g. the bird cages in a portrait) still legible at its chosen size? Needs judgement by eye. (5642 · U4 · L54 · 04:11–05:01)
- **Macro rhythm check.** Viewed as a strip of reduced spread thumbnails, does visual weight rise and fall in a way that supports the piece's ending, or does it dissipate too early? Needs judgement by eye, done deliberately at reduced scale. (5642 · U4 · L56 · 08:05–10:31)
- **Line-count/field-division check.** Does a column's line count divide evenly into the number of fields wanted, once the needed gutters are subtracted, or does it leave an awkward remainder? Directly measurable: the arithmetic itself. (5642 · U4 · L64 · 02:55–04:48)
- **Grid-snap check.** Does a paragraph style's "align to grid" setting make its lines land exactly on the baseline grid? Directly measurable from the file: line position vs. grid-line position. (5642 · U4 · L64 · 06:48–07:19, 07:19–09:29)

## 4. Examples

- ABC circular monogram — unity from the dialogue between very circular letterforms and their circular container. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 01:37–02:44)
- Westinghouse logotype — unity from the plainness of its strokes, balance from a precise, tightly fitted symmetry. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 01:37–02:44)
- MIT Media Lab dynamic identity — twelve colour/shape variations built from the same repeated compositional traits. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 02:45–03:35)

- Francesco Franchi, IL Magazine covers — unity through repetition of a simple geometric motif, which makes any anomaly stand out. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 04:53–05:55)

- Type a formula (e.g. "*1.618" or "+14.5") directly into a point-size or margin field to compute a proportional or Fibonacci value in place. (5642 · U4 · L60 · 09:46–13:38; 5642 · U4 · L64 · 07:19–09:29)

- Set an object style's frame-fit option to fit content proportionally and enable transparency, for a reusable image-frame treatment. (5642 · U4 · L57 · 05:19–07:31)
- Rotate a photo or text block (e.g. -90°, 90°) to change how it fills a grid column. (5642 · U4 · L59 · 03:52–04:58)
