# U05 · The laws of visual perception: unity, weight, balance and movement — working notes

Source: `references/course/U05-L65*.md` through `U05-L78*.md` (5642 · U5), plus `references/course/U05-unit.md`. No `BLOCKED.md` exists for this unit — all 14 lessons were read in full: L65–L78.

Teacher: Pepe Gimeno. Lessons L65–L69 are biographical/case-study lessons (his own personal projects across decades) that set up the working method; the method itself and its four principles are taught in L70–L78. Colour theory itself belongs to `color-theory`; it is flagged in **Overlaps** and kept above only where it is doing composition/weight work.

## 1. Method steps

The teacher states his method explicitly in three steps (L71), preceded by a framing point about working with constraint (L70) and followed by four ordered exercises, one per principle (L73–L78), which end in one fully worked example.

1. **Treat the material's limitations as generative, not just restrictive.** Waste material cannot be resized or altered, which forces a solution — exactly like a design brief's fixed texts, images and page count. (5642 · U5 · L70 · 01:08–01:53, 01:53–02:46)
2. **Strip the material of its former identity so it becomes pure form** — a tin lid becomes "a semicircle with a certain fold," a brick becomes "a rectangle in a pinkish tone." (5642 · U5 · L71 · 00:38–02:12)
3. **Analyse the form patiently, over time, for the tension it already contains**, without forcing a single quick reading — a piece can reveal something different on a different day. (5642 · U5 · L71 · 02:12–04:10)
4. **Reinforce that energy by applying the principles of perception**, one at a time even though in practice they combine and accumulate. (5642 · U5 · L71 · 04:10–04:50)
5. **Build compositional unity first**, ordering scattered elements so the mind isn't faced with chaos, using one or more of: superposition, proximity/similarity, a grid or other structure, evoking a whole figure, or the distribution of space itself. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 01:52–02:56; summarised at 5642 · U5 · L74 · 09:30–11:13)
6. **Categorize and set weight next** — decide what is visually "number one," then "number two," and so on — using size relative to the format, contrast/colour of the object against its background, position within the frame, and the shape's own internal directional force. (5642 · U5 · L70 · 04:50–05:48; worked through at 5642 · U5 · L75 · 00:58–02:04, 07:37–08:27, 10:34–11:56)
7. **Work balance and imbalance.** Recognize that the basic balanced figures (line, triangle, square, circle, sphere) come from forces that cancel out symmetrically, then deform them — bend a line, sharpen an angle, deform a circle — to inject energy; in three dimensions, offset a heavy element with a counterweight on the other side of an axis. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 00:06–00:58, 04:04–05:03, 06:20–07:44, 15:49–16:24)
8. **Guide movement out of that imbalance.** Movement can come from a shape's own internal force, from everyday visual experience (wind, a moving body), or from repetition/translation of a form; then build and control a deliberate visual path through the piece, including paths built to end on a small but important element such as text. (5642 · U5 · L77 · 00:06–01:25, 04:09–05:03, 06:09–06:34, 09:00–09:22; 5642 · U5 · L77 · 12:34–13:19)
9. **Worked example applying the whole method to one found object** (a bicycle-tyre fragment): isolate the shape → analyse which orientation it "wants" (tested face-down vs. face-up) → decide the piece's intention (here, "this shape needed to fly") → fit/reduce the format to the piece's weight → test position within the format (heavy vs. flying) → adjust the format's proportion (doubled it, worked horizontally) → add a smaller companion element for scale and comparison. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 11:56–13:15, 13:15–13:57, 13:57–14:40, 14:40–15:10, 15:10–15:40)

## 2. Principles and claims

- The four principles covered in this unit, in order: composition/unity, categorizing/weight, balance/imbalance, movement. (5642 · U5 · L70 · 04:50–05:48)
- Working with existing design material instead of narrative imagery keeps attention on pure structure and form. (5642 · U5 · L70 · 04:09–04:50)
- Material must lose its name and referent and become pure form before it can be composed with. (5642 · U5 · L71 · 00:38–02:12)
- The principles of perception are not mutually exclusive — they generally accumulate and appear together, even though it is best to work through them one at a time. (5642 · U5 · L71 · 04:10–04:50)
- Valuing and distributing all of a compositional space, with nothing wasted, is its own compositional skill. (5642 · U5 · L72 · 03:25–04:45)
- Texture is a way of distributing space that adds material interest beyond line or shape alone. (5642 · U5 · L72 · 04:45–05:27)
- Loading visual weight toward the top or one edge of a composition can make a heavy form read as rising or floating rather than falling. (5642 · U5 · L72 · 06:08–07:27, 08:09–08:42)
- The tension of an oblique/diagonal line combines the distribution of space with directional pull, and can add depth on its own. (5642 · U5 · L72 · 08:42–09:21)
- Fragmenting a form (showing only part of a circle) creates interest because the eye tries to complete it. (5642 · U5 · L72 · 10:24–10:52)
- Repetition and transformation of a form across a sequence is a historical technique for representing movement. (5642 · U5 · L72 · 14:26–14:50, 14:50–15:03)
- A deliberate visual path organizes how the eye enters and crosses a work, and can reroute the "natural" entry point. (5642 · U5 · L72 · 15:03–15:54)
- Figure and ground can be made deliberately ambiguous, so it is genuinely unclear which one is the subject. (5642 · U5 · L72 · 16:36–17:29)
- Composing means ordering disparate elements so the whole reads as attractive, understandable, and worth continued looking. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 00:00–00:53)
- The mind automatically groups, joins and simplifies what it sees, completing incomplete forms into a whole it recognizes (three dots read as a triangle; a cable tie plus one twist reads as an apple). (5642 · U5 · L73 · 00:53–01:52)
- Unity is the first job of composition, because the mind resists chaos and disorder. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 01:52–02:56)
- Superposition — simply overlapping unrelated elements — is the most elementary route to unity. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 03:02–04:10)
- Proximity and similarity (of shape, colour, or line direction) reinforce unity between otherwise unrelated elements. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 04:36–05:27)
- A structure (grid, silhouette, single line) is an independent second route to unity, and can hold together material that shares nothing else. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 06:55–07:23)
- A structure can nest smaller unities inside a larger overall unity. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 07:23–08:26)
- Some compositions sit deliberately at the "edge of chaos," where unity is barely legible, to show how little is needed to hold a composition together. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 06:04–06:55)
- The more closed/dense a structure, the more powerful it reads. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 10:03–10:55)
- Superposing layers creates depth, like building a stage set by placing elements behind one another. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 12:17–13:00)
- A progression (e.g., circles growing in size) always carries movement with it. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 13:00–13:38)
- Once a set of elements evokes a whole recognizable figure, its parts read as one block regardless of how they are physically arranged. (5642 · U5 · L74 · 00:07–00:47)
- Distributing and dividing space is itself a unifying device — both filling all of it and leaving almost all of it empty can create unity. (5642 · U5 · L74 · 01:54–02:45, 03:00–03:33)
- A structure built around emptiness/void rather than the solid figure can still create unity by giving value to what is not there. (5642 · U5 · L74 · 08:00–08:35)
- Four resources for compositional unity, by the teacher's own summary: proximity/similarity, the grid, figuration, and the ordering of space. (5642 · U5 · L74 · 09:30–11:13)
- Weight is the visual prominence an element carries, used to tell the eye where to look first, second, third, and so on. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 00:06–00:58)
- With material that cannot be resized, weight is instead set by resizing the format around it. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 00:58–02:04)
- There is a right relationship between compositional space and the shapes within it — pushing size too far "suffocates" the shapes rather than adding weight. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 00:58–02:04)
- Lowering contrast against the background lightens an element's weight; darkening the background raises it. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 04:14–04:40, 04:40–05:23)
- Colour itself can carry weight independent of size — a vivid element reads as heavier than a pale one of the same size. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 06:23–06:39)
- Position in the frame affects weight: centred reads as stable (the frame's pull on both sides cancels), low in the format reads as heavy, high reads as light. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 07:37–08:27, 08:27–09:22)
- A shape's own internal directional force adds to or fights the frame's pull on its weight. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 10:34–11:56)
- Balance means a set of forces acting on a form cancel each other out; the line, equilateral triangle, square, circle and sphere are the basic balanced figures. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 00:06–00:58, 03:53–04:04)
- Imbalance is what happens when forces go out of control — bending a line, sharpening an angle, or deforming a circle concentrates energy at specific points. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 04:04–05:03, 05:03–05:45)
- The more tightly an angle closes, the more energy it carries; relaxed angles read as passive. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 05:03–05:45)
- A curved line is experienced as a straight line that has been deformed and wants to spring back, which is why deformed circles and curves hold so much visual energy. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 06:20–07:44)
- The more complex and irregular a curve, the more energy it holds, because the eye must keep following its curves and counter-curves. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 07:44–08:19)
- Setting a highly energetic form beside a calmer one increases the perceived energy of the first by comparison. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 11:00–11:28)
- A form's internal energy can be pushed further by where it sits in the format — the frame's pull adds to or fights the shape's own directional force. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 13:52–14:54)
- Balance and imbalance apply in three dimensions as much as in two — a sculpture can offset a heavy element on one side of an axis with an opposing form on the other. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 15:49–16:24)
- Imbalance can be created by absence — a missing part of an expected figure, or a hidden part of a grid — because the eye notices what it expects but does not find. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 18:10–18:32, 18:32–18:45)
- Imbalance/internal energy in a form reads as movement, even in a completely static image or object. (5642 · U5 · L77 · 00:06–01:25)
- Movement can be read from ordinary visual experience alone — wind-bent trees, a body mid-motion — independent of any perceptual construction. (5642 · U5 · L77 · 03:01–03:13, 03:30–03:42)
- Repetition and translation of the same form across a space is a historical technique for depicting movement (chronophotography, Futurism, Cubism). (5642 · U5 · L77 · 04:09–05:03, 06:09–06:34)
- Repeating and slightly varying a rigid form can make a static structure look as if it is toppling or collapsing. (5642 · U5 · L77 · 06:34–07:05)
- A visual path is the route the eye is made to take through a composition via the tensions of its shapes. (5642 · U5 · L77 · 09:00–09:22)
- The same principles of visual perception underlie composition across very different disciplines: bonsai, plated food, photography, sculpture, graphic design, architecture. (5642 · U5 · L77 · 09:53–11:04)
- A single, unified visual path reads with more immediate force than a complex or crossing one, though the complex one can hold interest for longer. (5642 · U5 · L77 · 14:24–15:03)
- A visual path is often used deliberately to lead the eye to a small but important element, such as a piece of text. (5642 · U5 · L77 · 12:34–13:19)
- A single successful image can carry several perceptual principles (unity, weight, proximity, expression, movement) at once. (5642 · U5 · L78 · 02:15–03:06)
- A strong visual path can deliberately delay its own meaning — the eye follows the lines and angles before the substitution/subject becomes clear. (5642 · U5 · L78 · 00:35–01:25, 01:25–02:15)
- Empty or "unfinished" space in a composition can carry as much compositional weight as the filled space. (5642 · U5 · L78 · 02:15–03:06)

## 3. Checks the teacher uses

- **Progressive-reduction check for weight.** Shrink the format around a fixed object step by step until the shapes are "suffocated," then back off one step. Needs judgement by eye — no numeric threshold is given, only a live demonstration. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 00:58–02:04)
- **Contrast check for weight.** Compare an object's tone against its background; lighten or darken either side and judge the change in prominence. Could be measured from a rendered image: the luminance/contrast difference between object and background. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 04:14–04:40, 04:40–05:23)
- **"Number one, two, three" categorization check.** Ask whether one element in a piece is clearly the most important, with a bad advertisement shown as a negative case where two elements compete as "number one" and confuse the eye. Needs judgement by eye — the fault is about relative emphasis, not a single measurable value, though it could be approximated by comparing each element's size and contrast. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 06:39–07:37)
- **Move-it-and-watch test.** Physically move or reorient a found piece to see how it "behaves" — whether it advances, retreats, rises or falls — repeated until the piece's intention becomes clear (used on the bicycle-tyre fragment, tested face-down then face-up). Needs judgement by eye; this is the core working method for reading any single form. (5642 · U5 · L71 · 02:12–04:10; 5642 · U5 · L75 · 11:56–13:15, 13:57–14:40)
- **Force-cancellation check for balance.** Confirm a figure is balanced by checking whether the forces pulling at its extremities are symmetric — an elastic pulled taut from both ends stays a straight line; a triangle's, square's and circle's forces are shown summing to zero. Could be measured geometrically, in principle (symmetry of the figure), though the course only demonstrates it physically and by drawing. (5642 · U5 · L76 · 00:06–00:58, 02:40–03:53)
- **Edge-of-chaos legibility check.** Ask whether a set of elements still reads as one unified group or has tipped into pure chaos; some examples are shown deliberately at that limit. Needs judgement by eye. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 06:04–06:55)
- **Grid-holds-it check.** Test whether tying every element to a shared grid or line is what makes an otherwise unrelated set of objects read as one. Could be measured from a rendered image, in principle: whether each element actually touches or aligns with the grid. (5642 · U5 · L73 · 07:23–08:26)
- **Single-vs-combined path check.** Compare how a composition reads with one unified visual path against a more complex, crossing one, and judge which gives more immediate force versus more lasting interest. Needs judgement by eye. (5642 · U5 · L77 · 14:24–15:03)

## 7. Overlaps

- **Gestalt-style grouping (→ repeats U02):** proximity, similarity, and figure/ground, used here to explain unity (5642 · U5 · L73 · 04:36–05:27; 5642 · U5 · L74 · 08:35–08:51), restate the same Gestalt laws taught explicitly in U02 L09 (pregnance, closure, proximity, figure/ground), now reapplied to found-object and sculptural work rather than 2D graphic marks.
- **Grid-for-consistency (→ repeats U02):** using a grid/structure to unify a set of otherwise unrelated elements (5642 · U5 · L73 · 07:23–08:26, 09:31–10:03) extends the grid-for-a-consistent-series idea already taught in U02 L19, here applied to physical materials rather than a page series.
- **Diagonal/oblique movement (→ repeats U02):** the oblique line's tension and depth (5642 · U5 · L72 · 08:42–09:21) restates the diagonal-for-movement principle from U02 L20, with new examples (the Library of Alexandria, a photographer's converging shadows).
