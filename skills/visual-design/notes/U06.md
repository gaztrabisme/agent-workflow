# U06 · Graphic design from simple geometries — working notes

Source: `references/course/U06-L79-symbolism.md` through `U06-L91-the-presentation-of-the-project.md` (5642 · U6). No `BLOCKED.md` exists for this unit — all 13 lessons were read in full: L79–L91.

This unit has two parts: theory (L79–L81: symbolism, iconicity, geometry) and one continuous worked project (L82–L91: a canned-drink visual identity built entirely from circle/square/triangle geometry). The whole worked project is the unit's practice; see **Tasks** for what the platform names as discrete assignments versus what is simply the teacher's own demonstration.

## 1. Method steps

The three theory lessons build the vocabulary (sign/symbol, iconicity, the three basic shapes and their felt meanings); the project lessons then apply that vocabulary end to end to one product. The lesson order is the working order.

1. **Treat geometry as the visual language for building meaning**, understanding first that a sign is what carries meaning and a symbol is a sign that has grown beyond its established senses. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 00:30–01:19)
2. **Decide where the design should sit on the iconicity scale** — from the natural image (highest information, most literal) to non-figurative representation (fully abstract) — before choosing a graphic style. (5642 · U6 · L80 · 00:28–00:50, 05:18–06:02)
3. **Use circle, square and triangle as the base vocabulary**, keeping in mind what each shape already carries by itself (circle: infinite/perfection/unity/wholeness; square: order/resistance/stability/strength; triangle: sacred/hell, stability/instability, future/past, success/defeat). (5642 · U6 · L81 · 00:26–00:50, 03:50–05:28)
4. **Run the whole project through three lenses tied to the three shapes**: the blue square for reason (product, target, market, hard facts), the red circle for emotion (differential values, feelings, sensations), the yellow triangle for attention (personal point of view, whatever catches the eye from the periphery). (5642 · U6 · L81 · 07:07–07:43)
5. **Read the brief** — product category, characteristics, target audience, differentiator — before making any naming or visual decision. (5642 · U6 · L82 · 00:37–00:49, 01:26–01:33)
6. **Choose the project's name from your own mood board, with the heart, not the intellect** — pick a band, musician or song you love, not one you think you could visually execute. (5642 · U6 · L82 · 01:34–02:17)
7. **Once named, research the choice and sort every finding into the three lenses** (reason/emotion/attention); nothing that catches your attention should be left out at this stage. (5642 · U6 · L82 · 02:49–03:25, 03:26–04:12, 07:42–08:19)
8. **Clean the three messy research lists into tidy summary cards**, one per shape. (5642 · U6 · L82 · 08:20–08:31)
9. **Turn the sender–message–receiver model into sender–story–receiver**: find one differential phrase, filtered from the three lists, that becomes the concept and the story the design will tell. (5642 · U6 · L83 · 01:47–02:42, 04:29–05:20)
10. **Build a visual-universe mood board by hunting references column by column** (rational/emotional/attention), combining columns when useful, and never using material that is already graphic design (a finished poster, a processed photo) as a reference — look at architecture, photography and clothing instead, to keep the search personal rather than trend-driven. (5642 · U6 · L84 · 01:44–04:03, 04:59–05:59)
11. **Mine the finished mood board for three types of elements**: the close gaze (primary images, square), the distant gaze (textures/patterns, circle) and the complementary gaze (miscellaneous elements, triangle). (5642 · U6 · L84 · 10:58–12:07)
12. **Extract geometric shapes directly out of the gathered references** (a building's windows, a doorway, a staircase) to build a personal shape vocabulary for the next stage. (5642 · U6 · L84 · 12:07–14:44)
13. **Set the physical support (e.g. a can) up only as a rough guide box**, then compose the primary shape from the extracted geometry, working out symmetry/balance, before deciding final size. (5642 · U6 · L85 · 00:59–01:23, 02:18–02:57)
14. **Place typography and information zones early, even with placeholder text**, to test where information will actually sit before final copy exists. (5642 · U6 · L85 · 02:57–03:31, 03:31–04:07)
15. **Identify the system's elements** — logo, typography, colour, image, plus concept, composition, tone and support — and decide what level of complexity, what strategy (formal/conceptual/mixed) and what flexibility (closed/open) the system needs. (5642 · U6 · L86 · 01:50–03:03, 03:03–04:39, 04:39–10:23)
16. **Duplicate the base composition into several variants and separate constants from variables**: identify which elements carry the fixed, fundamental concept and which secondary zones (a container shape, an emblem, a typeface) can mutate one at a time to signal difference between pieces. (5642 · U6 · L87 · 00:51–01:16, 01:16–01:48, 01:48–02:16)
17. **Build the colour palette by sampling directly from the mood board's own references**, rather than starting cold or from the product's literal colour, and test combinations directly on the design until one is convincing. (5642 · U6 · L88 · 00:07–00:53, 01:46–03:45, 04:27–06:38)
18. **Set a text hierarchy before choosing typefaces** — brand name highest, category mid, technical data lowest — then build a second, typography-only mood board (period references, the source material's own lettering, city ephemera) before picking a typeface. (5642 · U6 · L89 · 00:07–01:09, 01:09–02:56)
19. **Pair an elaborate/display typeface with a simple/rational one** (or the reverse) rather than using one typeface throughout, and revisit colour and scale once typography is in, since it can change which elements read as primary. (5642 · U6 · L89 · 06:29–08:20, 09:52–10:36)
20. **Apply the finished system to front and back of the primary support, then stress-test it on other pieces** (other product-line strengths, a gift bag, a t-shirt, a poster series) to see how flexible the identity really is. (5642 · U6 · L90 · 00:23–01:47, 04:55–07:31)
21. **Mock the design up at real scale in a realistic render before presenting it**, then build the final presentation deck in the same visual identity as the project — open with the concept, reuse process material (mood boards, colour references) as content, and export/publish. (5642 · U6 · L90 · 02:43–04:28; 5642 · U6 · L91 · 05:51–06:53, 07:37–08:14, 08:23–09:31)

## 2. Principles and claims

- Every sign has symbolic potential; a symbol is a sign that has overflowed its established senses and opens itself to every possible sense (Fernando Zamora). (5642 · U6 · L79 · 00:30–01:19)
- The word "symbol" comes from the Greek *sym-ballein*, to join or bring together. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 00:30–01:19)
- Signs can be verbal, visual, auditory, tactile or non-verbal. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 01:20–01:33)
- A sign involves intellect and reason; a symbol is the same, crossed by emotion, culture, religion and other forces that make it grow. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 01:34–01:55)
- *Symbolum* originally meant a community's set of religious beliefs, standing at the head of all symbology. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 01:56–02:18)
- Fandoms (Star Wars, Led Zeppelin) function like "religions," generating a shared symbolic code between creators and followers. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 02:19–02:47)
- A symbol is a sign that has grown: it does not stop being a sign, but is something more than one. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 02:19–02:47)
- Primitive humans gave visible form to the essence of things, not just their appearance, using simple geometric signs made with natural tools. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 02:47–03:31)
- Expression moved historically from primary needs (hunger, danger, fear) toward more complex emotions (joy, tenderness, sadness, wonder at birth or death). (5642 · U6 · L79 · 03:32–03:49)
- Most symbols were, in principle, geometric simplifications — a synthesis of everything they meant to express; the sun is one of history's most frequent symbols, rendered very differently across cultures. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 03:50–04:21)
- Proposed redefinition of the human being: the only animal with the creative capacity to generate symbols, and therefore to be an artist. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 04:21–04:34)
- We can change a sign freely, but not a symbol, without changing ourselves; a symbol is worth dying or killing for, a sign only worth money. (5642 · U6 · L79 · 04:35–04:56)
- Iconicity is the relationship of appearance between an image and its referent: more realistic means more iconic, more abstract means less iconic. (5642 · U6 · L80 · 00:28–00:50)
- The scale runs 11 levels, from the natural image (level 11, highest information) down to non-figurative representation (level 1): natural image, 3D model, hologram, colour photo, B&W photo, realistic painting, non-realistic figurative representation, pictogram, motivated schema, arbitrary schema, non-figurative representation. (5642 · U6 · L80 · 01:13–01:33, 04:57–05:17)
- Motivated schemas (e.g. maps) keep an organic relationship with the original without matching its real proportions; arbitrary schemas (e.g. traffic signs) share no logical relationship with the original at all. (5642 · U6 · L80 · 03:55–04:28, 04:29–04:55)
- A simplified model collapses the 11 levels into three: representative, symbolic, abstract, with many degrees between them. (5642 · U6 · L80 · 05:18–06:02)
- Any subject has a minimal unit at which it can still be represented and recognised. (5642 · U6 · L80 · 06:03–06:39)
- Geometry studies the properties of figures in the plane or space, and shows up both in human-made design and in the proportions of natural forms. (5642 · U6 · L81 · 00:26–00:50, 00:51–01:20)
- Geometric organisation does not by itself generate a concept, but it supplies a compositional process — a system for relating elements into a coherent, balanced whole. (5642 · U6 · L81 · 01:44–02:13)
- In design, geometry organises the layout of elements on the plane and, through proportion, carries an aesthetic function. (5642 · U6 · L81 · 02:14–02:38)
- Several art and design movements built vital use of geometry into their work: Art Deco (geometry serving illustration and typography), Cubism (the geometric absorbing recognisable forms into multiple dimensions), Futurism (geometry at the service of the surreal), Op Art (geometry and its operations pushed to an extreme), and typography itself (letterforms constructed from circle, square and triangle). (5642 · U6 · L81 · 02:49–02:59, 03:31–03:49)
- The circle is considered the mother of all forms: a single cell, having no beginning or end, representing infinity, perfection, unity, wholeness and the endless cycle of life. (5642 · U6 · L81 · 03:50–04:03)
- The square conveys order, resistance, stability and strength — but tipped onto a vertex (a diamond), it instead reads as temporary and uncertain. (5642 · U6 · L81 · 04:31–04:48, 04:49–04:59)
- The triangle corresponds to the number 3 and the Trinity, the sacred and divine, the elements and the sexes: point-up it is sun/fire/masculine, inverted it is moon/water/feminine; in ancient Egypt a symbol of divinity, and in Dante's Inferno the shape of hell as a nine-level inverted cone. (5642 · U6 · L81 · 05:00–05:28, 06:11–06:30)
- In signage, the square (generally blue) conveys informative content, the circle (generally white or red) conveys regulatory content, and the triangle (generally yellow) puts the viewer on alert. (5642 · U6 · L81 · 06:31–07:06)
- The naming choice for a project should be made with the heart and from emotion, not from reason or from what you think you could execute based on a reference's own visual style. (5642 · U6 · L82 · 01:34–02:17)
- Nothing found during research should be left to chance — everything that catches attention goes into a first list, later refined into a tidier version. (5642 · U6 · L82 · 07:42–08:19)
- A visual-identity project needs a story behind it — strong design or good typographic/colour choices are useless without something interesting behind them. (5642 · U6 · L83 · 00:24–00:45)
- Three pillars recur through the course, each tied to a shape: the square for reason (product, target, market), the circle for emotion (differential values, emotional links), the triangle for voice/attention (the way of speaking that makes the product unique across every communication). (5642 · U6 · L83 · 00:45–01:47)
- Turning the standard sender–message–receiver model into sender–story–receiver adds nuance, because it matters not just what is said but how, and to whom. (5642 · U6 · L83 · 01:47–02:42)
- A single differential phrase, filtered from the reason/emotion/attention research, works as the trigger for the whole visual concept. (5642 · U6 · L83 · 04:29–05:20)
- Rule for image search: avoid material that is already graphic design (something already processed by another designer's eye); look instead at architecture, photography and clothing, to build your own search criteria rather than following trends. A poster can still be used, but only to understand an era's or band's aesthetic, not as a current design reference. (5642 · U6 · L84 · 04:59–05:59)
- A finished mood board can be mined for three types of visual element: the close gaze (primary images), the distant gaze (textures/patterns) and the complementary gaze (miscellaneous accompanying elements). (5642 · U6 · L84 · 10:58–12:07)
- Geometric shapes can be pulled directly out of photographic or architectural references (a building's windows and doorways) to build a personal shape vocabulary. (5642 · U6 · L84 · 12:07–14:44)
- A design's core shape can evoke a concept ("a traveller in time and space") without being too literal about it. (5642 · U6 · L85 · 01:23–02:18)
- Composition work can start entirely in black and white; colour is deliberately deferred to its own later stage. (5642 · U6 · L85 · 00:00–00:59, 04:44–05:02)
- A system is more than the sum of its parts: it is the parts, their individual functions, and their intrinsic relationships (compare the solar system or the nervous system). (5642 · U6 · L86 · 00:26–01:50)
- The core elements of a visual-identity system are the logo, typography, colour and image (photography, illustration, patterns, textures). (5642 · U6 · L86 · 01:50–03:03)
- Further elements make up a system: concept (the idea/slogan behind it), composition (how elements sit in space), tone (adult/childlike, bright/dark) and support (the final material) — support is often left for last consideration, but is decisive, since a design can fail to perform once carried onto a material it wasn't designed for. (5642 · U6 · L86 · 03:03–04:39)
- Systems vary in complexity (a denim brand's low-complexity system vs. a basketball championship's high-complexity one), in strategy (formal/morphological, conceptual, or mixed), and in flexibility (closed/predictable vs. open/looser). (5642 · U6 · L86 · 04:39–06:41, 09:08–10:23)
- A system needs two or more parts in dialogue, a sense of order/sequence, avoidance of flat repetition (unless deliberately closed), and objectives that are both particular to each piece and complementary to the whole. (5642 · U6 · L86 · 10:23–11:46)
- Constants are the elements that stay fixed across a system (typically the fundamental concept); variables are the elements that mutate, swap or complement each other across pieces. (5642 · U6 · L87 · 00:21–00:51)
- Isolating constants and variables works better with colour and typography set aside at first, so it is clear which elements alone carry the variation. (5642 · U6 · L87 · 00:51–01:16)
- Typography itself can serve as a system variable: a heavier weight suits a stronger product variant, a lighter weight a lighter one, a more technical face an odder one. (5642 · U6 · L87 · 03:13–04:47)
- Revisiting the mood board to source a colour palette from what's already gathered works better than inventing colours from scratch or defaulting to a product's literal real-world colour. (5642 · U6 · L88 · 00:07–00:53, 01:46–03:45)
- It is acceptable to push a sampled colour toward its "true" real-world hue when the source photo rendered it dull or muted. (5642 · U6 · L88 · 01:46–03:45)
- The same base design elements can carry different colour combinations, shifting the whole toward a more modern, retro, or vintage feel. (5642 · U6 · L88 · 03:45–04:27)
- Set a text hierarchy before choosing typefaces: the brand name is highest, its category is mid, technical data is a third level. (5642 · U6 · L89 · 00:07–01:09)
- Building a typography-only mood board (period references, the source material's own lettering, local ephemera) grounds a typeface choice in something more than personal taste. (5642 · U6 · L89 · 01:09–02:56)
- Pairing an elaborate/display typeface with a simple/rational one (or the reverse) is a usable trick, rather than using a single typeface throughout a project. (5642 · U6 · L89 · 06:29–08:20)
- Once typography is chosen, the whole system may need revisiting — colours, scales, and which elements read as primary can all change once type is in play. (5642 · U6 · L89 · 09:52–10:36)
- The back of a package typically carries more generic text plus required legal iconography (recycling mark, allergen information, barcode). (5642 · U6 · L89 · 08:57–09:52)
- One structural system can generate several product lines by varying only the colour palette and a couple of container shapes, without redesigning the layout — this is simpler when the system relies on structure/form rather than colour itself as the differentiator. (5642 · U6 · L90 · 00:23–01:47)
- Small recurring details (e.g. how many times an icon repeats) can work as quiet meta-messages signalling strength or variant. (5642 · U6 · L90 · 01:47–02:43)
- Testing a system on further, unrelated supports (a gift bag, a t-shirt, a poster series) shows how flexible and dynamic the identity really is, and gives a client a fuller sense of the project. (5642 · U6 · L90 · 04:55–06:21, 06:21–07:31)
- A presentation deck should carry the same visual identity as the project itself, not just list the finished pieces. (5642 · U6 · L91 · 05:51–06:53)
- Opening a presentation with the concept, rather than a plain summary, hooks the viewer before showing the rest. (5642 · U6 · L91 · 05:51–06:53)
- Nothing developed during a project is wasted — mood boards, colour references and process sketches can be reused and re-curated as presentation content. (5642 · U6 · L91 · 07:37–08:14)
- The cover image is the single most important image in a portfolio post; choose one strong, final mockup shot to represent the whole project. (5642 · U6 · L91 · 11:27–12:59)

## 3. Checks the teacher uses

- **Minimal-unit check.** Ask what the minimal unit is for representing a given subject (Picasso's bull, stripped across eleven drawings to a single line). Needs judgement by eye — the course gives no formula for when a form has been stripped too far. (5642 · U6 · L80 · 06:03–06:39)
- **"Chosen with the heart" check on a name.** Confirm the project's name/reference came from genuine personal attachment, not from calculating what could be visually achieved with it. Needs judgement — this is a check on the chooser's own motive, not on the artefact. (5642 · U6 · L82 · 01:34–02:17)
- **Completeness check on research.** Ask whether everything that caught your attention made it onto one of the three lists before cleaning them up. Needs judgement by eye — the course sets no minimum count or source list. (5642 · U6 · L82 · 07:42–08:19)
- **"Already designed" check on a reference.** Ask whether a candidate image is itself already graphic design (a finished poster, a processed photo) rather than raw material (architecture, photography, clothing); reject it as a reference if so, unless it is being used only to read an era's or band's aesthetic. Needs judgement by eye — no objective test is given for "already designed." (5642 · U6 · L84 · 04:59–05:59)
- **System check.** Ask whether a set of pieces has two or more parts in dialogue, a sense of order/sequence, avoidance of flat repetition (unless deliberately closed), and objectives that are both particular to each piece and complementary to the whole. Partly measurable from a rendered set: the number of shared/repeating parts across pieces could be counted; whether they read as "in dialogue" or as flat repetition needs judgement by eye. (5642 · U6 · L86 · 10:23–11:46)
- **Constant-vs-variable check.** Duplicate the base composition, vary one zone at a time, and check that the fundamental concept still reads across every version. Needs judgement by eye — the course works this by visual comparison of the duplicated compositions, not a metric. (5642 · U6 · L87 · 00:51–01:16, 03:13–04:47)
- **Colour-combination check.** Apply a candidate palette directly to the real design (the mark, the medallions, the wordmark) and keep testing combinations "until we're convinced we have the one that best fits the project." Needs judgement by eye; this check is colour-theory territory in substance (see Overlaps). (5642 · U6 · L88 · 04:27–06:09, 06:09–06:38)
- **Live typeface test.** Type the actual brand mark into a foundry's own live tester before buying a typeface, rather than judging a specimen sheet alone. Could be partly measured from a rendered result — e.g. whether a name's middle letter actually falls at the visual centre of the mark (the course notes this for "Kashmir" and Cako Black, where the H sits with three letters on each side) — but the overall fit judgement stays by eye. (5642 · U6 · L89 · 03:41–05:23, 05:23–06:29)
- **Cover-image check.** Judge whether a candidate cover image is "a really strong image, one with a mockup or some final piece of the design" before publishing a portfolio post. Needs judgement by eye — the course gives no measurable criterion beyond strength/impact. (5642 · U6 · L91 · 11:27–12:59)

## 6. Tool-specific steps

**Illustrator**
- Drag reference images straight into the working file, onto a new artboard, to build a mood board without a separate application. (5642 · U6 · L84 · 01:44–04:03)
- Draw the physical support (e.g. a can) as a guide box and mark it as a guide, so it constrains composition without yet fixing final size. (5642 · U6 · L85 · 00:59–01:23)
- To try a typeface you don't own: screenshot a live web test of it, bring the screenshot into Illustrator, trace the image, and delete the white — this introduces small tracing errors but works for evaluating fit before buying. (5642 · U6 · L89 · 03:41–05:23)
- Export finished artboards as a single PDF, or as PNG at 150 dpi with "Use Artboards" checked, naming the file for search (product, category, discipline keywords) before uploading to a platform. (5642 · U6 · L91 · 08:23–08:57, 08:57–09:31)

**Photoshop**
- In a mockup file, find the labelled smart-object layer (e.g. "put your design here"), double-click it, delete the sample art it ships with, and place your own design; saving updates the design back in the main mockup file automatically. (5642 · U6 · L90 · 02:43–04:28)
- Swap a mockup's background layer/colour to test how the design reads against different surfaces. (5642 · U6 · L90 · 02:43–04:28)

**Adobe Color**
- Use the colour-wheel tool's harmony rules (monochromatic, analogous, complementary) to generate quick starting palette combinations. (5642 · U6 · L88 · 00:53–01:46)

**Behance**
- Create a new project, add content blocks (Image, Text, Photo Grid, etc.), and set Content Spacing (e.g. down to a small value) so images run on continuously. (5642 · U6 · L91 · 09:31–10:28, 10:28–11:27)
