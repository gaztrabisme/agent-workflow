# U07 · How to think like a graphic designer — working notes

Source: `references/course/U07-L92*.md` through `U07-L104*.md` (5642 · U7). No `BLOCKED.md` exists for this unit — all 13 lessons were read in full: L92, L93, L94, L95, L96, L97, L98, L99, L100, L101, L102, L103, L104.

Unlike U02, this unit has no single project built up lesson-to-lesson. It teaches one repeatable method — take a real, broken piece and rework it — through eight separate case studies (a screen presentation, a business card, a publisher's mark, a drink label, a business-school identity, a theatre company's image, a hardware-store franchise, a music-festival campaign) plus a closing pair of lessons on screen display and on sustaining an identity over time. **Method steps** below distils the moves that recur across those case studies, anchored to where each is clearest. Colour theory itself (RGB/CMYK conversion, spot colour, hue connotation, vibrating-colour pairs as a colour-space fact) belongs to `color-theory`; it is flagged in **Overlaps** and kept above only where it is doing composition/legibility work (contrast, figure/ground, unifying a set).

## 1. Method steps

1. **Work from a real, broken example and rework it into one that works** — stated directly as this course's own method. (5642 · U7 · L92 · 01:37–01:54)
2. **Define identity broadly before designing anything**: not just the logo or imagotype, but the whole graphic language across every application (labels, networks, stationery), and potentially beyond the visual (phone manner, in-store music). (5642 · U7 · L92 · 01:12–01:28, 01:29–01:36)
3. **Find the piece's hardest, most important application and design for that constraint first** — a publisher's mark has to survive on the spine; a single-application business card has to carry the whole identity; an online-first mark has to survive the avatar. (5642 · U7 · L96 · 00:00–00:36; 5642 · U7 · L95 · 00:09–00:29; 5642 · U7 · L103 · 02:24–03:14)
4. **Strip the piece back to its essential element before adding anything back** — the bare wordmark with the stock photo removed, the bare capital with the historicist background removed, the bare mark with the legal form and description bar removed. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 01:09–01:47; 5642 · U7 · L97 · 04:13–05:28; 5642 · U7 · L100 · 05:05–07:31)
5. **Fix basic typographic correctness before anything else**: turn off autocorrect's spelling underline, use a genuine apostrophe rather than a prime or accent mark, drop false small caps, stop hyphenating flush-left text, and fix stray capitalisation on ordinary words. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 02:40–03:29; 5642 · U7 · L95 · 01:48–02:14, 02:15–03:07; 5642 · U7 · L96 · 04:03–05:43; 5642 · U7 · L93 · 10:10–10:39, 12:05–12:30)
6. **Check every page or application against one consistent construction axis**, and fix any mismatch between a centred element and a flush-left one. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 06:29–07:22; 5642 · U7 · L100 · 02:07–02:40)
7. **Remove decoration and clichés that aren't specific to this name or product**: a busy or historicist background, a dated pictorial symbol, a filler graphic used only to occupy space, a legal denomination. (5642 · U7 · L97 · 04:13–05:28; 5642 · U7 · L96 · 06:19–07:26; 5642 · U7 · L100 · 05:05–07:31, 03:20–03:54)
8. **Choose or commission a typeface for the piece's own character, not by habit** — replace a fanciful default with a well-made system or free font for corporate use, or commission real hand lettering when a calligraphic typeface only imitates handwriting. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 03:35–05:46; 5642 · U7 · L95 · 03:49–04:37; 5642 · U7 · L97 · 05:28–06:29)
9. **Use a type family's own weights, not capitals or extra tracking, to carry emphasis and hierarchy.** (5642 · U7 · L93 · 06:05–06:28; 5642 · U7 · L94 · 00:39–01:08; 5642 · U7 · L95 · 07:38–08:37)
10. **Convert a dense paragraph into a bold lead line plus bullet points, and narrow an over-wide column instead of shrinking the type.** (5642 · U7 · L94 · 01:09–03:41)
11. **Build a fixed margin/header band and let content grow from it in one direction** (right and down for flush-left, Latin-alphabet design), keeping the same margins across every piece in a set. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 07:55–08:56, 08:57–09:20, 09:21–10:09)
12. **Choose one deliberate compositional scheme instead of scattering elements to fill empty space (horror vacui)** — a vertical or horizontal division, a single strong axis, a margin-only structure. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 06:23–07:05; 5642 · U7 · L97 · 12:29–15:33; 5642 · U7 · L98 · 10:27–12:09)
13. **Replace generic stock photography with commissioned or constructed imagery** (illustration, pictograms, or hand lettering) matched to one visual language and one level of abstraction across the whole set. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 05:49–07:22, 08:18–10:27, 10:27–12:09, 14:57–16:23)
14. **When photographs are supplied from outside and vary in quality, unify them with one deliberate treatment** (greyscale, a shared reframing, or a cut-out) rather than presenting them as received. (5642 · U7 · L102 · 00:00–01:31)
15. **Choose colour by contrast and by what it connotes, not by fixed meaning** — avoid pairing colours of the same tonal darkness ("vibrating"), and use the support's own colour as background where that gains contrast outdoors. (5642 · U7 · L100 · 08:35–09:33; 5642 · U7 · L94 · 04:57–06:09; 5642 · U7 · L101 · 07:48–08:57)
16. **Unify a row of secondary marks (sponsor logos, icons) to their monochrome versions, then balance their sizes optically**, not to a common width or height. (5642 · U7 · L101 · 08:57–10:26)
17. **Build the identity as a flexible system that can grow indefinitely** — symbol alone, wordmark alone, or both, chosen case by case — rather than a fixed rule tied to specific applications. (5642 · U7 · L96 · 09:55–11:05; 5642 · U7 · L100 · 10:33–11:49)
18. **Use a separate, more legible typeface for running or small text than the display face used in the logotype or titling.** (5642 · U7 · L100 · 11:49–12:46; 5642 · U7 · L97 · 09:58–11:10)
19. **For a piece that must read at both close and far range, set type large with tight or negative leading, and retouch any letterform that collides with a rule or another letter by hand.** (5642 · U7 · L101 · 03:59–07:48; 5642 · U7 · L102 · 02:08–03:13)
20. **Design colour and proportion for the medium the identity will mostly be seen in** — pick colours that survive their main medium's colour system, and confirm the mark still reads at avatar size before finishing. (5642 · U7 · L103 · 00:00–02:24, 02:24–03:14)
21. **Plan the identity to adapt across formats (horizontal, square, vertical) from the outset**, using a complementary graphic element separate from the logotype to extend it into formats the mark itself doesn't cover. (5642 · U7 · L103 · 04:24–05:43)
22. **Before finishing, check the mark is legible, doesn't say more than intended, isn't built to dominate what sits beside it, and is easy enough for someone else to apply without the original designer present** — then hand over templates and worked examples rather than a long rulebook. (5642 · U7 · L104 · 00:00–00:31, 01:01–01:17, 01:17–01:30)

## 2. Principles and claims

- Identity is only one part of everything an entity communicates, though designers commonly use the word to cover the whole graphic image. (5642 · U7 · L92 · 00:08–00:29)
- Heraldry — the study of shields, flags and how such signs work together — already existed under that name as the historical precedent for identity systems. (5642 · U7 · L92 · 00:30–00:44)
- Mid-20th-century identity manuals turned loose descriptions ("a yellowish colour", "a sans-serif typeface") into fixed specifications (a Pantone number, Helvetica Bold). (5642 · U7 · L92 · 00:45–01:11)
- Screen presentations are usually left in the client's own hands, built in software that has to stay editable by a non-designer — which limits typography and composition and invites errors. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 00:49–01:43)
- Fanciful ("thematic") typefaces can serve a special effect but not corporate or formal use; well-made system or free fonts serve that purpose better. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 03:35–05:46)
- Mixing a centred axis with a flush-left axis on the same piece is one of the most common signs of amateur design. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 06:29–07:22)
- Modern flush-left design grows text right and down by default, the way signage systems do, letting pages grow mechanically instead of being manually re-fitted. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 09:21–10:09)
- Hyphenating flush-left text doesn't make sense — hyphenation belongs to justified text. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 10:10–10:39)
- An answer shouldn't appear on screen without its question; put both on one screen. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 11:34–12:04)
- There's a tendency to capitalise short words unnecessarily (agua, hoy) even when they aren't proper nouns. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 12:05–12:30)
- Bold is for emphasising what actually matters, not for repeating a keyword every time it appears. (5642 · U7 · L94 · 00:39–01:08)
- A process should be explained in reading order — left to right for the Latin alphabet — matching how narratives (before/after, now/future) are naturally read. (5642 · U7 · L94 · 03:42–04:56)
- "Vibrating" colours share the same tonal darkness, so figure and ground fight each other and neither reads as dominant. (5642 · U7 · L94 · 04:57–06:09)
- A stock photo carrying a visible watermark should never be used without buying the licence; such licences are inexpensive. (5642 · U7 · L94 · 06:10–06:48)
- Mixing a photographic image with a pictogram on the same page mixes two graphic languages; pick one. (5642 · U7 · L94 · 06:49–08:10)
- The underlying system of a design (axes, margins, growth direction, hierarchy) is reusable across many different typefaces, colours and moods — style is a separate decision from solving the structural problems. (5642 · U7 · L94 · 09:14–10:29)
- A stock image whose background eats into it (the "window effect") often means the image wasn't worth keeping in the first place. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 00:30–01:08)
- A calligraphic or script typeface is usually built to be read in mixed case; setting it in full capitals cramps and shrinks its letterforms. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 03:08–03:48)
- Don't mix a hand-lettered logotype with a typeset element trying to frame it, and don't mix positive and negative treatments arbitrarily — pick one and commit. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 04:38–05:28)
- Matching a recognised standard size (here, credit-card dimensions) can genuinely help users even for a small, everyday object. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 05:29–06:22)
- Horror vacui — filling a page for fear of leaving it empty — reads as untidy; a strong, simple scheme (e.g. a vertical division) reads as more contemporary. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 06:23–07:05)
- The back of a two-sided piece is real, usable design space. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 07:06–07:37)
- Spot colours stay bright and saturated in a way a CMYK build of the same colour cannot reproduce, but are hard to use beyond a small, special print run. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 08:38–10:58)
- In design, colours rarely carry fixed meaning but almost always connote a mood. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 15:08–16:00)
- A good design should be describable, and memorable enough to explain, in a single sentence — the "explain it by phone" idea. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 10:59–11:46)
- A mark's weakest, most reduced application (here, a publisher's mark on a book spine) is the real test of whether the mark works at all. (5642 · U7 · L96 · 01:48–02:32)
- The justified text block is built for long bodies of text; overusing it in a logotype or short piece of lettering looks old-fashioned and can create uneven stroke weight between lines of different length. (5642 · U7 · L96 · 02:32–04:03)
- False small caps result from mechanically shrinking capital letters rather than using a typeface's genuine small-caps set (mainly available, and mainly appropriate, in serif faces). (5642 · U7 · L96 · 04:03–05:43)
- Combining two typefaces that look too similar to each other defeats the point of combining them — there's no contrast to justify the pairing. (5642 · U7 · L96 · 05:43–06:19)
- A symbol should be designed with its most important application in mind, and a graphic system should be built to grow indefinitely rather than tailored separately to each application. (5642 · U7 · L96 · 06:19–07:26)
- A drop shadow, if used at all, should read as light falling from above (shadow below and to the right); in many cases it's better removed altogether. (5642 · U7 · L97 · 01:43–02:21)
- Filling a label's central space well, rather than leaving unplanned gaps, matters especially on a rotating surface like a bottle. (5642 · U7 · L97 · 02:21–03:05)
- Adding tracking (letter-spacing) to lower-case, italic, or script letters isn't advisable — that convention applies mainly to capitals. (5642 · U7 · L97 · 03:05–04:13)
- Applying a historical style without real reason is a common typographic mistake — even an artisanal product doesn't need fake period lettering. (5642 · U7 · L97 · 04:13–05:28)
- A typeface must be checked for the scripts/alphabets it actually needs to set (Latin, Cyrillic, Greek) before it's committed to, or text will need a second typeface partway through. (5642 · U7 · L97 · 08:12–09:58)
- Stock photography reads as fake to the viewer even when it's technically well made — the viewer already knows the staged scene isn't real. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 03:34–05:02)
- A set of individually good stock photos can still fail as a collection if they don't share a consistent optical/lighting/colour treatment. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 05:02–05:49)
- Arial is a substitute ("sucedáneo") for Helvetica, made to avoid paying royalties, with fewer weights and worse letterforms. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 02:14–03:34)
- Composing the same text in lower case, mixed case, or all capitals gives a noticeably different final effect; the choice of case is itself a design decision, not just a typeface choice. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 13:05–13:49)
- More geometry in a compositional grid doesn't necessarily mean more order — a designer shouldn't become a slave to their own system. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 13:49–14:57)
- Designers can and should propose the content itself (how much text, what kind of text) rather than only styling whatever the client hands over. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 08:18–10:27)
- Conceptual or abstract illustration can't explain every idea; a collection needs margin for what images can't cover, and should keep one consistent level of abstraction throughout. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 14:57–16:23)
- The same brief can be answered with several equally valid points on a gradient of abstraction, offered to the client as a choice rather than a single fixed design. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 12:09–13:05)
- An identity can benefit from deliberately not staying consistent: a very fixed, strongly styled logotype can work against integrating into a varied context (here, a theatre company's mark redrawn to match each show's own type). (5642 · U7 · L99 · 07:23–09:08)
- A "weak" but flexible base mark that keeps adapting and accumulating variations over time can be an asset, not a liability. (5642 · U7 · L99 · 09:08–10:23)
- No more than one or two things should "happen" in a logotype, or the viewer forgets what it's actually saying. (5642 · U7 · L100 · 05:05–07:31)
- A mark needs one unambiguous axis: if it's meant to be centred, it has to be centred; it shouldn't merely look symmetrical while several elements pull against each other. (5642 · U7 · L100 · 02:07–02:40)
- Legal denominations ("SA", "SL", "GmbH") make a company look small in a logotype; they belong on an invoice, not in the mark. (5642 · U7 · L100 · 05:05–07:31)
- Auxiliary graphic elements shouldn't exist purely to fill available space — use them only when they genuinely help. (5642 · U7 · L100 · 03:20–03:54, 03:54–04:43)
- Signage is often placed out of habit (the top of a façade) rather than where a pedestrian's eyes actually fall. (5642 · U7 · L100 · 02:40–03:20, 09:33–10:33)
- A logotype's own typeface doesn't have to be the typeface used for the rest of a brand's running text. (5642 · U7 · L100 · 11:49–12:46)
- A symbol that competes with or misrepresents its content (a jazz saxophone for a mixed-genre music festival) is a cliché worth dropping. (5642 · U7 · L101 · 02:40–03:59)
- Fixing inconsistency by moving to a tidy, orthogonal grid can still read as bland — that result is itself a cue to reconsider the typeface, not just its arrangement. (5642 · U7 · L101 · 02:40–03:59)
- Wood type — oversized 19th-century letters, first cut in wood for cost and weight once printing began serving posters read from a distance — is the ancestor of many display sans faces still used today (Impact, Gill Sans, systematic revivals like Knockout). (5642 · U7 · L101 · 03:59–07:48)
- A systematic type family with varying weight and width (Knockout, Frutiger, Univers) lets faces mix the way wood type originally did, but with far more range than the original fixed-size wood fonts. (5642 · U7 · L101 · 03:59–07:48)
- Equal width across a row of logos is not equal visual weight; sizes need optical, not mechanical, matching. (5642 · U7 · L101 · 08:57–10:26)
- When photography is supplied by outside parties rather than commissioned, unity has to come from the system around it (colour, type, layout), not from the photography itself. (5642 · U7 · L101 · 10:26–12:06)
- A single weak supplied photograph can drag down the level of an entire piece if left untreated. (5642 · U7 · L102 · 00:00–01:31)
- Tight or negative leading on a wood-type-style face can make accents or descenders collide with rules or with each other; redrawing the offending letterform by hand is normal, accepted practice. (5642 · U7 · L102 · 02:08–03:13)
- Outdoor communication needs strong contrast, and using the support's own colour (e.g. white canvas) as the background can supply it without needing a separate background colour. (5642 · U7 · L102 · 09:03–12:04)
- CMYK-to-RGB colour conversion is usually safe; RGB-to-CMYK usually isn't, because a colour built to look bright on screen often can't survive being printed. (5642 · U7 · L103 · 00:00–02:24)
- Some colours (fluorescents, some pastels and cream tones) don't reproduce faithfully in either RGB or CMYK. (5642 · U7 · L103 · 00:00–02:24)
- It isn't a bad idea to build a brand's core colours based on the medium it will mostly be seen in. (5642 · U7 · L103 · 00:00–02:24)
- The avatar (a small, roughly square-to-round space, present almost everywhere online) is a genuine design constraint, and doesn't have to carry the logotype at all. (5642 · U7 · L103 · 02:24–03:14)
- "Responsive" branding (an identity that simplifies for the space available) is a current label for something graphic design has always done, such as simplifying a mark for a book spine. (5642 · U7 · L103 · 03:14–04:24)
- Platform formats keep changing, so an identity should be planned for horizontal, square and vertical formats from the outset. (5642 · U7 · L103 · 04:24–05:43)
- An illegible logotype, or one carrying an unintended meaning, undermines everything built on top of it. (5642 · U7 · L104 · 00:00–00:31)
- A strong graphic system can carry an identity further than the logotype's own drawing does — a visually weak logotype can survive decades of redesign if the graphic language around it develops well. (5642 · U7 · L104 · 00:31–00:45)
- Build brand colours and typefaces to last; treat only genuinely seasonal, time-limited work as disposable by design. (5642 · U7 · L104 · 00:45–01:01)
- A brand shouldn't dominate what sits alongside it, and should be simple enough for someone else to apply correctly without the original designer present. (5642 · U7 · L104 · 01:01–01:17)
- Templates and worked examples get followed more reliably than a long, complicated set of written brand guidelines. (5642 · U7 · L104 · 01:17–01:30)

## 3. Checks the teacher uses

- **"Explain it by phone" test.** Ask whether the finished piece is memorable and describable enough to explain to someone verbally, in a sentence. Needs judgement by eye — there's no formula for memorability. (5642 · U7 · L95 · 10:59–11:46; reapplied at 5642 · U7 · L97 · 12:29–15:33)
- **Vibrating-colour check.** Look at two adjacent colours and ask whether they share the same tonal darkness, so figure and ground fight rather than one reading as dominant. Could be measured from a rendered image: compare the luminance/darkness value of the two colours. This check is colour-theory territory in substance; see Overlaps. (5642 · U7 · L94 · 04:57–06:09)
- **Column-width check.** Count the characters per line in a text block (the sample column, at 87 characters, was flagged as too wide) and compare against the type size. Measurable directly from the rendered text: characters per line versus point size. (5642 · U7 · L94 · 01:09–03:41)
- **Single-axis consistency check.** Look across every page or application for one shared centred or flush-left axis, and flag any element that sits on a different one. Measurable from a rendered image: compare each element's alignment coordinate (centre point or left edge) across the set. (5642 · U7 · L93 · 06:29–07:22; also 5642 · U7 · L100 · 02:07–02:40)
- **Overflow check.** Ask whether a text block actually fits its allotted screen or space, or whether content has had to spill onto a second screen. Measurable from a rendered image/layout: does the text block exceed the available frame. (5642 · U7 · L94 · 01:09–03:41)
- **Stock-photo credibility check.** Ask whether the viewer will read a photo as staged rather than real (extras standing in for a family, a couple that clearly isn't one). Needs judgement by eye. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 03:34–05:02)
- **Collection-consistency check.** Look at a set of photos or illustrations together and ask whether they share optics, treatment, colour and level of abstraction, not just subject matter. Partly measurable (colour values, treatment) but mostly judged by eye. (5642 · U7 · L98 · 05:02–05:49, 14:57–16:23)
- **Typeface script-coverage check.** Check whether a candidate typeface actually includes the alphabets/scripts a project needs (Latin, Cyrillic, Greek) before committing to it. Directly verifiable from the font's own character set, not a matter of judgement. (5642 · U7 · L97 · 08:12–09:58)
- **Weakest-application test.** Test a mark at its hardest, smallest, or most constrained application (a publisher's mark at spine width, a mark shrunk to avatar size, a mark reduced to fit a shop-front panel) before considering it finished. Needs judgement by eye, though it can be anchored to a stated minimum size. (5642 · U7 · L96 · 01:48–02:32; 5642 · U7 · L103 · 02:24–03:14; 5642 · U7 · L100 · 02:40–03:20)
- **Optical vs. mechanical size-matching check.** For a row of logos, ask whether they read as the same visual weight, not whether they share the same width or height. Needs judgement by eye — the check explicitly rejects a measurable, mechanical substitute (equal width). (5642 · U7 · L101 · 08:57–10:26)
- **Outdoor contrast check.** Compare a mark or type colour against its background (black on blue judged too weak; black or colour on white canvas judged strong). Could be measured from a rendered image: contrast between the mark's colour value and the background's. This check is colour-theory territory in substance; see Overlaps. (5642 · U7 · L101 · 07:48–08:57; 5642 · U7 · L102 · 09:03–12:04)
- **Letterform-collision check.** At tight or negative leading, look for an accent, descender or ascender colliding with a rule or another letterform. Measurable from a rendered image: overlapping bounding boxes or a lack of white space between the glyph and its neighbour. (5642 · U7 · L102 · 02:08–03:13)
- **RGB/CMYK round-trip check.** Convert a brand colour between the two colour systems and compare the result against the original. Directly measurable: compare colour values/appearance before and after conversion. This check is colour-theory territory in substance; see Overlaps. (5642 · U7 · L103 · 00:00–02:24)
- **Basic legibility check.** Read the finished logotype and ask simply whether it reads well and doesn't say something unintended. Needs judgement by eye. (5642 · U7 · L104 · 00:00–00:31)
