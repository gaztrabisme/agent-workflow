## Introduction: images come first

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L52 · 00:00–00:57

Images, without question, play a fundamental role in any communication project. This lesson explains how images work: some typologies for them, how they behave within their own limits, and how they behave with each other and in relation to space.

Images are the first thing we look at in a piece of design. They're direct, fast, emotional, and they spark curiosity — they are not subordinate elements in a composition, so don't underestimate them. The first thing to understand is that every piece of iconography has an intention, an expression and a system of interpretation. This course won't get into the complexity of interpreting an image; instead, it stays in the area of expression.

## Classifying images by complexity

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L52 · 00:57–01:43

One way to look at images is through their complexity. Following the principles of simplicity, images can be classified by their degree of complexity. The first criterion is set by evaluating the number of elements or visual attributes an image has. The more elements, shades, colors, pieces and parts it has, the more size it may demand in the composition so its complexity can be properly appreciated — but at the same time, an image reduced to very few elements can also raise its own complexity, because the result feels novel.

## Iconicity: how closely an image resembles its subject

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L52 · 01:43–02:29

Another criterion is iconicity (*iconicidad*), which refers to how similar an image is to the object it represents. A realistic photograph sits at one end of this index; at the other, most abstract end, sits a typographic expression — an acronym like "CR7" carries none of the physical features of Cristiano Ronaldo, the object it represents.

![5642 · U4 · L52 · 02:07](frames/U04L52-F0207.jpg)

On screen: "CR7".  
Shows: A photograph of the footballer Cristiano Ronaldo in play, paired by a double arrow with the acronym "CR7" set in bold type — the same subject at opposite ends of the iconicity scale.

If an image's iconicity is high, it can condition the other elements in a composition: they have to live alongside that degree of realism and give it the prominence it calls for. If, on the other hand, an image leans toward abstract expression, it can demand size or contrast in order to be read at all.

![5642 · U4 · L52 · 02:28](frames/U04L52-F0228.jpg)

On screen: "Heinz 57 Varieties" (label); "Emigre" (magazine masthead) [rest omitted].  
Shows: A Heinz ketchup advertisement where an aloe sprout grows from the bottle's cap, beside an orange-toned Emigre magazine cover collaged from camera equipment shapes — a photorealistic product shot and an abstract collage at opposite ends of the same iconicity scale.

## Descriptive and narrative images

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L52 · 02:29–03:27

Another typology for approaching images is to classify them as descriptive or narrative. Descriptive topographic images describe a natural or artificial space; they demand attributes that clarify their parts, because they tend to be groups of objects. Descriptive portraits represent a person's physical characteristics, some psychological or emotional circumstance, or a social condition. This last category tends to use a motif (*motivo*) — an attribute or an object — to associate the person with a social group; the composition has to prioritize that motif, or the portrait won't be understood.

Shows: A twilight desert highway beneath red rock cliffs, beside a rendered modern bedroom interior with a green accent wall and a perforated screen — a natural space and an artificial one, the two kinds of descriptive topographic image.

Finally, there are narrative images: they represent more than an object — an event, a moment within a process. It can be inferred that there was a situation before it, and one after.

## Framing, shot scale and camera angle

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L52 · 03:27–04:55

Photography and illustration also share many variables that describe their expression. Light, structural lines and color are some of the attributes that connect shapes in space. In these images there is also a factor called framing (*encuadre*) — the format that acts as a frame for the image; as we know, its perceptual axes push at the shapes it contains.

![5642 · U4 · L52 · 04:14](frames/U04L52-F0414.jpg)

On screen: "Esquire" / "'Merry Christmas. I'm the 100th G.I. killed in Vietnam.'"; "Interview" / "Marion Cotillard"; "Esquire" / "Sex and the British Man".  
Shows: Three magazine covers with very different lighting, cropping and color treatment — a grainy black-and-white wartime portrait, a moody close-up lit mostly in shadow, and a bright close-cropped detail shot — illustrating how light, line and color each shape an image's expression.

Shot scale (*plano*) is the way figures occupy the frame, the format. In wide shots, the shapes and their forces are more scattered; in medium shots, the figure takes on structural prominence. As shots close in, abstraction increases: the iconicity index drops, and we see heavier, more solid-feeling forms. Camera angle (*emplazamiento*) — the observer's tilt relative to the object — produces different dynamic forces, and this changes how images in a composition speak to one another.

![5642 · U4 · L52 · 04:27](frames/U04L52-F0427.jpg)

On screen: "Acabando esta entrevista vuelas a Kuwait. ¿Qué harás ahí?" [Wrapping up this interview, you're flying to Kuwait. What will you do there?], "¿Qué otros proyectos tienes ahora?" [What other projects do you have now?], "Sigues jugando Jaime…" [You're still playing, Jaime…], "¿Y cuál es el papel del disfraz en tu vida?" [And what role does costume play in your life?] [rest omitted].  
Shows: A magazine spread combining a sketchbook close-up, a chromed horse-head sculpture, and a chandelier installation shot at different distances, beside a Spanish-language interview with several question headings — an example of how varied framing and shot scale can sit on the same spread.

An image that advances needs more air ahead of it. A person looking downward leads the reader's eye with their gaze, which is useful for placing elements lower in the frame; images with a steep downward tilt (*picada*) tend to work better in that lower part of the frame, and an upward tilt (*contrapicada*) in the upper part. This treatment simulates the moment of observation and projects the image beyond its own limits.

On screen: "Villa Verdin" [rest omitted]; "May 12" / "Good Room" / "Bambounou" / "Dan Wender" / "Blacky II".  
Shows: Two posters: a small boat trailing a diagonal wake across a dark blue field, and a concert poster where a man's head, resting on his hand, tilts downward over the event listing beneath it.

## Bleeding, color and grouping as compositional pull

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L52 · 04:55–05:25

Bleeding images — running them past the edge of the format — lets you crop within the shot, focus on the key parts, and give the piece a greater sense of dimension.

![5642 · U4 · L52 · 04:55](frames/U04L52-F0455.jpg)

On screen: "contacts for construction" / "2013.06. START" [rest omitted]; "KII+O:" (KIITO, the Kobe Design and Creative Center) [rest omitted].  
Shows: An open book spread showing a black-and-white street photograph of Barcelona, beside a yellow poster for a dance workshop, "Contacts for Construction," a triangular diagram overlaid on a photo of dancers — both examples bleeding their images past the page's own edge.

Color, diagonal placement and the weight of some images pull the subordinate ones toward the suggested movement. Grouping makes for more solid sets where individual images would otherwise falter, stretching the composition toward them, as in the example just shown.

## Analyzing a photographic portfolio

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L52 · 05:25–07:57

With these concepts in mind, the material available for the publication being designed can now be analyzed. The first source is a photographic project by Rosario Curi — more a portfolio, really, documenting the area just outside central Barcelona, at its edges, where a significant urban transformation took place: many people had to migrate, and many of those places are no longer the same.

![5642 · U4 · L52 · 05:36](frames/U04L52-F0536.jpg)

On screen: [none].  
Shows: Twenty photographs from the Rosario Curi project laid out in a strict five-by-four grid, mixing portraits with topographic shots of streets and buildings.

Looking closer, the grid is very clear because its images sit on very symmetrical axes, both in the portraits and in the topographic images. The portraits are all what's called prosopographic (*prosopográficos*) — meaning they describe the relationship between the sitter and a social group — so they need their attributes to stand out. One such attribute, a motif, is the canary cages this man is holding; it's important to give that photo enough size for the cages to be seen and understood.

![5642 · U4 · L52 · 06:27](frames/U04L52-F0627.jpg)

On screen: [none].  
Shows: A cropped, enlarged detail of the man's canary cages resting on the ground beside his legs, showing the birds inside clearly.

Because all the axes are so symmetrical, other elements may be needed to relate the images in space — they shouldn't sit too solidly on their own. This isn't a stand-alone portfolio book; it's being folded into a larger project, so the page layout's intentions and forces need to work with these strongly marked axes. For example, one image has a very marked diagonal that could be related to another image with an equally marked diagonal, and that starts to generate paths through the graphic space.

## A second material: products, places, and imposing a structure

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L52 · 07:57–08:56

The other iconographic material is, in a way, the opposite problem: it's harder to find clear relationships beyond the lighting and the variety of shots — interesting on its own, though a medium shot is missing, since this material is really about people. This second material is for an article about changing customs, or rather about the activities of people leaving the city and taking up more rural pursuits. So there are products, there are places, but people are missing — and since this material is already fixed, it's likely they simply can't be added.

## Completing the mood board

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L52 · 08:56–11:04

The mood board is now complete, with every element gathered across all the observations made along the way. There are forms of interest to reproduce, and counterforms too — very white, very generous, very open counterforms, alongside strongly marked, angular forms — plus an interest in the verticality of columns that stretch the spaces upward and downward. The colors and images, it seems, can stand out against a very white, open structure; it's not settled whether they need a background color, or whether everything has to be this minimal — but it's interesting to see how the images function with unconventional crops and situations.

There is also a typographic direction of interest: something much softer that contrasts nicely — not only the Cooper Black-like typeface, but also raw, direct headline treatments with no effects and no restraint in their spacing, which feels very honest. There's also a fondness for how the folios' structural elements build a grid that invites being broken; the page numbers' size is appealing too, giving the whole piece an interesting general unity.

### Key points (derived)

- Images are not secondary in a composition — they're the first thing read, and they carry direct, fast, emotional weight of their own.
- Complexity can be judged by counting an image's elements and attributes, but an image reduced to very few elements can read as complex too, simply by being unusual.
- Iconicity places images on a scale from realistic photograph to pure typographic abstraction (like an acronym); high-iconicity images demand the rest of a composition defer to their realism, while abstract images need size or contrast to read at all.
- Images can be sorted as descriptive (topographic or portrait, often built around a defining motif) or narrative (implying a before and an after).
- Framing, shot scale and camera angle all push at the forms inside an image — closing the shot raises abstraction, and tilt (picada/contrapicada) changes where an image wants to sit and lead the eye.
- Bleeding an image past the frame's edge is a direct way to crop toward its key parts and add a sense of scale.
- Diagonals, shared color and simple grouping are all ways to build relationships between otherwise independent images and give a set of them more solidity.
- Two visual materials can call for opposite strategies: read a structure out of one when its own axes are strong and clear, or impose a structure on the other when it lacks internal relationships.
