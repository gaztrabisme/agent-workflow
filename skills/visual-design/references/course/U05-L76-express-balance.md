# Lesson 76 · Express/Balance

## Balance as physics: forces that cancel out

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L76 · 00:06–00:58

This third lesson is about balance (*equilibrio*). Balance is closely tied to force in physics: a body is in balance when a set of forces acting on it cancel each other out. Take this piece of elastic, for example: when it's pulled from both ends, the pulls cancel out, and this piece — this line, a straight line — is in balance.

If a straight line is defined as the shortest distance between two points, then two opposing forces can be said to act on that line, one at each end, in opposite directions. That's something like what's happening here with this elastic. When that happens, the straight line is a balanced figure — it's the starting principle of any balanced figure.

## From line to triangle

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L76 · 00:58–01:58

Moving on: if another line is added in a different direction, an angle is created. Closing that figure produces a triangle — a triangle whose forces, when summed, act with equal strength. So there's a line, and another line, and the forces running along each act here, here and here, and all three cancel out. That's a balanced figure: an equilateral triangle.

## The square: four right angles in balance

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L76 · 01:58–02:40

Taking this same concept further: making a square means superimposing a shape so that its two lines cancel each other out exactly, because both lines cross at the centre and what one pulls upward and downward, the other pulls right and left, neutralizing each other. That gives the start of another balanced figure, the square — its right angles act on all four vertices with the same intensity, so they cancel out, giving another figure of perfect balance.

## The circle: infinite balanced lines

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L76 · 02:40–03:53

Taking that same line and multiplying it around a central axis — acting this way, then repeating the action an infinite number of times — produces a surface, a series of points that eventually join up and form a circle. A circle is made up of a huge number of straight lines acting outward with the same intensity: another regular, balanced figure.

Taking this into three dimensions gives a square base raised evenly in every direction — a solid figure that is also balanced, one where every point carries the same force and they cancel each other out.

## Imbalance begins: bending a line

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L76 · 04:04–05:03

From here the subject turns to imbalance. Imbalance always carries a set of forces that get out of control, that pull away from the whole. The first thing to look at is the line: when a straight line has something acting on one of its points and deforming it, a force throws the two ends out of control — the two forces that used to meet at the line's ends now separate, and there's more energy concentrated at that point than at the extremes. This energy increases as the angle is pulled more sharply closed, becoming more acute (captions: *más obtuso*, "more obtuse") — the more tightly it's closed, the more energy that angle contains compared to a wider one. As the angle closes further, the resulting figure carries more and more energy.

Shows: Two separate angle shapes drawn on the board — a wide, shallow "peak" angle and, beside it, a narrower, sharper angle — comparing how a tighter angle concentrates more visual energy.

## The same energy in triangle and square

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L76 · 05:03–05:45

This same idea applies to every geometric figure. Relative to the square and to three- and four-sided elements generally: closing the angles of the equilateral triangle sharpens its imbalance the more those angles are closed. The energy becomes far more intense, concentrated above all at the point where it's sharpest. The same thing happens with the square — its energy concentrates along whichever side is being altered, and so on for every figure. As the angles are pushed further, they read as more energetic; relaxed, they read as more passive.

## Breaking a line into a curve

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L76 · 05:45–06:20

Now for the curve. A straight line broken at one point becomes a bent, broken line — a force has acted on one point of a straight line and turned it into two straight lines meeting at an angle. But when a force acts on every point along the line with the same energy, and there are two such forces working as in this demonstration, a double force is generated — the two pulling from the sides produce a single force emerging toward the centre, and it creates a very powerful internal force.

## Deforming the circle: oval, egg, kidney

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L76 · 06:20–07:44

Several closed shapes will now be shown on screen — each, in the end, a result of deforming the circle. The gentlest deformation of a circle is an oval: making a cross where the forces acting on it are unequal on each side produces an oval shape. The egg shape below carries much more energy at its rear than at its top, as can be seen here.

This is where the deformation of the circle becomes an internal shape that counteracts in the opposite direction to all its interior forces — exactly the way a finger pressing on this balloon behaves: there's always a tendency for the shape to spring back. In that sense, a curved line is always, in the mind, a straight line that has been deformed and that tends to return to its natural state; the same is true of the circle, which is why these shapes hold so much energy. Examples of how this has been used, from Arp to Picasso's heads, were mentioned earlier — the energy these forms contain, by way of anecdote.

## Pushing a shape's expression by moving it

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L76 · 13:52–14:54

Finally, that internal energy of a shape can be increased — not just its presence in the image, but its inner potency — by working the piece within the format, that is, by placing it deliberately within the composition. This shape now on screen is made of a curve and a counter-curve; it's an absolutely balanced shape, and yet unbalanced, carrying all its weight in the upper part. At the time, the aim was to push its expression as far as possible.

To do that, it was moved through the space to see how it reacted, how it behaved — the best system, really, for judging and analysing it. Naturally, the pull the frame exerts on the left side increases the unity and the potency of the shape considerably, adding real force to the energy concentrated there. Finally, to increase this sense of movement, of fleeing toward the left, breaking the frame with this piece entering it creates a line in that direction, which further supports the movement and the energy already in the curve. With this element, the basic compositional idea already established gets an extra layer of support.

### Key points (derived)

- Balance, in the strict sense used here, means forces cancelling out: a straight line, an equilateral triangle, a square, a circle and a sphere are each built from forces acting with equal intensity in opposing directions.
- Imbalance comes from forces going out of control: bending a line, sharpening an angle, or deforming a circle into an oval, egg or more complex curve all concentrate energy at specific points.
- The more complex and irregular a curve, the more energy and visual interest it carries — a plain oval reads as far calmer than an asymmetric, twisting one.
- A shape's own internal direction (set by where its curves and angles meet) can be reinforced or opposed by the frame's pull, by position within the format, or by a counterweight.
- Imbalance can also come from absence: a missing part of a figure, or a hidden section of a grid, creates visual tension precisely because the eye expects to find it.
- These principles apply equally in two dimensions (drawing, page layout) and in three (sculpture, plated food, bonsai), and often several act together in a single piece.
