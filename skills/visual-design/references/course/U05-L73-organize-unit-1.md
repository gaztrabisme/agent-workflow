## Composing means ordering, and the mind seeks the simplest reading

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L73 · 00:00–00:53

This last unit of the course is built from four exercises, covering four of the principles of perception. The first is compositional unity (*unidad compositiva*). Composing is ordering a set of scattered elements within a space so that they read as visually attractive — so that the eyes carry an image to the mind that is understandable, pleasant, and comprehensible enough to make us want to keep looking at it.

The human mind always tries, when it sees different elements, to group them, to join them, to simplify — to reduce what it sees to the minimum needed to understand it.

## The mind completes what it is shown

Take a cable tie (*brida*) — the mind sees, almost, the shape of an apple, and add one more element and it becomes unmistakably an apple: you no longer see a cable tie with a piece of glass, you see an apple.

## Unity is the first job of composition

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L73 · 01:52–02:56

As already said, composing means ordering a set of disparate elements within a space, and the first thing to do is create unity — an order, a category. The human mind hates chaos, hates disorder, hates not knowing where to direct itself. So the first task is to order and bring together, into a single piece, the whole image in front of us. This concept of unity is one of the fundamental principles of composition.

There are several resources for achieving it. One is superposition (*superposición*): when there are different elements in the compositional space, simply crossing these pieces over one another creates a unity — we see it as a single piece rather than, for instance, three separate branches.

## Structure: a second way to create unity

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L73 · 06:55–07:23

Having covered the first point of composition, there is another element worth discussing: structure — an element that joins and compacts the different pieces within a composition. Another way to unify a set of elements in the compositional space is the grid or reticule (*retícula*): a structure that contains the different elements.

## Grids that hold together dispersed material

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L73 · 07:23–08:26

In this case, the elements have very different forms and are only loosely united by colour, but they are held together because each one touches some edge of a 10-by-10 grid in the space. That creates a net that, for reasons hard to pin down, holds the composition together simply through that grid.

## Overlap creates depth, like a stage set

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L73 · 12:17–13:00

Two elements are superposed here, and each line also overlaps the other, creating a network that becomes even more powerful, even more closed. This recalls something mentioned when the *Grafía Callada* book was first presented: its interest lay in a double superposition — on one hand the unity created by the grid, on the other the lines that fall onto the white in a somewhat random way. Layering two unities together so they merge into one, even though you can still half-read them as two, has its own charm, because it gives depth. Superposing elements is how depth begins to appear — it is like building a kind of theatre stage, placing some things behind others. A grid is very powerful when its structure is tightly closed and its forms are very alike: that creates a very, very strong unity.

## A layered map: the final example

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L73 · 13:38–14:33

This image is very interesting for the superposition already discussed, in terms of layers. Here there are things that, as with the caps and the branches, are superposed with nothing obviously in common: the oval shapes that give form to the map of the Comunitat Valenciana; the borders, reinforced by lines like rings; a first layer of text grouped together because it sits inside a grid working at 90 degrees, itself set at 45 degrees to the support; and, separately, the headline, integrated at the same angle and in the same colour. With that, he has shown examples of how superposition and the similarity of elements go on to create unity — and now he would like to move on to another way of creating the unity composition requires.

### Key points (derived)

- Unity is the mind's own habit: shown a triangle of dots, a near-apple, or grouped teardrops, the eye completes the form on its own — a resource the designer can lean on deliberately, even leaving a form incomplete.
- Superposition (overlapping unrelated elements) is presented as the most elementary way to force unity; proximity and similarity of shape, colour, or line direction reinforce it further, and stacking several of these resources compounds it.
- A structure (grid, silhouette, column, single line) is a second, independent route to unity: it can hold together material that shares nothing else, and can itself nest smaller unities inside a larger one.
- Some examples are shown specifically at the edge of legibility ("al borde del caos") to demonstrate how little unity a composition can retain before it reads as pure chaos.
- Superposing layers also creates depth — the teacher compares it explicitly to building a theatre stage, placing elements behind one another.
