## Pairing typefaces

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L13 · 00:00–02:18

The previous lesson advised against using more than two typefaces unless there is a clear purpose. Two faces that look almost the same can make a design feel noisy, so choose faces with a visible difference. A reliable combination is Futura, a sans serif (*sin serifa*), with Garamond, a serif (*con serifa*). Their styles contrast, but their proportions work together. Their capital A letters both begin from a similarly proportioned triangle, and the crossbar sits at a similar height. Their lowercase O forms are also close to circles.

This pairing of a serif and a sans serif appears in posters where the two typefaces form part of the main design. A second example combines two faces in a two-word name; because the words work almost like a logo, the designer can let them “dance” without giving each typeface a strictly separate role. The U in “Studio” is italic, joining the lettering into an image. This practice is called font pairing (*pairing*). Google Fonts has a Pairings tab: choose a typeface and the site suggests others whose proportions work with it.

## Alignment and line length

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L13 · 02:18–03:41

When laying out paragraphs, left alignment is usually the easiest to read for people who read from left to right, as in Western reading. Centering or right-aligning a long paragraph may fill the space neatly, but the line endings jump around and make it harder to follow. These alignments can still work in short, narrow contexts such as Instagram posts, where the screen is not very wide; use them occasionally and deliberately.

Line length should fit the type size. Small type can sit in shorter lines, but small type in a very long line is tiring to track. Large type in very short lines forces the reader to jump down repeatedly. Look for a middle ground: a medium line for small type, and a line that is not too short for larger type.

## Adjust spacing for size

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L13 · 03:41–05:19

Typeface defaults are not optimized for every use. Letter spacing (*interletrado*) and line spacing (*interlineado*) can make text more compact and comfortable. With very large text, the spaces between letters and lines look bigger, so reduce them and bring the letters and lines closer. With small text, default spacing can feel cramped; increase letter and line spacing a little.

Kerning (*kerning*) is the spacing between a particular pair of letters. The default spacing between letters does not always suit every combination: a capital T beside a lowercase O needs a different adjustment from a capital T beside a capital O. These pair-specific exceptions are kerning.

## Check a typeface before using it

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L13 · 05:19–06:09

When downloading a typeface, check that it is consistent and that the spacing has been designed carefully. Some fonts may lack characters needed for a particular language. Others may have uneven letter shapes—for example, an O that is very heavy beside an A that is very light—so the letters do not sit well together. Some free-download fonts also use the same space between every pair of letters and do not account for kerning exceptions. The next lesson applies these typography principles to the course project.

### Key points (derived)

- Pair visibly different typefaces whose proportions still relate.
- Choose alignment and line length for the reading direction, type size, and display context.
- Adjust tracking, leading, and individual kerning pairs; check language coverage and consistency before using a font.
