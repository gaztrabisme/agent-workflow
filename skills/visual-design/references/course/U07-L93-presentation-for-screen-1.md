## Why these presentations are a design blind spot

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L93 · 00:49–01:43

Screen presentations are generally left in the company's own hands; designers rarely make them, and in the end they're made within the software's own defaults. They project a company's image very significantly, and on top of that they have a problem: they need to be editable documents. That means we have to work with a type of software — frankly, PowerPoint or similar — that has to be usable by someone who isn't a graphic designer, because this content keeps being generated, has to be modified, gets reused from one presentation to the next, and so we're fairly limited in the typefaces, the composition, and a whole range of other things we can use. And on top of that, whoever makes them doesn't have a designer's habits, and falls into a series of errors.

## Fixing the basics: autocorrect and aspect ratio

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L93 · 02:40–03:29

Let's start with the home page. One thing we should remember is to turn off the autocorrect function that flags spelling, because it looks quite ugly when it shows up throughout the presentation. Normally, in full-screen mode, these applications make that function disappear, but sometimes, if we're not working from our own computer, it can keep showing up throughout the presentation — which is fairly annoying. Another thing we might want to reconsider nowadays is whether we should keep working with the default 4:3 format; these days we mostly work in 16:9. We normally use monitors with this other ratio, which is now the other standard, so it's worth considering switching to this format, which we can then also use on other media, such as video.

## Choosing typefaces: from Hobo and Arial to Calibri or Open Sans

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L93 · 03:35–05:46

Next, let's talk for a moment about typefaces. As we've seen, this piece uses two typefaces, Hobo and Arial — two typefaces found in every operating system, chosen here, it seems, because they read as fanciful or fluid, being associated with water — whatever that means in the case of Hobo and Arial, which is the more formal, default typeface. Working with typefaces that come with the operating system is almost mandatory here, because we're working with a document that might move from computer to computer, so we can't rely on typefaces we'd have to install on that machine — but in this case we should avoid the more fanciful ones. This kind of fanciful typeface can serve certain effects — for a logo, for something special, let's say — but for something more corporate we have, for example, a very good solution close at hand: an excellent typeface, Calibri. Calibri, even though we have it readily to hand, doesn't seem like it should be a very good typeface — but it was custom-made by Lucas de Groot, a great type designer who created it for Microsoft, and it works excellently at both large and small sizes. It also comes in several weights, in roman and in italic, so we can work very well with these letters — or, staying in the world of free typefaces, we can work with Open Sans, for instance, which isn't in the operating system but can easily be downloaded from Google Fonts. In other words, we have typefaces available to us that are, let's say, not fanciful, but rather suited to corporate use, signage, more formal purposes, or a more contemporary design, and are perfectly serviceable. So there's no need to use this more fanciful kind of lettering, when we have available to us, above all, free typefaces that come with the operating system and work perfectly. So here a good change would already be to switch to Calibri, for example. We've replaced both Arial and Hobo with these typefaces, and now we have something a little more formal, or more modern.

## The growth principle: text that grows right and down

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L93 · 09:21–10:09

That is, modern design — design from the mid-20th century onward — is a kind of design where, by default so to speak, text is almost always set flush left (*a caja izquierda*) and grows to the right and downward. We can see this very clearly, for example, in signage systems. Take a sign with several directions: those directions are, of course, different words with different lengths, so we're almost always left with white space to the right and at the bottom. This lets us grow mechanically, without having to keep adjusting the text to whatever space we happen to have. This idea of text that always grows in the same direction will help us keep every page in order.

## Dropping the hyphens

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L93 · 10:10–10:39

Another thing we can also do is stop hyphenating words. Hyphenating makes sense when we justify text, but if we're setting it flush left, it doesn't make much sense. So we can drop that too, which gives us better-ordered paragraphs, without this word-breaking that, after all, is still a difficulty for reading.

## One screen for the question and the answer

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L93 · 11:34–12:04

Remember that in some of the other screens we had, we set out a question and an answer on two separate screens. Frankly, it's better to work with just one question and one answer on the same screen, simply having the second sentence appear afterward. ### Key points (derived)

- Screen presentations are usually built by the client, not the designer, in software (PowerPoint or similar) that has to stay editable by non-designers — which limits typography and composition and invites errors.
- Turn off autocorrect's spelling underline before presenting, and consider today's 16:9 monitors over the legacy 4:3 default.
- Avoid decorative mid-word capitals and "fantasy" typefaces (Hobo, Papyrus, Brush Script, Marker Felt); prefer a well-made system font such as Calibri (by Lucas de Groot) or a free download such as Open Sans.
- Build hierarchy either through a case distinction (capitals vs lowercase) or through weight (bold vs light); switching to weight also let the teacher drop the extra line spacing between levels.
- Keep one consistent construction axis across a deck; mixing a centred axis with a flush-left axis is a common sign of amateur design.
- Simplify decoration: a regular frame beats an irregular one, a plain colour beats a busy photo background, and a thin band can replace a heavy frame.
- Once a header band exists, use its edges as the margins for body text, and remove excess top space.
- Modern flush-left design grows text to the right and down, the way signage systems do — which lets pages grow mechanically instead of being manually adjusted, and makes hyphenation at line breaks unnecessary.
- Apply the same system consistently to the title (and closing) page, but let it read as visually special within that continuity.
- Merge a question and its answer onto a single screen, not two, and don't show an answer without its question.
- Watch for unnecessary capitals on ordinary words (agua, hoy) — a small detail, but worth fixing.
