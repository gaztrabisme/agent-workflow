## Why work with waste material

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L70 · 00:00–01:08

So far the teacher has covered his professional profile, his personal projects, and his relationship with waste material. Now he wants to explain why working with waste material is worthwhile. Leaving aside the ethical and environmental reasons — a way of denouncing the hyper-consumption of the society we live in — there is also a point of redemption in the matter itself: giving a second chance to material that we throw away, that we treat with contempt.

But beyond the ethical and environmental motives, he believes the fundamental reason waste material matters for this course, and for working with the principles of visual perception, is the limitations and constraints this kind of material imposes.

## The real reason: limitations that generate solutions

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L70 · 01:08–01:53

Waste material brings very interesting qualities: it contributes forms, values, textures — a whole series of important and interesting elements. But at the same time, waste material cannot be enlarged or reduced, at least not the way he uses it, and he cannot modify it either. That places a series of limitations on the person handling it, limitations the author has to overcome. And those very limitations that must be overcome sometimes give rise to the solution itself.

## A designer's parallel: the editor's brief

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L70 · 01:53–02:46

This reminds him strongly of the work of a graphic designer (*diseñador*) faced with a commission. For example, when someone is handed a set of texts with headlines — as in the case he is about to show — a series of headlines that need to be highlighted, texts that need to be weighed against each other, a page count that must be respected, and a set of images with titles that must be laid out and ordered within those pages.

These are limitations very similar to the ones faced when working with waste material — limitations that have to be overcome. The texts and images are what then get converted, by applying the principles of perception, into an attractive page layout.

## The Suma+Sigue example: a grid that crosses pages

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L70 · 02:46–04:09

He had texts he wanted to work with, headlines he wanted to bring forward, and images whose titles he also wanted to highlight. The concept the team followed was to build a kind of grid (*rejilla*) in which the headlines cross the pages. These headlines were inserted alongside images that carried an important title of their own, and each headline carried its own accompanying text.

They achieved this layout through two concepts. First, the grid itself — the constant repetition that gives unity to every page. Second, the text: sometimes longer, sometimes shorter, which brings variety to the page within that same grid, some blocks running to several short lines and others running longer. Finally, the captions and comments that accompany each image act as small brushstrokes that break up the rigidity of the page's structure. That is how, little by little, they were able to build the page structure they needed out of the very limitations the project imposed.

## Working with design material sharpens focus on form

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L70 · 04:09–04:50

Another reason he finds this kind of work valuable, beyond the limitations it offers, is that it lets him work with existing design material — working purely with structures, with the structure of the image itself. That focuses attention much more closely on the principles of perception, on the centre and axis of each exercise. In other words, it sets aside whatever narrative an image might otherwise tell, and bases the work solely on form. He considers this a very interesting example that can later be applied across many different disciplines.

## Four principles ahead: composition, categorising, balance, movement

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L70 · 04:50–05:48

The exercises he is proposing cover several of the principles of visual perception. First, composition: how to organise elements within a space, the compositional space itself. Second, categorising: giving that series of elements proportion relative to the format, deciding the colour each should have, the presence each should have within the format, the visual weight each one should carry — in other words, deciding what is visually number one, the most important point, then number two, number three, and so on to the end.

After that, he will discuss balance and imbalance, which carry the expression of the form — or its calm — and how the tension and internal forces that generate imbalance lead into the final exercise: movement, and how that internal tension and movement translate into visual movement.

### Key points (derived)

- The teacher frames waste material's core value for this course as constraint, not virtue: it cannot be resized or altered, and those limits are what force a solution.
- He draws a direct parallel to editorial design: a brief with fixed headlines, texts and page count creates the same kind of productive constraint as waste material.
- His own worked example is "Suma y Sigue," a magazine of contemporary art: a repeating grid gives the pages unity, variable text length gives them variety, and captions break up the grid's rigidity.
- Working with existing design material (rather than narrative imagery) keeps the focus on pure structure and form — which is why the course's exercises use waste material rather than pictures with stories to tell.
- The four principles to come, in order: composition (organising elements in space), categorising (assigning weight and importance), balance/imbalance (tension and calm), and movement (translating internal tension into visual movement).
