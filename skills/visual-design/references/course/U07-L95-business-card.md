## Intro: fixing a single-application identity

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 00:09–00:29

In this lesson we're going to look at a very common case: a business that depends on a single piece, a business card. We'll review the logotype and give it identity with the help of a professional. And in this case too, we'll look at some alternatives at the end of the lesson.

## Starting point: the stock photo and the "window effect"

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 00:30–01:08

Let's start with an image whose main application would be a business card. This would be the case of a hair salon, where we clearly have a stock image and the name of the stylist, or the owner, or whoever runs the place. We're talking about a single application in the sense that there are probably other elements still to fill in, but here we'll focus on the card as the strongest element from a commercial point of view. We have this stock image, which would surely work better against a white background. This is what we call the window effect: working with this coloured background ends up eating into the image a little. We'll come back later to whether it's worth using this kind of image at all, but in any case, one thing we should avoid, if we do work with images like this, is a background that eats the image — the image has to stand out.

## Apostrophe, prime and accent: getting the character right

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 02:15–03:07

It's a very common error, and honestly, sometimes it's not even our fault: when we type the key for this character on our keyboard — the one that comes after the zero on most keyboards — we get this straight mark. Here we show it in different typefaces so we can see its different shapes. This isn't correct. Nor is this — which is actually an accent mark, often used where an apostrophe belongs — that isn't correct either. What's correct is what we see here on the right: this is a genuine apostrophe. As you can see, it takes different shapes across different typefaces, but it's a character we need to make a point of using correctly — especially important not just in headlines, but in logotypes, or anything meant to last, a form we're meant to remember. It matters to use the right character in these cases.

## A calligraphic face wants lower case, not caps

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 03:08–03:48

Once we've fixed this character, we should also ask ourselves whether this typeface should be used in capitals at all. This face we're using — Zapfino-style (*Zafino*) — imitates calligraphy, and faces like this are really meant to be used mixing capitals and lowercase. The R, this first letter we see, clearly has a form that dips below the lowercase letters, and these faces generally work much better in lowercase. So if we use typefaces like this, it's best to set them in lowercase — and what will surely happen, as it did here, is that it will look a lot smaller, so we can simply enlarge it.

## One script, one voice: fixing the description text

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 04:38–05:28

Another element now tied to this new logotype is the product description. Here we run into a problem: we're mixing styles between hand-lettering and typeset text, and we're also mixing positive and negative. The logotype text is reversed out (negative), while the typeset writing is set in positive. One thing we could do is pull this unnecessary description out from around the edges of our new logotype — that is, stop trying to enclose the logotype with this kind of typographic formula — and, while we're at it, switch it to positive. ## A bigger logo, and a vertical axis instead of horror vacui

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 06:23–07:05

We can make this logotype bigger — we have plenty of space, and it's the main element — and now might also be the moment to rethink how these other texts are distributed. We're occupying the centre of the card and distributing things as if we were just filling up the card's space. This is a somewhat untidy way of handling space, of handling composition, without really thinking about how we're filling it. Sometimes we're almost afraid of the space we're working with. ## Spot colour versus CMYK

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 08:38–10:58

Let's talk for a moment about colour. We've been using this maroon we had in the background of the card's first version, but since we're now working with only two inks — we no longer have the old photograph — this might be the moment to take advantage of working with a low number of inks, and use what we call a spot colour (*tinta plana*). Blue, for instance. This particular blue is an electric blue, a fairly bright blue, one that can't be reproduced in four-colour process — that is, a blue that only exists as a spot colour. The maroon we were using before may also have been a spot colour, but, so to speak, we weren't taking advantage of what spot colours offer. Spot colours are inks that aren't made up of the four-colour process inks, and when they're converted to four-colour process, they precisely lose this capacity for luminous colour. This trick works quite well in editorial design, but in corporate identity it's hard to apply to anything beyond very special pieces, such as stationery, because in the end a brand is sometimes reproduced in the media — in printed media — and it's hard to give it special colours there. Think of a colour like Milka chocolate's: Milka has a very distinctive violet, but we only see it on products made specially, like packaging, or things produced with spot colours. When that colour gets reproduced in another medium — a magazine ad, say — it loses that special quality. When we have a single, unique piece, it's worth using spot colours, or "direct" inks, as we call systems like Pantone, among others, and it's worth using them for special colours. So in this case, since we're using two inks, one being black and the other a spot colour, it's worth taking advantage of this special colour. This special colour, if we apply the system we mentioned earlier — a one-colour back, for example — will work very well for us. We end up with a very distinctive card, in a colour that's easy to remember, and that, fundamentally, is the idea of a card: it has to be memorable. This is also, in a way, how we could think about alternatives to this same project.

## Recap: general design

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 11:47–12:41

Let's sum up the most important points in this lesson. As for general design, consider the format: standards are sometimes useful, and they exist for a reason. On this card we used the same measurements as credit cards, which will serve us quite well — it will probably help save paper too, and these standards do serve a function, so it's something to bear in mind. Remember that the sheet's back can be useful: a piece has two sides, so something can happen on the back too — let's factor that in on these pieces, since it's useful space. A good design, as we saw at the end, is a statement — something you can easily put into words. ## Recap: typography

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 13:05–14:35

As for typography, make sure some typographic characters are the correct ones. We talked about this with apostrophes — it happens with apostrophes, sometimes with quotation marks, with dashes, with certain special characters that get used wrongly. This is what we know as correct typographic usage (*ortotipografía*), and it's especially important in a logotype, because it's something we're going to repeat many times, and it's going to be built. There are even logotypes made incorrectly that get built in three dimensions and placed on top of a building — and maybe they have a badly made apostrophe. Avoid using certain typefaces in capitals, especially scripts — think about which typefaces call for capitals and which for lowercase. And instead of text that looks hand-made, it's better to actually make something by hand: instead of using plastic flowers, better to use real ones, or none at all. This matters quite a bit. So, a logotype has to develop in a stylistically coherent way: if we have a hand-lettered logotype, let's not add a typeset element to it — it's a bit contradictory. One thing is writing, or lettering, and another is typography, and it's better to remove this kind of leftover element. And choose typefaces that look good at the size they'll be used. Here we made a change from Helvetica to Gotham, and we really gained legibility. This is just a matter of trying out typefaces that look good at their size — it's hard to explain why some letters look better at small sizes, but you simply have to try them and see what shows up, what it is we're actually doing.

## Recap: composition

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 14:36–15:07

And as for composition, it's important not to scatter elements across the page as if we were afraid of empty space — what's called horror vacui. If we don't know how to place something, it's better to use a strong scheme, such as dividing the page in half. We shouldn't be afraid of leaving white space; instead of scattering elements, we should find a scheme, such as a vertical division. This will give our card a considerably more modern style — and this applies just as well to a poster, a magazine page, or anything else.

## Recap: colour

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 15:08–16:00

And as for colour, in design, colours rarely have fixed meaning, but they almost always connote. In other words, don't worry too much about what colours "mean" — we didn't even discuss this: we worked with a maroon, then changed it to blue, and nothing happened; there's no meaningful value at stake here. And you should make the most of the power spot colours give you: colours create sensations more than they signify meaning. So if you're working on something and get the chance to use spot colours, it's worth making them clean, cheerful, eye-catching — connoting whatever you want them to connote. But don't use spot colours for effects you'd easily get in CMYK, which is what most printing we encounter uses. So it's worth taking advantage of the opportunities spot colours give us.

## What's next: an editorial identity

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L95 · 16:01–16:14

We've now worked through a business card as the basis for an identity, and in the next lesson we'll look at a somewhat more complex case: an editorial identity.

### Key points (derived)

- Strip a piece back to its essential logotype before styling it further; a stock photo that has to fight its background (the "window effect") often isn't worth keeping.
- Check the logotype's own typography: a Saxon genitive needs a capital S, and the character after an apostrophe-shaped keystroke is often a prime or an accent, not a real apostrophe — compare shapes across typefaces to find the correct one.
- A calligraphic or script typeface is usually built to be read in mixed case; setting it in full capitals cramps and shrinks its letterforms.
- Commissioned or self-made hand lettering, tried multiple times, can beat a typeface that only imitates handwriting.
- Don't mix a hand-lettered logotype with a typeset element trying to frame it, and don't mix positive and negative treatments arbitrarily — pick one and commit.
- Let the logotype's own shape suggest the card's orientation, and consider matching a recognised standard size (credit-card dimensions, rounded corners) for a small, functional gain.
- Avoid horror vacui: don't spread text to fill the space out of fear of blank areas; a strong scheme, such as a single vertical or horizontal axis, reads as more contemporary.
- The back of a two-sided piece is real estate: move secondary information there, and it can even carry the same spot colour as the front.
- Swap a legacy typeface (Helvetica) for one built for the medium (Gotham) when legibility matters, and use a family's weights for emphasis instead of capitals.
- Spot colours (Pantone/"tintas planas") stay saturated and bright in a way CMYK builds of the same colour cannot reproduce — worth using on a small run of special, memorable pieces, though not on anything that will later be reproduced in ordinary process printing.
- Colour in design rarely carries fixed meaning but reliably connotes a mood; changing a brand colour (maroon to electric blue) doesn't inherently break anything.
- A good design should be describable in a sentence, and memorable enough to explain over the phone — a single distinctive element (a colour, a letterform) often does more than several ordinary ones.
