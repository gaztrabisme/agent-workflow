## Choosing a palette from the inspiration

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L18 · 00:00–04:18

Now that I understand color better, I can apply it to the Instagram posts. The goal is to build a palette and decide the proportion in which to use each color. We left off choosing typefaces. The lettering is still black and white, so now I’ll give it color. I first return to the inspiration page and its examples of flat color: large areas of color without gradients or small decorative details. Their primary colors—red, blue, green, and white—could suit Patio. The typeface we chose is sophisticated, so livelier colors can complement it.

I add a page called Color at the end of the file, then arrange it there to keep the pages in order. To try colors together, I make rectangles and place them side by side, duplicating them with the Option key. They come in gray by default before I assign colors. I want primary colors that recall play or a childlike world, but not colors so saturated (*saturados*) that they fall into clichés. I have already tried a few. The red I choose is vivid and bright, but not pure red. In the color picker, moving left toward gray makes a color less saturated; moving right increases its saturation. Moving toward the upper-left corner and white makes it lighter and brighter; moving toward black makes it darker and less luminous.

Figma shows RGB values because it is intended for digital work on screens. The chosen orange-red is R 230, G 101, and B 60. A complementary-color scheme (*esquema de colores complementarios*) can create strong contrast and a cheerful effect, so I pair that red-orange with blue. I also choose a green that leans toward blue rather than using a pure, bright green. Its near-complement is an orange-yellow. These four colors work well together, but for contrast and legibility I also need white, especially for text, and a dark color to make the palette feel more sober and sophisticated. Once I have played with and decided on the colors, Figma reminds me which colors I have used recently.

## Testing color with text

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L18 · 04:18–06:01

I copy the shapes and the typography samples—a headline and body text—into the posts so I can test combinations on the actual square canvases. Figma’s canvases are white by default; selecting one lets me choose a background color in Fill. Trying the colors directly on the posts also starts to suggest the rhythm of the Instagram feed.

Dark text on blue does not have enough contrast. It is almost invisible on green, although the shapes might make that combination work as decoration. Dark text on red also looks poor. By contrast, yellow against blue looks good. Yellow text on blue works for the smaller copy too, but at phone size it may look noisy or become hard to read, so I leave that text white for now. I can adjust it again when the posts are final.

## Trying the palette on shapes

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L18 · 06:01–08:38

I test the colors on the shapes as well. One possible post could fill each rectangle with a different color, so I make five colored ovals: blue, green, yellow, red, and dark green. I imagine using these for the studio’s disciplines: branding, digital design services, art direction, strategy, and illustration. I align the words with their ovals, then check them in white. White works with all the colors, which reassures me that this system should be usable.

I do not yet know which colors I will use for each shape, so I place three forms provisionally. Enlarging them has changed the corner radius, so I increase it with the corner-radius control and arrow keys. A green background could make the three shapes read as one form. On red, yellow text looks good; blue is too noisy, so I keep the yellow. On a yellow background, green works for the headline, and I settle on dark blue for another version. I place the text without arranging it for now; we’ll lay it out properly in the composition section. I also try a dark background: the different colors all sit well on it. I use a repeated word as a placeholder and will change it once I know what each post is for. It feels sober and elegant, while the colored lettering keeps it playful.

## Developing color posts and checking the palette

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L18 · 08:38–11:37

I bring back the confetti shape and test it in yellow and red, perhaps over black text. I also try a more ordered arrangement, with a red piece in each corner and yellow and green pieces between them. It reminds me of Parchís, a Spanish board game with pieces in these four colors arranged in four corners. That gives me a useful color sketch. For one last option, I make a subtler rainbow from the palette that could work as a graphic element behind text.

The palette works overall. Dark text has poor legibility and contrast on blue and red backgrounds. Pairing blue or red with yellow, and using white or the dark color with the other hues, gives the posts stronger contrast. The palette has impact and suits the typeface: its seriousness is balanced by a brighter, more cheerful feel.
