## Typography and the Madrid typeface

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L11 · 00:00–02:18

*5642 · U02 · L11 · 00:00–02:18*

Now that you can make images for a concept, you can complete them with typography (*tipografía*). Some designers find it the most tedious part of design, but Silvia says it is extremely important and almost as central as the image. Designers can even use typography itself as an image.

The word originally referred to a printing method that used small lead or wooden types. Today, designers often use “typography” to mean different letter styles or typefaces (*tipos de letra*). Although that use is not strictly exact, it is common to ask which typography someone is using or whether two typographies fit together.

Silvia designed a typeface based on Madrid, where she lives. Municipal posters and signs used different lettering and seemed to speak in different tones, so she thought it would be useful to unify them in a typeface for the city. She studied ceramic street plaques made by sign painters and brought some of their distinctive features into her design. This became Ferpal Sans, designed to be readable at large sizes and to carry details from the ceramic signs.

The city council happened to see Ferpal Sans on her website and used it for San Isidro, a well-known city festival. The campaign paired it with illustrations by Mercedes de Bellard (captions: *Belart*) and became famous mainly thanks to those illustrations. The council then wanted to improve the typeface; after that redesign, Chulapa became Madrid’s official typeface.

## Classify typefaces to find a suitable one

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L11 · 02:18–03:21

*5642 · U02 · L11 · 02:18–03:21*

There are millions of typefaces, so it can be difficult to choose the right one for a design. Computers have some fonts installed already, but you can download others that better fit your needs. Knowing the main classifications helps you discuss type and search for the kind you need online. The broadest division is between typefaces with serifs and those without. The word **sans** in a name such as Comic Sans means that it has no serifs. A serif (*serifa*), also called a terminal or finishing stroke, is a small ornament at the end of a letter’s strokes. Serifs were once said to be more legible in print, but both serif and sans-serif faces can be legible today; other qualities distinguish them.

## Serif and sans-serif styles in context

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L11 · 03:21–04:49

*5642 · U02 · L11 · 03:21–04:49*

Serif type can feel elegant, academic or professional, and is often used in newspapers. Silvia points to *The New Yorker* as an editorial example. Apple used a serif face in a 1980s campaign to distinguish itself from competitors and look more sophisticated and refined. Fashion magazines also use serif faces for an exclusive, polished feel.

Sans-serif type often feels more modern. Early on, it was sometimes called “grotesque,” which is why some sans-serif families have *grotesque* in their names. Its use at leading schools such as the Bauhaus helped people see it as contemporary and as a sign of modernity. Many companies now use sans-serif type in their branding. Volkswagen, for example, uses Futura, a typeface with enough weight and personality to act as a graphic element in its own right.

## Use display and script faces sparingly

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L11 · 04:49–06:11

*5642 · U02 · L11 · 04:49–06:11*

When searching for typefaces, two further classifications often appear: display and script. Display faces suit headlines and headings because they have a strong personality. Silvia advises against using them too much: they can make a design feel crowded, especially when the letters are ornate, raised or dimensional. Script faces imitate hand lettering. They can work well in very specific details, such as a quotation, but it is difficult to find one that is carefully drawn; a script can look too systematic. These typefaces are valid choices, but she recommends using them selectively. For a particular phrase, you can also draw your own letters so they feel more natural.

The lesson resources list websites where you can download free and paid typefaces, and give simple instructions for installing a typeface on your computer.

## Make a word speak through its letterforms

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L11 · 06:11–07:57

*5642 · U02 · L11 · 06:11–07:57*

Choose a short word—around five letters—and use the letterforms to express what the word says. You can do it on paper or on a computer. If you work digitally, pay close attention to your chosen typeface: Silvia recommends something minimal and sober so that the treatment and arrangement of the letters can carry the idea. This resembles the earlier exercise with geometric shapes, but now letters can communicate through both their form and their typographic arrangement.

Silvia chooses **aire** (“air”) and spreads the letters around the page so they feel empty and as though they are floating. For **roto** (“broken”), she breaks apart the R, O, T and O so the word itself looks broken. Finally, she chooses **engordar** (“to gain weight”). Instead of changing where the letters sit, she varies their weights (*pesos*), mixing thinner and wider forms so the word grows heavier as it is read. She asks you to choose words and represent them with letters, then share the result. The next lesson looks at common mistakes people make with typography.

### Key points (derived)

- Typography can function as an image as well as written language.
- Serif, sans-serif, display and script faces have different visual qualities and uses.
- Letterform, placement and weight can make a word’s appearance echo its meaning.
