## Clarity through hierarchy

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L55 · 00:08–01:27

In this lesson we'll see the importance of defining a visual centre of interest. Working from considerations like weight, colour value or size, you'll be able to guide your audience's gaze through your composition. Let's look at what this involves.

Clarifying a composition comes naturally if we reduce the factors that generate ambiguity.

![5642 · U4 · L55 · 00:41](frames/U04L55-F0041.jpg)

On screen: "Simplicidad" (Simplicity) / "— reducir las ambigüedades —" (— reduce ambiguities —)  
Shows: A concept card with the word centred above a short subtitle, on a pale background.

Marking the entry door to follow, and the order of its parts, is a way of simplifying the design. If composing means relating objects in space, determining one figure as dominant helps clarify the connections and the unity of the whole. Communicating hierarchy is key. If we try to make everything look equally important, nothing will be able to do so with clarity and pregnancy (*pregnancia*, strong visual impact).

## Visual weight, size, and contrast build a focal point

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L55 · 01:27–02:33

Although habit conditions us to read left to right and top to bottom, that convention can be altered if we handle visual weight skilfully. Let's clarify this point a little.

![5642 · U4 · L55 · 01:38](frames/U04L55-F0138.jpg)

On screen: large initial "J" [rest illegible]  
Shows: Another spread from the same book: a black double page with a hand reaching up from the lower-left corner and a singer's face shouting into a microphone at lower right.

Visual weight is achieved by an object, or a group of objects, because it differs from the rest in some attribute. That singularity, that extravagance, is more captivating to the eye.

![5642 · U4 · L55 · 01:52](frames/U04L55-F0152.jpg)

Size can help define a visual centre of interest: the main element should be large, or even gigantic, compared with how tiny the secondary one is.

## Position in the frame changes visual weight

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L55 · 02:33–03:52

Position in space is also influential. If we centre it evenly within the format, it's neutralised. If we place it in the space, it's neutralised. If we place it in the upper part, its weight increases and it stands out. And if the centre of interest is placed in the lower part, its weight increases even more. From an entry point at the top, the visual path will lead downward through the piece. This effect reverses when we alternate the position of the visual weight — the reading tends to move upward.

### Key points (derived)

- A composition reads clearly when one figure is made dominant and the rest are subordinated to it; trying to make everything equally important makes nothing read clearly.
- Visual weight comes from difference: an object that stands out in size, contrast, colour or position captures the eye's fixations before anything else.
- Size, contrast against the background, warm colour, and position all add weight — and position can reverse the usual left-to-right, top-to-bottom reading: a focal point placed low or alternated in position pulls the eye's path with it.
- Grouping elements into well-separated clusters creates clear entry points and rhythm; how close or far other elements sit from the focal point sets their place in the reading order.
- The designer's own process: on the "new periphery" spread, enlarging the photo cluster and adding a drop cap sharpened the entry point; on the Barcelona portfolio opening, the centre of interest went through several rounds — title first, then colour, then reworking the map and list's size and position — before the image, title, map and text settled into balance.
