## Move from figurative images toward abstraction

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L08 · 00:00–02:12

*5642 · U02 · L08 · 00:00–02:12*

Literalness and subjectivity concern an image’s concept; this lesson turns to style, which runs from the figurative (*figurativo*) to the abstract (*abstracto*). Style has no end point, and there are many ways to represent the same thing. In Bruno Munari’s *Design as Art*, for example, faces and human figures appear in many different styles. Photography is close to reality in a two-dimensional image. A portrait or hyperrealist painting is also close; moving farther away, illustration may omit details or use colors unlike those in real life. A geometric face still retains cues such as the nose, rosy cheeks and hair. A simple smiley is more symbolic, and its meaning depends on what the viewer has learned. Someone seeing one for the first time might not recognize a human face; likewise, a dove can mean peace only if that association is known. A colon and parenthesis can suggest a smile with fewer elements still. At the most abstract end, geometric shapes may have no necessary connection to a face.

At every point on the scale, images are built from elements such as points, lines and geometric shapes. Many shapes together can make a literal representation; used more expressively, they can become abstract without representing a specific object.

## Geometry and the Bauhaus

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L08 · 02:12–03:30

*5642 · U02 · L08 · 02:12–03:30*

For this course, Silvia focuses on geometry (*geometría*). Although geometric forms look basic, learning to use them well gives a solid foundation for building images without unnecessary ornament. She introduces the Bauhaus, a famous German school where art, architecture and design were brought together. The school used geometric forms and sought to reduce decorative details partly to optimize resources: fewer flourishes made objects easier and cheaper to produce. A logo by Kandinsky uses geometry to represent the school. With geometric shapes and primary colors, Bauhaus designers made prints, textiles, furniture and toys—objects that have lasted to the present day.

## Geometric forms carry character

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L08 · 03:30–04:21

*5642 · U02 · L08 · 03:30–04:21*

Basic shapes already suggest qualities. A triangle and square resting on a horizontal line feel stable. A circle may seem kind because it is rounded, while the triangle’s point can feel sharper. Many books from Chile use geometry because these are among the first forms we learn as children, and combine them to make other forms. Geometry can also be used abstractly: circles on a cornflake box stand for the cereal, while shapes can act like instruments or numbers when they are layered over one another. Perspective and superimposition are other ways to use them.

## Represent concepts with three basic shapes

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L08 · 04:21–05:56

*5642 · U02 · L08 · 04:21–05:56*

The exercise is to represent concepts using only a circle, a square and a triangle. Work in whatever way feels comfortable: cut the shapes out, draw them or use a computer. Keep two restrictions: do not use color and do not stretch the forms. You may repeat them, make them larger or smaller, rotate them, move them around the page or try other arrangements.

For “multitude,” Silvia repeats many basic shapes and scatters them in a slightly disorderly arrangement to suggest chaos and lots happening at once. For “balanced,” she stacks all three forms: the triangle rests on its base, the square is slightly tilted, and the circle sits on top while the whole tower stays balanced. For “empty,” she makes the shapes small and spreads them far apart, leaving more space around them. For “ordered,” she multiplies the shapes and aligns each type together in a structured arrangement.

## Continue the exercise and move to perception

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L08 · 05:56–06:20

*5642 · U02 · L08 · 05:56–06:20*

Silvia has left a list of words in the lesson tasks for practising this abstract way of representing concepts. Geometric figures can help represent many different ideas. The next lesson turns to the laws of perception and to how people make sense of images.

### Key points (derived)

- Style can move from realistic representation to abstract signs and forms.
- Geometry is a basic visual vocabulary; arrangements and combinations affect what shapes suggest.
- To practise, represent a concept using only circles, squares and triangles, without color or stretching.
