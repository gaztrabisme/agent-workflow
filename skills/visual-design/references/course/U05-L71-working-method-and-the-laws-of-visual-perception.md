## Step one: let the material lose its identity

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L71 · 00:38–02:12

The first requirement of working with this kind of material is that the material loses its identity — that it loses its name and loses its referent, that it becomes pure form. This is not the lid of a tin can; it is a semicircle with a certain fold. This is not a brick; it is a rectangle in a pinkish tone. This is not a mussel shell; it is a form that recalls a teardrop. This is not a cable tie (*brida*); it is a form with a brutal force that tends to spring open. This is not a wire, a bent piece of iron; it is a line with enormous intensity. This is not a straight line; it is a line that ends in three angles that reinforce the force the line carries at its end.

And so on: the intention is that we lose the referent of what the object is, that we forget what it is. It is nothing — it is pure form, it is a stain, it is a line, it is what it is, but it is not what it was before. He recalls a comment made earlier about the cover of the *Grafía Callada* (Silent Spelling) book: that was not a leaf, it was a vertical mark — the opposite of what René Magritte used to do. He considers this loss of the object's identity fundamental to this visual language.

## Step two: analyze the form and let it speak

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L71 · 02:12–04:10

The second, equally important step is to analyse the form and see what it suggests — to give the forms time, because this is a long process: forms reveal themselves and communicate with us as time passes, and one day we see one thing in them and another day something else. It takes a certain warming-up, a certain maturing.

For example, in one form we can see an enormous outward tension: a point where the force this shape exerts is balanced by the two pieces at its sides, so that it looks on the point of bursting — a very powerful internal energy. This has to be allowed to flow, to tell us how to use it and how to get the most out of it.

Another form is essentially a straight line that has been bent, and keeps being bent, even twisting as it folds; this must be used, because it creates a powerful energy toward the centre of the curve, something to bear in mind when composing.

Another form leads the eye — it is agile, like the bed of a river: a linear form, but a very suggestive one. And another is a stain that is, in practice, almost an oval. These are the forms and resources that must be reinforced through use.

## Step three: potentiate the form through perception

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L71 · 04:10–04:50

How are these qualities reinforced? Through the principles of perception — this is the third step. The energy and peculiarities of these objects are potentiated by using them in favour of the material's own intention and expression, applying one, two, three, or however many principles apply. The principles are not mutually exclusive; in general they accumulate and occur together, appearing jointly. Even so, he believes it is best to work through them one at a time.

## Four principles ahead, revisited

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L71 · 04:50–05:35

He will continue talking about unity: how to distribute these objects within a space. Second, how to control the weight and presence of each object within that space, giving one a greater prominence than the rest. The third part will be how to give the objects expression — how to analyse and reinforce the expression a form has, which comes down to the imbalance that produces and provokes that expression. Finally, another source of imbalance and expressiveness in forms is movement, the last resource to be used.

## Closing: collecting inspiration from other artists

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L71 · 05:35–06:06

Having shown and explained the three points his working method is built on — simple points, he believes, but very effective ones for him — he will now show how he collects and keeps these principles of perception, how some artists see them, and what he has learned from them and from the things he keeps gathering to use.

### Key points (derived)

- The method has three steps: (1) strip the material of its name and former identity so it reads as pure form; (2) analyse each form patiently, over time, for the tension or energy it already contains; (3) reinforce that energy by applying the principles of perception, which combine rather than exclude one another.
- The "this is not X, it is Y" reframing (tin lid → semicircle, brick → pink rectangle, mussel shell → teardrop, cable tie → springing form, bent wire → intense line, straight line → three-angled line) is the concrete technique for step one.
- Step two treats form-reading as slow and cumulative — a piece may reveal a different quality on a different day — rather than a single snap judgement.
- The four principles previewed for the rest of the unit, in order: unity (distributing elements in space), categorising (assigning weight and presence), balance/imbalance (expression through tension), and movement (translating internal tension into visual movement).
