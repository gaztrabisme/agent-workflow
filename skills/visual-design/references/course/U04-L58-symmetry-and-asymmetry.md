## Levelling, sharpening and the axes we impose

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L58 · 00:00–01:45

In this lesson we'll look at the differences between a symmetrical composition and an asymmetrical one. Movement and rest are fundamental to both concepts. Let's see what this is about.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 00:08](frames/U04L58-F0008.jpg)

On screen: "JAVIER ALCARAZ" / "Simetría y asimetría" (Symmetry and asymmetry)  
Shows: Title card for the lesson, over the same softly blurred book photograph used earlier in the unit.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 00:27](frames/U04L58-F0027.jpg)

On screen: "Simetría y asimetría" / "Entre la acción y la reflexión" (Symmetry and asymmetry — Between action and reflection)  
Shows: Concept slide with small red, black and blue squares scattered off-centre around the title.

To whatever we look at, we impose imaginary axes to adjust its balance, and a structural map to understand it. Every shape or structure is a diagram of forces: in a design, remember, we perceive tensions between its components and the format. Balance is the compensation of these perceptual forces. If you've made it this far in the course, you can surely already tell the two apart.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 00:34](frames/U04L58-F0034.jpg)

On screen: "TATE'S BAKE SHOP" · "ORIENT → OCCIDENT" · "ZENIT" / "ЗЕНИТ" [rest omitted, small print on the magazine cover]  
Shows: A green t-shirt with a small abstract badge, a Yugoslav avant-garde magazine cover ("Zenit"), and a green bakery branding card grouped side by side as one set of examples.

The first is an effect of fixedness, of a static balance. When we talk about tension, on the other hand, the perceptual forces come into play. When any object confirms its geometric centre and its horizontal and vertical relationship, we say the composition is levelled.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 01:13](frames/U04L58-F0113.jpg)

On screen: "CORBEAUX BAKEHOUSE" [rest omitted, address and website]  
Shows: A bakery's branding across paper bags, a gift box and wrapped loaves, each item carrying the same crest of two facing birds and ornamental type.

Using that same reference of structural axes, we can shift the objects and provoke a sharpening. Here too a compositional clarity appears, because it's obvious that levelling isn't what's being sought.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 01:26](frames/U04L58-F0126.jpg)

On screen: "STOLE N / ELF OAF" (large, hand-cut lettering) [rest omitted, dense secondary type]  
Shows: A poster stacking several unrelated words and phrases in overlapping type at different sizes, angles and colours, none of them lined up with the others.

This movement of tensions, then, sharpens the composition — hones it — without moving away from the structural axes, but through them. Levelling and sharpening are two ways of making a piece's purpose evident.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 01:38](frames/U04L58-F0138.jpg)

On screen: "Biennale d'Art Contemporain" · "Nuit de la Photo" · "70" / "1" (oversized numerals) [rest omitted, dates and venue]  
Shows: A grey exhibition poster where two huge numerals overlap the event's name, off-centre, against a faint grid of registration marks.

What opposes them both is ambiguity.

## Symmetry: stillness, stability, predictability

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L58 · 01:45–02:58

When a composition rotates or reflects around a central axis and its structure doesn't vary, we say it's symmetrical. Its simplicity grows because there are fewer variants to the vertical and horizontal axes, which are fundamental. The stability of symmetry impresses: it invites stillness, and so contemplation — but it also reduces the spatial variables available to highlight and emphasise elements.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 01:45](frames/U04L58-F0145.jpg)

On screen: "een kleine keuze uit onze lettercollectie" (a small selection from our letter collection) [rest omitted, jumbled type samples]  
Shows: Two posters side by side — an ornamental green monogram of the letters O, T and R mirrored around a shared stem, and a colourful, deliberately scrambled type specimen.

It's an attribute present in publication design, because the spine works as an axis of symmetry. Any codex, any publication, is bound to that central axis, and so the movement through any codex runs left to right — well, in the East, right to left — but that flow is part of a publication.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 01:59](frames/U04L58-F0159.jpg)

On screen: [none] (a monogram, no running text)  
Shows: A black ligature monogram interlacing the letters T, C and Q into one symmetrical ampersand-like mark.

As it regularizes its shapes it harmonizes them and produces a controlled rhythm, but also a predictable structure that tends toward an obvious axis of the format.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 02:11](frames/U04L58-F0211.jpg)

On screen: "THE MAKIOKA SISTERS" · "JUNICHIRO TANIZAKI" · "A masterpiece of great beauty and quality." — Chicago Tribune  
Shows: A book cover split into four mirrored quadrants, each holding part of a woman's portrait in muted vintage colour photography.

Balance and contrast are still necessary to achieve centres of visual interest. The variety and material quality of the objects has to increase, because their details, sitting there still, are set off more by symmetry. So be careful with the quality of the things you place so fixedly.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 02:34](frames/U04L58-F0234.jpg)

On screen: "S..." (large drop cap opening the article) [rest omitted, dense body text]  
Shows: An open magazine spread, its drop-capped column facing a photograph of brightly coloured balls of yarn beside a woman in traditional dress.

## Asymmetry: content over form

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L58 · 02:58–03:45

On the other hand, when we rotate an asymmetrical composition from its geometric centre, we no longer recognise the original, or its proportion. Asymmetry — the teacher says *simetría* (symmetry) here, but means asymmetry — is based on the prominence of the content, the activation of negative space, and the dynamism of the objects. The relationships between the different components ask us to see the whole and compare its fragments.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 03:04](frames/U04L58-F0304.jpg)

On screen: "nicholas fox weber" · "the bauhaus group" · "six masters of modernism"  
Shows: A book cover built from six flat colour panels (yellow, red, black-and-white photograph, cream, blue), each holding one line of the title off to a side.

In a way, context imposes itself over particularities: it depends much more on its format, on its space.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 03:20](frames/U04L58-F0320.jpg)

On screen: "人権" · "共生" · "Human Rights" · "Living Together" [rest omitted, small credit line]  
Shows: A pink poster with a stylized ink-line face crowned by a radiating burst pattern and two bird-like creatures in dense linework, Japanese and English text running down the left edge.

Its challenge, then, is to offer a certain predictability, to lessen the constant surprise and keep it from getting lost in inconsistency.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 03:35](frames/U04L58-F0335.jpg)

On screen: "kunstgewerbemuseum zürich" · "USA baut" (USA builds) · "9. september – 7. oktober 1945"

## Composition as a semantic tool

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L58 · 03:45–04:37

Although we don't always keep it in mind, composition is also a semantic tool of design. Meaning doesn't live only in the data or symbols that appear in a graphic piece, but also in the forces of the scheme — the way the message is organised. An asymmetrical structure, because of its historical tradition, can refer to conservative ideals; it's not for nothing that almost every avant-garde movement in art declared itself against it.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 03:45](frames/U04L58-F0345.jpg)

On screen: "Giselle" (oversized lower-case) · "Basler Freilichtspiele 1959 · 19.–31. August im Rosenfeldpark" [rest omitted]  
Shows: A ballet poster where the title's giant lower-case letters overlap a blurred photograph of a dancer's raised leg.

It can also invite reflection: it arranges things so naturally that we can do nothing but contemplate them.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 04:04](frames/U04L58-F0404.jpg)

On screen: "H. L. MENCKEN" · "Prejudices: A Selection" · "PAUL RAND" (small credit) · "hub-tones" / "freddie hubbard" [rest omitted, imprint and price details]  
Shows: Two covers side by side — a torn, hand-lettered portrait collage for a book of essays, and a jazz LP sleeve built from a row of solid vertical bars broken by one shorter, off-set bar.

In the same way, it tends to evoke a sophisticated style — the expression of fashion and elegance exploits asymmetry, from typographic use to the layout of its messages.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 04:15](frames/U04L58-F0415.jpg)

On screen: "ROSES et ROSIERS" · "PAR DES HORTICULTEURS ET DES AMATEURS DE JARDINAGE" · "PARIS" [rest omitted, publisher's imprint line]  
Shows: A botanical book's title page, its centred classical type balanced by a single line-drawn rose to one side.

I'm on the runway, look at me, it says.

## Dynamic composition and its trade-offs

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L58 · 04:37–05:18

A dynamic composition, by contrast, is far more restless, and tends to slip easily out of the designer's hands. It can represent energy, because it activates its forces and moves them across the whole space.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 04:37](frames/U04L58-F0437.jpg)

On screen: "VOGUE" · "SUMMER PLANS:" · "The Black and White Idea" · "The Linen Life" · "Transparent Fashions" · "April 1, 1950" [rest omitted, edition and price details]  
Shows: A Vogue cover with a woman's face obscured by a veiled hat, the masthead balanced off to one side against the empty half of the frame.

Sometimes this links it to innovation — that need to never stay still.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 04:45](frames/U04L58-F0445.jpg)

On screen: "Geschichte als Argument" · "41. Deutscher Historikertag München" · "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität" · "17. bis 20. September 1996"  
Shows: A poster whose title dissolves into a scatter of straight lines crossing the type at every angle; no portrait is visible, only lettering broken up by the line pattern.

But it can also account for a more informal message, with nothing clear to anchor or align it.

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 04:52](frames/U04L58-F0452.jpg)

On screen: "Mozart" · "Kunst im Quartett" · "Günther Kieser" · "Ottfried Zielke" [rest omitted, dates and venue repeated in jittered type]  
Shows: An event poster whose every line of information is set twice, each repetition offset and overlapping the other, so no line sits still.

Whatever strategy you choose for your design, always think about the advantages and disadvantages of each system. But above all, don't hesitate to try to make them coexist, because being opposites, they always need each other.

## Closing: neither imbalance nor boredom

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L58 · 05:18–05:38

![5642 · U4 · L58 · 05:23](frames/U04L58-F0523.jpg)

On screen: "I WAS A DANCER" · "THE TATTOO ARTIST" · "THE RIVER WIFE" · "THE MAKIOKA SISTERS" [rest omitted, further titles too small to read]  
Shows: A closing grid of twelve book covers, mixing centred, symmetrical designs with off-axis photographic ones, drawn from the whole run of examples just shown.

As you've seen, asymmetry doesn't imply imbalance, and the symmetrical doesn't have to be boring. In the next lesson we'll talk about the rule of thirds.

### Key points (derived)

- We read balance through imaginary axes and a structural map: levelling keeps an object on its geometric centre and its horizontal/vertical relationship; sharpening shifts it while still working through those same axes, not away from them. Ambiguity is what happens when neither is clearly chosen.
- Symmetry (rotation or reflection around a fixed central axis) reads as simple, stable and contemplative, but narrows the room available for emphasis — which is why material quality and detail matter more in a symmetrical design, and why the book spine, as a symmetry axis, gives publications a predictable, left-to-right (or right-to-left) structure.
- Asymmetry favours the prominence of content, negative space and dynamic relationships between parts over a fixed proportion; its risk is inconsistency, so it still needs some predictability to avoid losing the viewer.
- Composition carries meaning on its own, apart from any symbol or data in the piece: asymmetry's avant-garde history can read as reflective or sophisticated (fashion and editorial design lean on it), while a more dynamic, unanchored composition can read as energetic, innovative or informal — and is harder for the designer to keep under control.
- The two systems are opposites that need each other: choose deliberately, weigh what each gives up, and don't be afraid to let symmetry and asymmetry coexist in the same design.
