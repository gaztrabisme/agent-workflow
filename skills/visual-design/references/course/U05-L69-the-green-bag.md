## Fragmenting paintings before the Green Bag

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 00:00–01:33

At the end of 2013, after two or three years of working with waste material, Pepe Gimeno felt a need to work more openly. He set that material aside, picked up a brush and black acrylic paint, and began a series of exercises done partly out of necessity, to detoxify himself. These pieces became part of an exhibition of 59 fragments, shown alongside the Green Bag project he covers later in the lesson. He shows some of them now, promising to come back to a few with more comment later on.

A question occurred to him: why, in film and in literature, can a piece or an argument be cut up and reordered to produce a different expression while telling the same story? He wanted to test whether that worked in painting, in a single piece. So he started cutting: in some pieces, the fragments come from different works joined together; in others, it is a single piece that has been cut and reordered, saying exactly the same thing in a different way. One such piece is painted on wood boards.

## Discovering the green bag

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 01:33–02:44

While working on these fragmented paintings, he was searching for material on his studio shelf when he found a green bag full of wires. He tipped them out onto the table, and the sight suggested words in a dictionary (*diccionario*) — waiting for someone to join them, complete them, and give them meaning. They also reminded him of the work of a poet: a poet does not invent words, but chooses existing words, joins them, and gives them an expression. That became his purpose with these wire pieces.

The pieces already existed as they were — some very small, some large, some very complex, some already cut — and he had no intention of altering their form; he planned to leave each one as he found it. What he decided to do instead was to work each one individually and turn it into a sculpture. That is how the project was born: the Green Bag.

## Naming the project: isolating and analyzing each piece

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 02:44–03:08

His method — which he expands on later, as the working method for the whole course — was to isolate each wire piece, analyse it and look at it closely. He photographed each one next to his mobile phone to keep a record of relative sizes, then began moving each piece around until it told him something, until it communicated with him.

## The crouching figure and its tension

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 03:08–03:52

One piece was so small it seemed insignificant, so it needed to be paired with something far more forceful. In it he began to see a crouched human figure with an arm stretched back, holding a brutal tension. He wanted to increase and intensify that tension as much as possible. So he set the wire on a pedestal of white cement and rested a bar of methacrylate against it, letting the bar carry all the piece's colour and force — the black — leaning it to the right and throwing the composition off balance. That imbalance greatly increased the effort the figure appears to make to hold the bar up.

## Minimal intervention and the weight of a cloud

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 03:52–04:45

He completed the rest of the pieces gradually. Some, like the next one, needed almost nothing done to them — he simply found the right orientation, with no manipulation at all: just looking for the sense already in the form and letting it express itself, because the shape itself was interesting.

Others seemed to ask to be completed because they were a cut, incomplete-looking form. For one of these he joined two pieces together and used the resulting loop like a kite, or a cloud. He called this piece *Sujetando mi nube* (Holding my cloud). He likes the idea of weight in it: the blue element — the piece that reads as the biggest "stain" in the whole work — sits at the top, held up, as if the thread were the only thing keeping the cloud from rising and floating away.

## Uniting two pieces: dialogue and Ya eres mía

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 04:45–05:47

In another case, he joined two separate pieces to create a dialogue between them — a technique he says he used often here and afterwards. The next piece is a similar case: two pieces that, joined, achieve real unity because both rise in the same direction, yet the two are very different from each other. One is very symmetrical, with a decisive, powerful, direct movement — even aggressive, as if attacking. The other is far more hesitant, more tremulous, more sensitive, more timid. A small bow at the end of the second piece, which was part of the original found wire, makes it read as feminine, and the pairing always reminds him of the masculine and feminine worlds. He titled the piece *Ya eres mía* (You're already mine), which he feels describes it well — like the male stalking the female.

## A visual helix in black, blue, and seaweed texture

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 05:47–06:37

The next piece cost him a great deal of work: it had a genuinely complicated form, and he could not settle on a focal point or generate visual interest from it. He finally succeeded by building a kind of visual helix that he manages to launch from a point made of tufts like seaweed hair (*pelos de alga*) at the centre of the image; the line runs down, the black rises, rests on the blue, and carries through to the end. Visually, there is a path through the piece that he finds interesting. The wood element that holds the whole thing up always reminds him of part of a tree, like a small bonsai, with a mass that reads a little like a galaxy.

## A tall base under tension: red plastic and the wood branch

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 06:37–07:18

He likes the expression and the contrast in the next piece — again a dialogue, this time between the base, which is tall and important, and the rest of the composition. The base is a very clean, very tall piece, with something on one side that reads as if it had exploded internally: a shape that erupts from the most unexpected place with tremendous force, growing straight out of the base. It is held in tension by the weight of a piece of red plastic (*plástico rojo*) at its tip, which pulls the form even tighter, together with the contained, gathered character of the wood branch (*rama de madera*).

## Hay vida después del caos

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 07:18–08:34

This next piece amuses him — he says he'll talk about it again later, in the visual path (*recorrido visual*) — because he feels he achieved the opposite of what actually happened: the wire originally had a rising, oddly wandering path with no real sense to it. By simply crossing it with a series of suspended points, the line now looks — visually — as if it deliberately threads through those points, even though it was really made the other way round. He finds it pleasurable to watch how the line crosses the points seemingly at random, yet lands precisely on each suspended point in turn. Given the piece's expression, he calls it *Hay vida después del caos* (There is life after chaos): a piece he sees as defeated, but a defeat carried with enormous energy. The wire even dips below the baseline the wires grow from, then rises again — and that resurgence, that twisting, pulling all the visual weight of the figure to the left, gives it a very interesting power.

## Beyond the bag: trophies, seaweed rhythm, and stone

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 09:01–10:14

After this sculptural experience he kept going: he became interested in plastic and began working with other three-dimensional materials, in pieces unrelated to the Green Bag project itself but which still worked in three dimensions and continued, in their own way, to draw on recycled material. Some of these pieces are two metres tall — powerful pieces where what really matters is the rhythm of how the elements are arranged; that rhythm is the essence of the piece.

Another piece here is also a rhythm exercise, made with seaweed (*algas*) that kind, generous people brought him back from a trip to the North Sea — even flying with it — knowing he would want it. Then there is a chaotic piece with a brutal sense of movement from the contrast of shapes and colours: a savage dialogue, like a conversation between 500 voices all shouting at once. Against that, a much quieter form — the dialogue between one shape that is simple and straight and another that is elegant. And finally, explosions of stone fragments, where he is working with, one might say, suspension and stumbling.

## Closing: from waste material to a working method

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L69 · 10:14–11:32

With this, he has finished showing all the projects for this part of the course — it is the last one he presents here. These images will be an important basis for explaining the exercises and lessons they will work on later, and he wanted to show them because he believes understanding where they come from, and why they were made, helps in understanding their essence much better. In the next unit he moves on to the working method itself: how to approach this kind of material, what has to be done, the shift in ways of looking that is required, and how to apply the principles of perception needed to draw the maximum expression out of material that is lying on the floor. After that, he will also show some of his sources of inspiration — his notebooks of collected images — and, above all, how certain artists have used these principles of perception in a very wise and intelligent way.

### Key points (derived)

- The Green Bag project began as a side effect of a painting series ("59 fragments") and a chance discovery of a bag of wires on a studio shelf.
- Working method: isolate each found piece, photograph it for scale, move it until it "says something," then add the minimum needed to bring out a form already latent in the material.
- Recurring techniques across the pieces: reorienting a single piece with no alteration; joining two pieces into a dialogue; using an added weight (methacrylate, plastic, painted "cloud") to create tension or imbalance; using found suspension points to make a line look intentional.
- The series runs to roughly 26 finished sculptures; for the teacher, the process matters more than the finished object.
- Later three-dimensional work (the "Trofeos" totems, seaweed pieces, the "500 voices" mobile, the stone-fragment piece) keeps working with recycled material but shifts focus to rhythm — how elements are arranged — rather than single-object tension.
