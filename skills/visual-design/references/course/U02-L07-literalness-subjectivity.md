# Lesson 07 · Literalness/subjectivity

## What graphic design does

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L07 · 00:00–02:04

*5642 · U02 · L07 · 00:00–02:04*

The course approaches graphic design (*diseño gráfico*) from a visual perspective, first through its elements—form, typography and color—and later by considering them together through composition. Graphic design is more than knowing how to use Photoshop, Illustrator or InDesign. It is about giving form to an idea, whatever program you use. Designers help communicate concepts, but also illustrate, diagram and explain things through images. Images are among the most universal resources we have: how they communicate depends on what is shown, how the elements are arranged, which typeface is used and which colors are chosen. We also use images to draw on what we already know from real experience, and to imagine things we have never seen.

Visual experience helps us understand our surroundings. Cave paintings, some more than 70,000 years old, are among our earliest visual records and help us understand what the world was like then. The more we train our eyes to read images, the easier it becomes to make new ones. Silvia mentions her other Domestika course on training this visual ability. One challenge in making images is how an image and text can work together: the combination can create a new narrative, so that one plus one becomes three rather than simply repeating the same message.

## Image and text can diverge or converge

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L07 · 02:04–03:57

*5642 · U02 · L07 · 02:04–03:57*

Silvia uses Fredun Shapur’s book *Circle, Circle, Square* to show how image and text can relate. Its characters are two circles and a square. In the opening pages, the geometric forms and words sit alongside one another in an abstract, subjective way; the text does not describe exactly what the shapes are doing. As the pages turn, the two strands gradually come closer together and the words begin to refer to the images. The shapes also develop from abstract forms into recognizable pictures. By the final pages, they have made much more literal images, while the text continues on its own and still does not work: neither the images without the text nor the text without the images. The two strands depend on one another.

## Choose a position to suit the brief

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L07 · 03:57–05:07

*5642 · U02 · L07 · 03:57–05:07*

Some subjects cannot be represented too literally: a village festival, or especially a book cover, where a literal image could spoil what happens inside the book. There is no inherently good or bad position on the spectrum from literalness (*literalidad*) to subjectivity (*subjetividad*); the brief determines where a design should sit. For a city festival poster, familiar cues might include fireworks, flowers, music and concerts, along with festive type and cheerful colors. Using all of them at once can make the result overly literal and redundant. A more subjective route might select one cue and explore it, or look for less obvious details: how the city smells during the festival, which flowers grow in the grass, how people relate to one another, or what they wear. Silvia’s own projects often lean toward the subjective end, while still making the message understandable and allowing her personal point of view to enter.

## Compare literal and subjective design examples

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L07 · 05:07–06:53

*5642 · U02 · L07 · 05:07–06:53*

Silvia compares designs that sit at different points on the spectrum. For an illustration event, Isidro Ferrer makes a pencil the main element and shapes it into an animal, instead of showing someone drawing or pages covered with sketches. This is a more subjective way to speak about illustration. New York City subway signage by Massimo Vignelli takes the opposite approach: it needs to be immediately understood, so it uses letters and colors simply to show which way to go for Brooklyn. A Madrid summer festival poster is also subjective. Its vivid colors feel cheerful, but it does not show heat directly; instead, someone peers out from behind a window, visibly hot but ready to go out to the festivities. By contrast, an energy bar uses its ingredients as the main graphic, stating very clearly what it contains. The packaging still uses that literal information creatively as its central visual idea.

## Read an existing image from literal to subjective

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L07 · 06:53–09:36

*5642 · U02 · L07 · 06:53–09:36*

To practise moving between literal and subjective readings, take a cutout from a magazine or catalog. Start by writing what is literally present: what you see and which objects make up the image. This is a useful first step when approaching a design. After making that list, look again from a more personal, subjective point of view and search for things you did not notice at first.

Silvia works through a photograph of a woman walking a dog. Her literal list includes a walk, a woman, a dog, summer, sun, joy, fashion and a boat, because she thinks they might be on a boat. Once that is clear, she looks for less obvious associations. The leash joins the woman and dog, but could also resemble a cable, which brings to mind a telephone or a cable car. Their connection without speaking, and the way they look at one another in a still image, suggest continuity or telepathy. She also thinks of Bluetooth, a lifeguard and balance: there is a shadow, and the dog walks along the line dividing light from shade.

The two lists give her more knowledge of the image. For a literal assignment, the first list inventories its parts. The second can generate ideas for a more personal and less obvious point of view. This kind of exercise can unblock creativity because it produces ideas without requiring you to design yet. Images that are not literally the same as the thing you must represent can still help you see it differently. Make both lists from an image of your own. If the subjective associations take longer, do not get frustrated: during ideation, generate many ideas without making it difficult; you can choose the useful ones later. The next lesson moves from concept to style, including geometry.

### Key points (derived)

- Images and text can reinforce, extend or deliberately diverge from one another.
- Choose how literal or subjective a design should be according to its brief.
- Separate what an image literally contains from the personal associations it can suggest.
