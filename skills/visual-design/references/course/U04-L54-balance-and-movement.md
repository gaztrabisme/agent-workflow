## Force, tension, and rest in shape

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L54 · 00:07–01:16

In everything we see, we look for balance, but that isn't the same as static. In this lesson I'm going to explain how to move from rest to tension without losing the balance in your composition. Let's look at what this involves.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 00:28](frames/U04L54-F0028.jpg)

On screen: "Equilibrio y movimiento" (Balance and movement) / "Un concierto de fuerzas" (A concert of forces)  
Shows: The lesson's title card, with a blue square top-right and small black and red squares lower on the page.

Every shape has its own form. It has its own structural axes. A simple straight line is balanced by the forces at each end pulling toward different sides, and so they cancel each other out. When these opposing forces compensate for and cancel each other out, balance occurs.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 00:44](frames/U04L54-F0044.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A single thin horizontal white line, centred on a black background.

The shape of an object is a diagram of forces. When all the forces in a shape compensate for each other equally, rest occurs. It's clear that there can be balance in rest, but it's also possible to produce it under tension.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 00:58](frames/U04L54-F0058.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A white square, circle and triangle of similar size, evenly spaced on black.

Angles generate forces because they bring lines together to meet at a point. The sharper the angle, the more force it generates. The same principle applies to curves. The tighter the curve, the more tension and force it transmits.

## Tension, direction, and depth

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L54 · 01:16–02:02

Rest, then, is a sensation of stillness, but it isn't synonymous with balance. The tension of opposing forces that attract each other can be balanced.

To produce visual movement, as Kandinsky used to say, besides tension you have to include direction. You have to stimulate the figures and give them space to orient the movement. The oblique position is the easiest way to get a directed tension. It distinguishes action from rest and enhances depth, as in perspective. Tension increases when one of the figure's ends grows. When tension is integrated between the shapes, the composition becomes dynamic.

## Weight, colour, and guided movement in space

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L54 · 02:02–03:12

There's also dynamism in spaces. As a book's margins narrow, the tension increases. Favoured by context, colour accents attract each other.

The same thing happens between complementary colours, as in this poster by Rosmarie Tissi. Where does the S go — toward the pink, or toward the red? Intensity makes the more muted object look dependent on the brighter one, and it moves toward it.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 02:27](frames/U04L54-F0227.jpg)

On screen: "OPUS: A Haven at Elevation" [rest omitted]; "OFFSET" (large stacked letters) [rest omitted]; "Anton Schöb / Buchdruck – Offsetdruck" (Anton Schöb, letterpress printing – offset printing) [rest omitted]

Movements, in any case, have to fit within the dynamic of the visual field. The heaviest object puts tension on the space and pulls it toward itself. Size or position can create enough weight to pull other elements in their direction.

A graphic device can be used to make the eye travel across the piece. But the tension between objects also inspires a path, as does the repetition that produces perceptual movement. Remember: what is dynamic is alive — it invites action and seduces with paths and destinations.

## Sketches for a portfolio spread

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L54 · 03:12–04:11

In the sketches I made for the portfolio piece Barcelona, Irregular Edges (*Barcelona Bordes Irregulares*), I started off badly. I wanted to impose a rather whimsical structure built on a diagonal — a diagonal tied to the map — and the map shouldn't have been so much the protagonist. It was generating counter-shapes that were a bit complicated and gave too much prominence, let's say, to the designer, and not to the content — that is, to the photographer. I decided to make much smaller sketches, to concentrate not on the details but precisely on the structure.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 03:48](frames/U04L54-F0348.jpg)

On screen: "A2 — Barcelona"  
Shows: A sheet of small pencil thumbnail spreads exploring different placements for photographs and text blocks.

I then understood that what mattered was to give the portraits a hierarchy, as I'd already grasped when reviewing iconography, and that the photos wouldn't share the space with much text. The forces and tensions each image carries would have to coexist in the space, so they wouldn't clash too much with the dynamic and compositional logic of the whole publication we're putting together.

## Testing a diagonal composition

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L54 · 04:11–05:01

In this first attempt, I tried to create a formal relationship between a large image and a small one, joined by colour, and also by the diagonal and the force of those circles. The text here has little to do with it.

In this other proposal, besides avoiding that ambiguity of the white space that existed at the edges, I also relocated the text to let me create a movement that's not so oblique, but more of a spiral instead. This solution doesn't work: first, because the portrait is too small and I can't see the cages, which is the key element or motif for understanding a social relationship or a particular trait of the person portrayed. And second, because the counter-shapes are too exaggerated, and the diagonal quality of that obliqueness becomes whimsical and too elementary.

## A composition that reads left to right

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L54 · 05:01–06:10

This solution is much better, because first it unifies the horizon line in each photograph and creates a reading from left to right. The pause of white space and the text placed a little further down slow the speed down.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 05:02](frames/U04L54-F0502.jpg)

On screen: "Puda nectur re nos dolo dolum quae porpore pedistius enimill" [rest omitted] (repeated dummy copy); "REVISTA YVY" (small caption printed over the road, repeated on later spreads).  
Shows: A spread with a large street-corner photograph reaching the page's outer edge and a smaller inset photo of a man standing beside bird cages.

This photo has a very strong diagonal built into it, and it also has a spot of colour placed in the lower part of the whole plane of the composition. Since I don't want the text to be the protagonist, I can't place it at the top, right where the force of that diagonal ends. Instead, I placed it near the horizon and in relation to the area with the spot of colour.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 05:19](frames/U04L54-F0519.jpg)

On screen: "Puda nectur re nos dolo dolum quae porpore pedistius enimill" [rest omitted] (repeated dummy copy).  
Shows: The revised spread with the portrait enlarged to full page — the wall's diagonal and the coloured bird cages at its base now clearly visible — and the street-corner photo reduced to a small inset on the facing page.

Here, again, we have a left-to-right reading, which is the usual one, but we emphasise it with that sequence of colour, with that repetition to generate movement, which then concentrates or comes to a stop when it reaches the person portrayed.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 05:42](frames/U04L54-F0542.jpg)

On screen: "Puda nectur re nos dolo dolum quae porpore pe" [rest omitted] (repeated dummy copy, cut short).  
Shows: A full-bleed photograph of a woman breastfeeding a child on a street, with a vertical caption on the facing blank page.

The diagonals within that image anchor them in the space, and in that way I think they create a very coherent rhythm. The pause in the middle is essential for coming back to look for the colour and that texture the clothing has.

## Reversed direction and the final dispersed layout

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L54 · 06:10–08:10

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 06:10](frames/U04L54-F0610.jpg)

On screen: "Puda nectur re nos dolo dolum quae porpore pedistius enimill" [rest omitted] (repeated dummy copy).  
Shows: The earlier placement the teacher says doesn't work: the market photo of children's red and pink clothes on the left page, the portrait of the woman in a red striped coat on the right — before the two are swapped.

This one, on the other hand, doesn't work, because the image is too coherent. It works too, actually, because the strong point is on the left side, so the sequence first comes to a stop and then wants to continue toward the right. In other words, the portrait reverses the direction. That forced me to move the text to that other position and pull it a bit further away.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 06:27](frames/U04L54-F0627.jpg)

On screen: "Puda nectur re nos dolo dolum quae porpore pedistius enimill" [rest omitted] (repeated dummy copy).  
Shows: A spread pairing a portrait of a woman in a red striped coat with a market photo of children's red and pink clothes hanging on a rail.

In this compositional intention, dispersion is what takes over. Here the images are related more by their forces than by any grid that might exist — there isn't one yet. There's a central point created by colour, and also somewhat by the size, of the image of the mother with her child. That green, and the diagonal quality the green has, starts to connect the images that sit at its vertices.

The lower portrait, the one carrying a basket of fruit, is small, but you can still make out the fruit — or vegetables, whatever they are — and it contrasts very well with the clothing. Placing that quite abstract house, or that small, strange little mound of earth, next to the text, in a way gives it hierarchy and takes away many of the problems caused by its reduced size.

The upper image, the one centred right on the spine, is a group portrait. But there's a motif in it that speaks to these people always going out for a walk. The motif, the object that makes us think these people go out for a walk, is the dog.

![5642 · U4 · L54 · 07:53](frames/U04L54-F0753.jpg)

On screen: page numbers "28", "29"; "Puda nectur re nos dolo dolum quae porpore pe" [rest omitted] (repeated dummy copy, run across several lines); "REVISTA YVY" [rest omitted].  
Shows: A spread scattering five photographs — two men with a dog, a woman with a fruit basket, a mother and child seated outdoors, a rubble heap, and a washing machine dumped outdoors — around a caption block, with no visible grid.

I ran a test placing some dummy folios to see whether that rigid element that always exists in a publication would constrain this type of composition. Just the opposite: I think that having a very rigid, very solid base makes the movement of all the elements in the space more evident.

As you saw, there are many possibilities between rest and tension (spoken: *atención*, attention).

### Key points (derived)

- Balance is not the same as stillness: rest and tension are two different states, and both can be balanced.
- Force comes from shape itself — sharper angles and tighter curves generate more visual force; a growing end or an oblique position (Kandinsky's tension + direction) generates directed movement and depth.
- Proximity and overlap change how movement reads: a small nearby shape absorbs the movement of a larger one, and overlapping shapes read as moving into depth.
- Weight (from size, position, or colour intensity) pulls the rest of a composition toward it; repetition and graphic devices can also guide the eye along a path.
- The designer's own process: an early diagonal/spiral treatment was rejected for hiding a key motif (the bird cages) and for exaggerating counter-shapes; the working solution unified each photo's horizon for a left-to-right reading, used a colour accent to pace it, and finally let the images' own colour and size relationships — rather than a grid — disperse them across the spread.
- A rigid base (like a dummy folio) can make surrounding movement more visible, not less.
