## Why the story matters

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L83 · 00:24–00:45

In any branding or visual-identity project it is essential to be clear about what story the graphics will tell. Strong designs or good typographic and colour decisions are useless if there is nothing unsettling or interesting behind them.

## Three pillars: reason, emotion, voice

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L83 · 00:45–01:47

The lesson works with three pillars that have run through the whole course, all rooted in geometry. The blue square holds the rational part: the product, the target, the market, the product or service's main features, and other questions that usually come from a brief. The red circle holds the differential values: the emotional links the designer wants to build with the consumer, and other data tied to emotional rather than rational links. The yellow triangle holds the voice — the way of speaking that makes the product or service unique, and that will show up in the visual identity across every communication.

## From message to story

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L83 · 01:47–02:42

A familiar communication diagram has a sender and a receiver, with a message in between. The teacher's own working principle is that this message should carry different nuances — it can be said in different ways, and it matters who sends it and who receives it. So in every visual-identity project it is worth turning that message into a story: what story are we going to tell? Doing this makes the project distinctive and gives much richer material to work with at every stage.

## Selecting the differential data

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L83 · 02:42–04:29

Moving to the actual project, and into the round of definitions that follows the brief: earlier, data was sorted into columns — points of interest and focus that the project raised. Now comes a deeper, more exhaustive selection, filtered through the designer's own point of view and what he wants to say about the project, again organised under the square (rational), circle (emotional) and triangle (points of attention).

On the rational side, the data kept includes: the 1970s in the United Kingdom, the song's length, and phrases tied to the song's name such as the demon or being reborn. On the emotional side: something intense, something repetitive, a pattern that keeps recurring, and a sense of mystical revelation. On the attention side, the points that appeal most: the idea of a journey, a safari, having crossed the Sahara while composing the song, the idea that the song became bigger than its own authors, and a concept embedded in the song itself — time and space. That phrase from the song is, for the teacher, a very strong trigger for the concept and the story he wants to tell through the project.

## The concept phrase and what's next

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L83 · 04:29–05:20

That phrase from the song works as the trigger for the whole concept. Between sender and receiver, between the product and its target, there will be one differential phrase that defines the concept. The message that could simply have said "this is a drink with certain characteristics" becomes a story instead: this drink is a traveller in time and space. With a story to tell, the next step is to build a visual universe that finds the best way to tell it.

### Key points (derived)

- A visual-identity project needs a story behind it, not just strong design or typographic/colour choices.
- The course's three recurring pillars: square = reason (product, target, market), circle = emotion (differential values, emotional links), triangle = voice/attention.
- Turning the standard sender–message–receiver model into sender–story–receiver adds nuance and richness to a project.
- Song data for this project (Led Zeppelin's "Kashmir") was sorted into rational, emotional and attention categories, then filtered down to a smaller, more personal selection.
- The selected concept phrase — "I am a traveller in time and space" — becomes the seed for the project's visual universe, developed in the next lesson.
