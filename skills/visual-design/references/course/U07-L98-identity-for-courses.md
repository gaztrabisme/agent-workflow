## The brief: stock photography, type and colour problems

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L98 · 00:00–00:44

The subject of this lesson is the communication pieces for a business school. We'll see how these are currently solved with stock photography, and the problems that kind of material creates for us — but we'll also look at some problems we have with the typography and the colours. From the start we'll see three alternatives to stock photography, using three different kinds of illustration: the first version is more figurative, the second more conceptual, and the third more abstract, and for all three we'll weigh up the pros and cons of each visual language. Each of the three proposals has a constructive structure that will help us develop the courses' subjects.

## A substitute typeface: Arial standing in for Helvetica

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L98 · 02:14–03:34

This is set in a typeface that's probably familiar to you: Arial. Arial is what we call a "sucedáneo" — a substitute — meaning it's a typeface really made to stand in for another, more famous one: Helvetica (captions: *Bética*). It was bundled into the Windows operating system and imitates that other typeface, but it has certain shortcomings. For example, one shortcoming is that it has fewer weights, so it's not as practical for what we're going to do — in one of our applications we'll use Helvetica instead of Arial. It also has some details in the letter shapes themselves that simply make it worse. That's why we call it a substitute: it's a letter that was chosen so as not to pay royalties for another, quite popular typeface. It's something professional designers should avoid, not just as a question of rights, but also because, as we can see in these images, the one on the left simply isn't as handsome as the one on the right — and I think we should develop some sensitivity to typography, enough to notice this for ourselves.

## Stock photography's first problem: it reads as fake

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L98 · 03:34–05:02

The main thing we're going to focus on, apart from resolving composition and typography questions, is how each of these courses gets explained — the strategy this business school has taken to illustrate each course's topic through images. These are what we call stock images: images bought from image banks, found through keyword or concept searches, and lately, through artificial intelligence, we can find quite useful concepts too. Stock images create a kind of code for the consumer or reader, one that tells them we already know this isn't real. So even if we're talking about family finances, say, we already know that this pair we have in the photo in the middle of the top row doesn't actually exist as a couple; they're not family, and they don't have a business together — they're extras. The same goes for this group in the top left: they're extras or models hired for this kind of thing. Maybe the most realistic one is the one in the bottom left, this worker in this facility. But apart from that, these are made-to-order photographs, and they raise two problems for us. One is an aesthetic question — this lack of credibility; we know it isn't real. And there's also, a little, the "click": we find this kind of photography everywhere, in any kind of product or communication.

## Stock photography's second problem: inconsistent style

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L98 · 05:02–05:49

Beyond that, these photos, taken together, simply don't work. The photos themselves are fine — good quality, good resolution, good colour, well balanced, photographically accomplished, made by professionals. But we can see they're done in absolutely different styles: different optics, some treated afterwards with graphic text overlays, like the ones in the lower or upper right, and the colour is slightly different too — some more natural, some more forced, different lighting, and so on. This is what we're going to address from here: what alternative do we have to this kind of image? These photos are useful because they express each course's ideas well, but we're going to look for alternatives to this visual language.

## Pictograms, a tighter palette, and a text element designers propose

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L98 · 08:18–10:27

In this case what we've done is work with a kind of illustration based more on pictograms, on linear drawing. The colour palette is more limited, more restricted. In the previous collection we had a base yellow that we used to hold the cut-out figures; here we have pictograms that in some cases use a bit of transparency, but basically we have four colours with some small variation: blue, green, red and black. We fit this into a scheme similar to the previous one, but in this case we've added a new element. Here we're working with Helvetica, not Arial — this being the "authentic" typeface that Arial, in a sense, replaces — and above all it has a slightly simpler scheme, dividing the vertical space into a series of bands. But this element up here is the key one: it's an element we, as designers, can introduce ourselves. It's a kind of small summary of what the course is about. Even though as designers we sometimes feel we have to wait for the client to hand us something like this, proposing what we're going to do is often part of the process. If we think a brochure cover should include a text of this kind, of this length, what we have to do is propose that such a text be written. This is what gives the piece the right visual language. And this matters: in this case, it's up to us as designers to take the initiative that it should be this way, and afterwards a professional will write the text to measure.

## Typography, and a gradient to negotiate with the client

## Recap: avoid substitute fonts, watch letter case

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L98 · 13:05–13:49

Let's go over some of the things we've said, and one or two we haven't, across this lesson. We talked about substitute typefaces, and for that we pointed to Arial. Avoid these typefaces for several reasons: they're not as good, there are rights questions involved, and it isn't right that large companies invent typefaces just to avoid paying royalties. Keep in mind that composing in lower case, in mixed case, and all in capitals can give a very different final effect — sometimes the answer isn't just finding the right typeface, but how you set it. This matters: the effect can be quite different.

## Recap: geometry serves the system, and propose the text yourself

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L98 · 13:49–14:57

We talked about each proposal's geometric composition. Finding an ordered, geometric compositional scheme can be useful, but more geometry doesn't necessarily mean more order — building these geometric systems can help put a system together, but you shouldn't become a slave to them. It creates a certain order, but you shouldn't do something that then works against your own creativity. So use these schemes or grids if you need to, but sometimes it doesn't make much sense to build a grid before you've seen how the system actually works. We also saw an element designers often don't propose themselves: how much text, what kind of text, what kind of paragraph, what kind of element should go on a page. We almost always wait to be handed all this content ready-made. It's good practice for designers to propose certain content ourselves, to give a certain visual language to what we're making.

## Recap: keep a collection's images consistent

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L98 · 14:57–16:23

As for the images: in a collection, avoid mixing different photographic and illustrative languages. If our series contains images, they need to have a certain relationship to each other — we can't choose photos just because they're photos and cover the topic; they need a real relationship if we want the collection to read as coherent. We also have to bear in mind that if we work in the territory of conceptual or more abstract illustration, we'll never be able to explain everything through images — this is something I hadn't mentioned, and it's a danger. Making conceptual illustration means finding clever ideas, things that explain the topic well, but you can't always find an image for everything, so it's better to leave ourselves some margin, since illustrations sometimes won't cover everything we want to say. And finally, if we're working with these images to build a collection, we should work at the same level of abstraction throughout — precisely to avoid what happens with stock photography, when we work with illustration we should keep one consistent language or level, so it gives the whole collection a certain consistency.

### Key points (derived)

- The lesson's throughline is that a stock-photo formula fails on two independent axes at once — credibility (the reader recognises the photos as staged) and consistency (the individually excellent photos don't share a visual language) — and each of the three alternatives (conceptual, pictogram, abstract) solves both by replacing search with construction.
- The "text you propose yourself" point (08:31–10:27) generalises beyond this lesson's brochures: it reframes a designer's job to include specifying content shape (how much text, what kind), not only styling content the client hands over.
- The three alternatives are explicitly pitched as a negotiable gradient, not a single right answer — the deliverable to a client is a choice of abstraction levels, not one finished design.
