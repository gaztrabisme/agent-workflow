## The shop sign: too big for its own good

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L100 · 02:40–03:20

Here's another fairly typical application: how we make a shop's sign. Without thinking about it, we almost always tend to occupy the top of the shop, or the top of the façade, or whatever part of the façade we're allowed to use, without stopping to think that this might not be the part passers-by, pedestrians, actually see most. This might not be the best spot at all — and on top of that, it also does something else: it makes the mark as big as it can within that space, which means we end up with two identical marks and one smaller one, because the mark simply didn't fit there. And that's a small problem too.

## Cutting the logo down: dropping the legal form and the description

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L100 · 05:05–07:31

The problem we clearly have here is a matter of graphic style, which I understand is debatable, but above all we simply have too many elements in this mark. Too many things shouldn't happen in a logotype, or we forget everything. If there are too many voices, we forget what they're telling us — and clearly the first thing we should eliminate is this legal denomination. This is something clients often resist; they insist their company has this legal denomination. But we have to remember that a logotype isn't necessarily what signs an invoice or a delivery note or whatever — a logotype is really what marks that company's image. To help the client understand that we need to drop this kind of element, it helps to point out that we don't picture Nike or Netflix or any big brand with this kind of denomination at the end — even though they probably have one. If you then check an invoice from these companies, you'll see the legal denomination doesn't even resemble "Netflix" or "Nike" — they work differently. This is what small companies do: it turns a company into something small, and often the business owner themselves doesn't realise that, legal as it is, this isn't where that kind of denomination should go. The description at the bottom is also a bit limiting — in this case it narrows the scope of a mark that could offer rather bigger services. It isn't necessary; the image already contains a nut, so you don't need to be this explicit about it, and especially not in a way that almost swallows the main mark, since this bar is almost more visible than the mark itself. And this auxiliary element is a cliché — it doesn't give us anything interesting or memorable, and it's limiting too. So the first thing we can do is leave the bare mark, which already works well on its own — no problem there. Let's consider colour later; for now we'll work on a black shape to work through the form.

## Keeping the nut: weight, case, typeface, and a rotation

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L100 · 07:31–08:35

Now let's consider this element that's interesting: it's a letter, and at the same time an icon of this company's activity. So it's interesting — it's what gives the mark its personality, or its joke; it's the key element we could keep. Let's see what happens if we give it a bit more weight, so it doesn't look so weak, so that both the nut and the letter gain more weight. Let's see what happens if we set it in lower case, which will work better, and let's reconsider this typeface. We'd been working with Gill Sans, both in the logotype and across the rest of the image — the labels, the catalogue, and so on — but let's change this Gill Sans for a different letter. It's purely a matter of style; let's work with Neo Sans and see what happens. After that, we could rotate this nut. Why rotate it? Simply because it gives it a more dynamic position — before it rested on one of its sides, now it does a better job of reading as an O. It's a bit more of an O now, while still being 100% a nut. Then we could try what happens if we remove the hole: the hexagon shape is already explicit enough on its own, without needing to draw it in more detail — it's already a shape we associate with tools, with mechanical things, and it works quite well for us. And looking at it closely, one thing we could do is match these rounded corners so it integrates a little better into this shape.

## Letting the sign breathe at eye level

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L100 · 09:33–10:33

Let's see now how this mark would work on the shop window. We're going to stop occupying the top, and place the mark more clearly within the pedestrian's field of vision. That is, something that could equally be seen from a distance now reads better — it has more contrast, fewer distracting details. We don't have these secondary elements any more, and it's much clearer, much more visible, and we're not making the mistake of occupying the top of the window almost out of tradition, or without thinking about it — a space that probably isn't as visible as we assume. In some cases it might actually be more visible, if there are objects in front of the shop, parked cars, or something else blocking this lower space. But in principle, this way of placing things makes our mark more visible, and we're not acting purely out of habit, but thinking a little about what the eye is actually going to find.

## A system that can grow: a display face for extensions

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L100 · 10:33–11:49

This mark, which for now has no auxiliary element, can be accompanied by other descriptions the way we had before — but we're going to do it so that it can grow, so it can branch out, so it can explain the different services and products the company has. For that we'll use the same typeface, but in a way that clearly reads as an extension, one that can grow indefinitely, so to speak — if there's a new service, it can be added in the future. It isn't baked into the mark as part of a boxed-in system that stops us growing. The most logical thing is to use the same letter we used for the logotype, Neo Sans, but in a light version. This is a display typeface with fairly formal characteristics — it isn't a letter meant for reading, but rather one for titles or logotypes.

## A second typeface for small sizes, and an optional stripe

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L100 · 11:49–12:46

And we're going to add one more element — we'll use it only when we need it. It isn't that tool we had before; it's more like a second typeface, one that isn't the display face we're using here. Notice that when we use the same display letter, it doesn't work as well at small sizes. So what we can do is work with an auxiliary typeface, such as Myriad, the typeface built into the Macintosh operating system — if you have a Mac, it works quite well, and it's much clearer. Notice the difference between this and this: this is a more legible, more ordinary letter, without the details a display typeface would have. And then there's another element we'll add if we need it — and this is the key to what we're explaining: we have to work with elements we bring in only when we need them, not necessarily force them in there regardless. This is a colour stripe set at a 30-degree angle, the same angle as the hexagon, which, for example, could work at the bottom of the catalogue. It gives us a bit of colour, gives us identity, and reinforces this tougher, more practical, more reliable image we now have.

## Recap: fewer voices, one axis, no legal form, no filler graphics

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L100 · 14:40–16:00

Let's go over some of the most important things we've seen in this lesson. One concerns identity, or how a mark is built: in a logotype, or in an imagotype-plus-logotype set, no more than one or two things should be "happening". The more anecdotes we add, the less we remember what's actually going on in that mark, so it pays to clean this kind of thing up. A mark also needs a clear axis — no more than one. That is, if it's centred, it has to be centred; if it isn't centred, it shouldn't be centred — this has to be unambiguous. Another good habit is to remove legal denominations when they aren't necessary: "SA", "SL", "GmbH" make your company look small. So this is the kind of element you should include on an invoice or wherever it's actually needed, but it's better left off the logotype — it makes our mark small. And auxiliary graphic elements shouldn't be there just to fill space; it's better to use them only when we think they genuinely help. Using them shouldn't be obligatory, and it's preferable to build a system that can grow indefinitely instead, adding and dropping descriptions as it suits us in the future — you could almost think of it as an investment.

## Recap: let the sign breathe, at eye level, and keep a separate type for text

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L100 · 16:00–17:13

When it comes to exterior signage, you have to let the marks breathe — a mark that's left cramped works better occupying a space without feeling like it's been forced in. And you also shouldn't place marks, let's say, out of habit, wherever we expect to find them or wherever a sign-maker would default to putting them — instead, faced with a façade, you have to consider what the right spot for that mark actually is. And it's probably often not the top space, but something closer to the centre, at eye level. And finally, one last point on typography, something we did ourselves: we chose a display typeface, but a more ordinary, more reading-oriented typeface works better for us elsewhere — one that doesn't have the characteristics of a display face, and that can perfectly accompany the logotype's own typeface. In other words, the logotype's typeface doesn't have to be the typeface we use to write the rest of our texts.

### Key points (derived)

- The redesign keeps exactly one element from the original mark — the hexagon standing in for the O of the umlaut, with the two dots kept above it — and treats everything else (the legal form, the description bar, the oversized signage, the space-filling wrench vinyl) as removable; the lesson's throughline is that a "wink" like the nut earns its place by being specific to this name, while GmbH/Eisenwaren/the decorative wrench are generic and interchangeable.
- The two typeface layers (Neo Sans for the logotype and any titling, Myriad for everything read at length) are introduced for a legibility reason, not a style reason — Neo Sans Light visibly fails at small sizes in the 12:10 comparison — and the lesson generalises this into a standing rule: the logotype's typeface is not obligated to also be the text typeface.
- "Let the mark breathe" and "place it at eye level" are argued from the same evidence: the original signage problem was never really about beauty, but about where the sign-maker's default (top of the façade) actually sits relative to where a pedestrian's eyes are.
