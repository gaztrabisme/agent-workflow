## Turning the paragraph into bullets, and fixing the column width

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L94 · 01:09–03:41

Notice that here we have a lead sentence in bold and a secondary text that lists a series of cases. We have a problem here: there's too much text for a single screen. This is also one of the most common errors in what we might call unprofessional presentations. It's very hard for whoever is listening to this presentation to retain all this information — it's practically impossible. So we need to find a way to deliver this information in parts that the listener can take in naturally, in a way they'll remember afterward. Now, if we clearly try to put all the information we had onto the next screen, we're actually still making the problem worse. So what we need to do is put the bold only where it matters, on the part we need to fix — but we can also work very well with bullet points. Using bullets to separate each of these parts will improve our work considerably. Even so, we still have a lot of information — in fact, we've even made the problem worse in one way, because we've brought over more information from the next screen. This text column averages 87 characters per line. That's very long; this column is too wide. A column of 87 characters is hard to read, because by the time we reach the end of the line, we sometimes don't remember which line we're on. So we need to change this, and, so to speak, grow the small type. Remember that until now, the images we've shown were barely more than titles; now we have titles plus small body text. So, following the same system we showed earlier of adding information gradually across each screen, what we can do is grow the type. Now we'll have fewer characters — the larger the type size, the fewer characters per column — and it becomes more readable. We can also list out each of the answers using bullets that can appear one by one across different screens, without needing to change the opening text. Now we'll have information that's much easier to remember, and where we can put much more emphasis on the part that matters to us — the part we've set in bold. In other words, we have to remember that bold serves to emphasise, so we need to apply it to what matters, not to more general text.

## The diagram page: logo placement and reading order

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L94 · 03:42–04:56

Another of the pages we had in our presentation was a more graphic page, where we made a very simple diagram of the concept behind this water-treatment company. It's a more graphic page that, translated into our new graphic language, would look more or less like what we see here. Here we can even improve a few other things. One is not using a logo in the middle of text — something advertising does a lot. We already have a logo at the top of every image, repeating throughout the whole presentation, so this is something we should avoid; we'll set it as normal text instead — there's no need to make it stand out. ## Vibrating colours, and a calmer palette

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L94 · 04:57–06:09

Now that we have these images, let's look at each one. This recycling-symbol image is fine in itself — it's a pictogram, it's well known — but we're clearly using two colours that vibrate. We mentioned this same idea of vibrating colours earlier, and now we come back to it and explain it in more detail. What are vibrating colours? They're colours that share the same level of darkness, so in a way they fight each other — we can't tell which is figure and which is ground. They're colours that vibrate. It happened to us with the logo too: we had a green logo over a blue band, and that vibrates as well. So we'd do well to work with colours that don't vibrate, and that also contrast enough — that would be the other problem we could run into. So, if we work with a single hue, we can do here what we did with the logo: we have that blue that reminded us of water, which we used on the top band, and we can make a colour derived from that same one — the same hue, just a little darker. And now we have, let's say, a more elegant colour.

## The recommendations, recapped: composition

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L94 · 11:38–12:41

As for the page's composition, don't design wide text columns — you may well find you have too many characters per line, so narrower columns and larger type sizes will solve this problem for us. Don't scatter the empty spaces: on pages where you have a lot of white space, it's better for that space to stay grouped in zones of the page, preferably toward the right and the bottom of the composition. If we work with this more modern kind of design — the kind we've been doing since the 20th century, working systematically flush left (*a caja izquierda*) — there are of course other options, but if we use this one, the white space has to sit on the right and at the bottom, and then follow the reading direction to narrate visually from left to right. Think about how we read: we read left to right. Before and after, now and the future, have to be ordered, let's say, this way.

## The recommendations, recapped: typography

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L94 · 12:42–13:59

As for typography, avoid what we might call thematic typefaces — fanciful typefaces, let's say. Think of these as more like costumes: things you'll wear one day but won't want to wear again another day. You'll put them on for a party, but never again after that. Work preferably with a single typeface family. If you work with a family that has a light version and a heavy version, and then a roman and an italic, that's enough to cover a whole range of needs, such as a screen presentation of this kind. Then, don't emphasise words by setting them in capitals — respect correct spelling (*ortotipografía*). We've seen that agua doesn't need to be capitalised. Hoy, even though it's a short word, has to be lowercase, and details like this will give your work a more professional, more credible look. You can also use bold: if you're working with a system that has a light weight and a bold weight, bold is exactly what can help you here. And then, don't break words in titles — if you set titles flush left, hyphenating words doesn't make much sense.

## Style versus system, and what's next

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L94 · 14:54–15:05

And lastly, this: keep in mind that style is what will set the overall communication. Working on details like these is what makes the message not only coherent, but also easier to remember. Now that we've looked at a PowerPoint-type presentation, let's look at another element that's simple but difficult: business cards.

### Key points (derived)

- A screen that overflows into a second one is a sign to restructure, not to shrink the type: convert a wall of text into a bold lead line plus bulleted, scannable points.
- Watch column width: a measure around 87 characters is too wide to read comfortably; narrower columns with larger type both shorten the line and improve legibility.
- Bold is for emphasis on what matters, not for repeating a keyword (like "agua") every time it appears.
- Keep logos out of running text — set the brand name as plain type instead of dropping in the logo mark.
- Explain a process in reading order: left to right for the Latin alphabet, matching how narratives naturally read (before/after, now/future).
- "Vibrating" colours share the same tonal darkness, so figure and ground fight each other; pick colours that are clearly light/dark and that harmonise, ideally derived from the same hue as your other brand colours.
- Never use a stock photo carrying a watermark without buying it — licenses for this kind of image are inexpensive.
- Don't mix a photographic image with a pictogram on the same page; pick one graphic language and match secondary elements (like an arrow) to it, e.g. rounded corners to match a rounded typeface.
- Split an overloaded closing page: contact details on one screen, a plain thank-you on the last, and treat the first and last pages as a matching pair.
- The underlying system (axes, margins, growth direction, hierarchy) is reusable across many different typefaces, colours and moods — the choice of style is a separate decision from solving the structural problems.
- The recommendations recap: question the default format; one screen, one concept; a single consistent axis; start with the most complex page; no wide columns; group empty space to the right and bottom; read left to right; avoid thematic fonts and stick to one type family; no capitals for emphasis; use bold instead; don't hyphenate flush-left titles; no logos in text; avoid vibrating or low-contrast colours; and keep decorative images meaningful and stylistically consistent.
