## Hue, saturation and value

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L50 · 00:00–01:03

There are many theories of color, but in this lesson the teacher wants to explain how to use color for composition: how to relate different shapes in space through color, and how to generate unity, or systematize your compositions.

First, hue (*tono*), saturation and value. Hue — also called tint or chroma (*matiz o croma*) — is color itself: the attribute that lets us tell one color from another. Saturation is the intensity or purity of a color, whether a hue comes out more vivid or more subdued. At one extreme is a pure color, and at the other a flat, neutral gray. Value, or luminosity, is a color's light intensity.

## Primary, secondary and tertiary colors

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L50 · 01:03–02:01

There are primary colors: colors that cannot be produced by mixing other colors — which has nothing to do with color space. Mixing primaries produces the secondary colors, which are strongly related to them. A secondary color sits opposite, on the color wheel, the primary color that is not part of its makeup. This is key for working the color wheel and building different palettes and harmonic relationships. Tertiary colors are a mix of a secondary and a primary.

## Warm and cool colors and their spatial effects

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L50 · 02:01–02:44

Colors convey a sense of temperature. As a general rule, warm colors run from red-violet to yellow: the more red a color has, the warmer it is, and these colors tend to be perceived as energetic, full of life and action. Cool colors are the range from yellow-green to violet by way of blue, and blue predominates in their makeup — if a color reads as cool, it is because blue is the dominant note in that hue.

Warm colors tend to advance in graphic space: they move closer to the viewer and bring objects to the foreground. Look closely and they seem to expand shapes, stretching their outlines, so figures in warm colors read as a little larger. Cool palettes, by contrast, push shapes back to a plane further from the viewer; in the same way, they contract figures and perceptually reduce their outlines. These spatial properties, combined with other attributes such as size, can be enough on their own to generate a sense of depth in a composition.

## The 60-30-10 proportion rule

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L50 · 02:44–03:33

To create emphasis, the hue itself matters as much as how it is used. The frequency with which colors repeat needs a marked difference between them. As a starting point, for example, you could use a 60-30-10 relationship, where the main color appears 60% of the time, the following shade 30%, and the remaining 10% is reserved only for the color that truly serves as an accent — as in a button on a web page.

There is another way to apply this across the whole palette of an image: 60% for the background or the context, 30% for the content, and just 10% for the accent — like the heart on the cover of The New Yorker.

## Color harmony formulas in Adobe Color

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L50 · 03:33–05:15

The color wheel is the basis for applying different harmony formulas, and one tool that works from the wheel is Adobe Color. You set a base color and apply different formulas and rules to it. In the middle of the wheel sits the base color, and everything else results from that color's position on the wheel; the left-hand column lists the different harmony rules.

A monochrome (*monocromía*) rule takes only one hue, varying only its degrees of value and saturation; the relationship works through similarity, by holding one component constant across the whole palette. The analogous (*análoga*) formula uses colors that sit next to each other on the wheel, immediately adjacent. Complementary colors sit opposite each other on the wheel — recall what was said about secondaries and primaries: the complementary of a primary is the mix of the other two primaries. Split-complementary separation (*separación complementaria*) is a formula that combines one color with the colors adjacent to, or analogous to, its complementary on the wheel; it is like drawing the skeleton of a capital Y. Triadic (*triádica*) harmony is achieved with three colors equidistant both from the center of the wheel and from each other — be careful, because this combination tends to be very strident, so it works best to prioritize one hue and use the other two as accents.

## Color problems: vibration and simplicity

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L50 · 05:15–06:10

Color has several problems of its own. One is vibration (*vibración*), which happens when two complementary colors cancel each other out. This can give a psychedelic effect, as in the work of Víctor Moscoso, but it can be neutralized by varying the saturation or the value of one of the two clashing hues. Using too much color, in general, can hurt the ability to distinguish the elements of a design — which is why the principle of simplicity should also apply to color. Not everything needs color: leaving a detail an honest black, white, or maybe gray, can help the colors you do choose stand out and read more strongly. In this way, color has a compositional use — a meaningful use within the forces that shapes exert in space.

## Checking contrast, accessibility and hierarchy

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L50 · 06:10–07:32

To know how much contrast a palette can offer between its colors, the best approach is to convert it to grayscale; the result shows how much a given hue needs to be pushed up or down. Contrast between hues matters a great deal for the accessibility of a design: any condition that reduces a viewer's visual capacity is helped whenever good contrast between colors can be guaranteed, above all between whichever colors take the role of figure and of background. This contrast can come from different properties of the colors themselves, and not necessarily from hue: there can be contrast between a saturated color and a desaturated one, a light one and a dark one, warm and cool, and so on. It's important to understand the range these properties give you, because it is easy to get lost among their endless variations.

A monochrome palette produces a less dynamic effect in a composition and neutralizes the tension between the parts of a piece. Complementary colors differentiate categories and separate elements, but they don't on their own establish an order or a hierarchy — for that, another attribute has to do the work, as in the example shown here, which shrinks the red and moves it to a different position in space. This is what you need to do to make an element stand out, or read as different from the rest.

## Color identity is relational: reading the project instead of imposing a palette

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L50 · 07:32–08:49

It's important to understand that the identity of a color doesn't live in the color itself: it is established by relation. Color is evasive — it lets itself be shaped by context, and the same hue can shift and transform a composition depending on how its surroundings change.

![5642 · U4 · L50 · 07:50](frames/U04L50-F0750.jpg)

On screen: "CLEAN-ON" (large warped lettering); vertical text "SPRING MODERNIZATION"; small credit "© 1967 Neon Rose" and signature "mmno" [rest omitted].  
Shows: A densely patterned psychedelic poster: red flowers on green scrollwork frame a central panel of green lettering on red, bordered by diagonal magenta-and-blue striped corners — an example of how the same colors read differently depending on what surrounds them.

One strategy for working with color in your compositions is to avoid imposing a fixed palette, guide or harmony from the outset, and instead read what the project's own material has to say. For instance, the magazine being developed for this course's project already has some content in place, so the approach is to analyze the palette, or the general coloring, of its photographs, to understand which tones will stay constant — and those can be used as a starting point. Analogous colors, fairly muted with low saturation, can be a good way to lighten the load and bring harmony with the visual material that will run across the pages.

## Building a palette in Adobe Color: a practical exercise

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L50 · 08:49–11:35

The next step is a hands-on exercise in Adobe Color to generate a color palette. The interface has a valuable tool: loading an image so the interface can extract the palette it contains, available under Extract Theme, or under Extract Gradient if you want to pull the values out of a gradient instead. Here the plan is to extract a theme: the street-portrait photo from the mood board is dragged in.

Once the image is loaded, the algorithm tries to generate a palette that follows certain harmony rules and criteria. Using the "all colors" mode pulls out most of the colors in the palette, but the result comes out very strident — which might work if used sparingly, just as an accent, but is probably too much on its own. A slightly lighter option, or maybe some muted colors, looks better. The palette can be adjusted by moving around within the photo and shifting the hue slightly; two colors in particular stand out as interesting, since they give the palette some life and stop the whole thing from becoming too monochrome. This looks like a good starting point to begin working from. Once the palette is extracted, switching to the color wheel view shows how the chosen colors sit in space, with room for a few small adjustments — though in this case the result already looks fairly good: practically a triad with a couple of adjacent analogous colors thrown in.

### Key points (derived)

- Hue, saturation and value are the three basic attributes of color; primary colors combine into secondaries, and secondaries combine with primaries into tertiaries, all positioned around the color wheel.
- Warm colors (red-violet to yellow) advance and expand shapes; cool colors (yellow-green to violet through blue) recede and contract them — useful for suggesting depth.
- A simple proportion rule like 60-30-10 keeps a palette's emphasis clear, whether applied to three flat colors or to a whole image's extracted palette.
- Adobe Color's harmony rules — monochromatic, analogous, complementary, split-complementary, triadic — are named after where the chosen points fall on the wheel relative to a base color.
- Complementary pairs can vibrate when placed at full saturation; toning down one hue's saturation or value removes the clash. Restraint (not coloring everything) is itself a design tool.
- Converting a palette to grayscale is a quick way to check whether it has enough contrast, which matters directly for accessibility, especially between figure and background.
- Color alone rarely creates hierarchy; size and position often have to do that work instead.
- A color's identity depends on context and relation, not on the color in isolation — so a sound working method is to derive a palette from a project's existing material rather than impose one from the start.
