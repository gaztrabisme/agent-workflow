## What a scale is

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L61 · 00:30–01:16

We already know that, beyond measurements, sizes and formats, something that always interests us as designers is proportional relationships. So we need to understand what a scale is, so we have no doubts about how to work with them. It's a proportioned sequence of intervals — which doesn't clear up much on its own, but really it's just a matter of understanding that there's a ratio, a proportion, for generating a sequence. But it's always important to have a starting point, and watch out for this one, because it tends to be a dilemma among designers.

## Choosing a starting point

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L61 · 01:16–02:13

The starting point can be arbitrary — or rather, it should be. No scale can start from zero; it has to start from something it can be compared against. It could be an internal margin required for a technical reason the printer asks for — that measurement can serve as the starting point for developing a scale. So don't get confused into thinking that if you want to make a Fibonacci scale from the golden ratio, the first thing to do is set a margin measuring 1.618 centimetres. The starting point is whatever you want it to be.

From there, the proportion comes into play. A proportion is expressed like this, and it means that, in this case, it's 1.5 times that initial measurement — that starting point. After that, the sequence starts working: each interval is the result of applying the proportion to the value you just found. Having a list of proportioned, harmonic possibilities is a real advantage.

## The second dilemma: which intervals to keep

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L61 · 02:13–02:42

But then another dilemma comes in: which one do you choose? It's like a musical scale — all the notes are there, but to make melodies you don't play them all; you choose the ones that are necessary. Depending on the job you have to do, you'll need to select some, not all, of that generated sequence.

## Proportion in nature and geometry

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L61 · 02:42–03:44

As we saw, there are proportional relationships that are "divine," because they're found not only in nature but in many geometric shapes too. Take the pentagon, for example: the one that fits perfectly inside a circle has diagonals where the longer is to the shorter in golden-ratio proportion. That's why this is one of the great proportions to keep developing, and it can work with practically any format based on a square — which is to say, all of them.

Diagonals are fundamental for continuing to explore proportions and spaces within a given format — not only for finding axes, crossings and points of interest, but also for energizing compositions. But it all comes from a square: a square that gets folded and generates rectangles of different proportions, and that's what interests us. And here's a key point: you'll always find the square as the starting point. Folding a square back and extending it across the graphic field is a strategy for starting to find connecting lines that support perceptual forces.

## The chromatic scale and defining graphic space

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L61 · 03:44–05:08

This relationship of distance and quantity is also something we find in music. Each of these relationships, and others I won't include here, appear within a chromatic scale, where the tone at one end would be represented by the square, and its octave at the other end would be a doubled square — a 1-to-2 relationship. Subdividing that sonic space produces the different musical notes, and each of these notes has a proportional relationship with the whole. If the chromatic scale interests you, I've left material on it in the additional resources.

These rectangles serve to define graphic spaces, but a graphic space — that place where you work with protection — can be defined in two ways: either by building it directly on the page, or as a consequence of first defining its margins.

## The root-2 rectangle and self-folding formats

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L61 · 06:18–07:08

Another widely known and very particular proportion is the root-2 proportion — that is, 1.4142... This relationship is the one behind the DIN formats, and it has the special property of being the only self-folding proportion: this means that when you close a format in half, the result is the same proportion as the smaller format. This doesn't just bring benefits for industry and paper savings — it also opens up enormous possibilities for designing at different sizes without losing the proportions.

As we said throughout this whole course, formats are structural axes of perception; they affect the space and, by extension, everything that inhabits it. Recognizing their structure is part of our job — it's part of composition. This fabulous poster by Hans Rudolf Bosshard is also based on the root-2 rectangle: all its spaces are folded back and divided with diagonals and different squares to generate the proportions of its elements, and, this way, to place, suggest, or even reframe a photograph.

As we already saw, there's a golden ratio in those typographic intervals we built earlier. ### Key points (derived)

- A proportional scale is a sequence of intervals generated by repeatedly applying one ratio to a starting value; the starting point can be any measurement you choose (for example a printer's required margin) but can never be zero.
- Once a scale is generated, the designer's job is to select only the intervals a given piece needs — like choosing notes for a melody rather than playing an entire musical scale.
- The pentagon inscribed in a circle has diagonals in golden-ratio proportion, and this proportion applies to any square-based format — which is effectively every format.
- Every proportioned rectangle traces back to a square folded and subdivided in different ways; diagonals from that folding help locate axes, crossings, points of interest and dynamism in a composition.
- A chromatic musical scale — where the square is one tone and a doubled square is its octave (a 1:2 relationship) — maps onto graphic proportions such as 1:1, 4:5, 3:4, 2:3 and 1:2, and can be built directly into an identity system.
- A graphic space (the area protected for content) can be defined either by drawing it directly on the page or by first setting margins and letting the space result from them.
- The root-2 rectangle (1.4142…), the basis of the DIN paper formats, is the only self-folding proportion: folding it in half always reproduces the same proportion at the smaller size, which saves paper and lets a design scale across sizes without losing its proportions.
- A design can carry two useful proportions at once — for example a golden-ratio width (1.618, or 5:8) paired with a self-folded 4:5 height (1.25) — and both can seed their own typographic scale.
- A "double-point" scale, built by repeatedly multiplying a starting size by a fixed ratio (here 1.25), gives more gradual size steps than a Fibonacci sequence; unlike a Fibonacci scale, each interval depends only on the ratio, not on the sum of the two previous terms.
