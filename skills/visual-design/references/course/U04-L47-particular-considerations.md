## The composition checklist, and how to look

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L47 · 00:09–01:37

The concepts of visual composition apply to every area of design, though of course each one has its own particularities. In this lesson I'll explain how these concepts apply to the different fields of design. Let's take a look.

We said that the whole a composition sets out to design must meet certain requirements before we can judge it effective — before it communicates in an organized, aesthetic and meaningful way. Clarity, to define its themes and parts precisely. It has balance, understood as an equilibrium of tensions. It also has contrast, with clear hierarchies that bring out the information, and variety that enriches the message. It must have conceptual and formal unity. And, of course, it has the strength (*pregnancia*, visual impact) to make contact and hold the user's interest. All of these objectives hold for any communication that uses visual language.

![5642 · U4 · L47 · 00:41](frames/U04L47-F0041.jpg)

On screen: "Composición" (Composition); footer "Composición para el diseño gráfico · Javier Alcaraz" (Composition for graphic design · Javier Alcaraz) and "DOMESTIKA.ORG".  
Shows: A recap title card for the concept of composition, the single word set inside a soft grey circle.

However, each area of graphic design has its own considerations, according to its media and context. To get the most out of this lesson, I'd ask you to sharpen your eye and try to see the shapes, their attributes and their relationships in space. Avoid getting caught up in the appeal of an illustration, a photo or an elegant typeface.

## Logotypes: simplicity as a compositional key

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L47 · 01:37–02:44

A logotype is a graphic signature, the single most important element of an identity. The simplicity of this iconic logotype by Paul Rand is key to a graphic brand, driven by the need for visual impact (*pregnancia*) and to guarantee good reproduction in any medium. The repeated horizontal lines give it a distinctive rhythm that has since become emblematic.

![5642 · U4 · L47 · 01:55](frames/U04L47-F0155.jpg)

On screen: "IBM".  
Shows: Paul Rand's IBM logotype, its letterforms built from horizontal stripes, shown alone against a plain background.

The unity in this ABC logotype is likewise explained by the relationship between its very circular letterforms and the circle that contains them. This dialogue between content and container is what expresses its balance and unity. The extreme, almost minimalist geometry produces an effect of clarity.

The Westinghouse logotype achieves unity through the simplicity and plainness of its strokes. As with the ABC logotype, its balance comes from a precise, tightly fitted symmetry.

On screen: "IBM" and "abc".  
Shows: The IBM logotype placed beside the ABC circular monogram for comparison.

On screen: "W" (Westinghouse), "IBM", "abc".  
Shows: Three logotypes side by side — the Westinghouse circular "W" monogram added next to IBM and ABC.

## Identity systems: repeating the brand's attributes

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L47 · 02:45–03:35

These compositional traits in a logotype become the visual parameters of its identity. By repeating them, you can recognize where there's an opportunity for variation, and lay the groundwork for, say, a dynamic identity.

![5642 · U4 · L47 · 02:56](frames/U04L47-F0256.jpg)

On screen: "MIT MEDIA LAB", repeated on twelve small marks and once on the full-size version.  
Shows: A grid of twelve colour-and-shape variations of the MIT Media Lab dynamic identity, next to the assembled full-colour mark and wordmark.

Every sign in an identity inherits the brand's key traits — its colour, its use of space. In this example you can see that each piece repeats them. Across the different pieces of a branding system, the compositional attributes are either all touched on together, or one of them is pushed further.

![5642 · U4 · L47 · 03:09](frames/U04L47-F0309.jpg)

On screen: "SabineHolland." on an invoice, a folder, business cards and an envelope; monogram "sh." on a card.  
Shows: A stationery set for the identity "SabineHolland" — invoice, folder, business cards and envelope — repeating the same navy-and-pink palette and the small monogram.

In Jessica Walsh's Parlé Agro, you can see how colour is inherited across the different pieces of the system — and likewise the typography, and even the distinctive diacritic mark over the E, which repeats as a pattern or, on its own, as a pin.

![5642 · U4 · L47 · 03:35](frames/U04L47-F0335.jpg)

On screen: "Parlé Agro" repeated on business cards, a gate sign, a football jersey and a tote bag; "FEELIN' FROOTI" on a pin.  
Shows: A collage of Parlé Agro brand applications — stationery, entrance signage, a jersey, a shopping bag and a pin — all sharing the red-and-navy palette and the accented "é".

## Posters: contrast at scale

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L47 · 03:35–04:53

On a poster the surface is larger, and the perceptual forces work differently. With more space, silences and accents carry more power. To catch attention on the street you need to stand out forcefully — and nothing does that job better than contrast.

In these posters by Karl Herzna, contrast is a key compositional factor. Only through it does an element gain unquestionable hierarchy over the rest. The order becomes clear, with no ambiguity about where the viewer should start reading. In the example on the left, balance is achieved by letting the shapes descend, easing the tension of the diagonal directions. The movement is obvious and matches the message of sport.

![5642 · U4 · L47 · 04:06](frames/U04L47-F0406.jpg)

On screen: "National Sport Zeitung lesen" (Read the National sports newspaper); and, on the "3" poster, "Der Staat des Klassenkampfes, der Verbandspolitik, der zum Vorteil Einzelner dirigierten Wirtschaft ist nicht der liberale Staat. [...] Liberale Partei: Liste 3" (The state of class struggle [...] is not the liberal state. [...] Liberal Party: List 3) [rest omitted].  
Shows: Two red-and-cream posters side by side — a diagonal "National Sport Zeitung lesen" poster, and a numeral "3" poster for the Liberal Party.

As with these posters by Braulio Amado, balance is achieved not through symmetry but through a compensation of tensions. Unity comes from the repetition of the typographic shapes, which contrasts with the hand-written information — and, of course, with the monochrome photographs. Contrast of size, of placement, even of colour, are the strongest attributes the illustration has against the rest of the elements.

## Magazine covers: hierarchy under information overload

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L47 · 04:53–05:55

A publication's cover works, in a way, like a poster. It has to give one element hierarchy in order to make contact with the user, and from there suggest a path for reading. Magazine covers generally have to deal with a lot of information, and to do that designers draw on every gradation of contrast they consider necessary. Dosing it out and keeping the harmony is a real challenge in this kind of piece.

![5642 · U4 · L47 · 05:22](frames/U04L47-F0522.jpg)

On screen: left, "Áurea Revista de arte+literatura" (Áurea, art+literature magazine) / "SEVERINO SALAZAR el novelista olvidado" (Severino Salazar, the forgotten novelist) [rest omitted]; right, "VISIBLE LANGUAGE VOLUME VIII NUMBER 3 SUMMER 1974 THE JOURNAL FOR RESEARCH ON THE VISUAL MEDIA OF LANGUAGE EXPRESSION" [rest omitted, table of contents].  
Shows: Two dense magazine covers side by side — a literary review with a duotone portrait, and Wolfgang Weingart's "Visible Language" cover, a radiating black sun shape over a table of contents.

Contrast also shows up between figure and ground on this cover by Alexei Brodovich. But what really stands out is the very well-balanced composition, placing the elements in the corners of the graphic space.

![5642 · U4 · L47 · 05:36](frames/U04L47-F0536.jpg)

On screen: "Harper's BAZAAR February 1948 / The well-spent dollar / The bride makes her plans".  
Shows: Alexei Brodovich's Harper's Bazaar cover, a fashion photograph set low to one side against a large area of white space.

Francesco Franchi, for the covers of IL Magazine, uses a simple, almost geometric style that guarantees unity through the repetition of shapes. It's also an excellent technique for generating variations — against a regular context, any anomaly stands out.

## Book covers: freer composition

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L47 · 05:56–06:29

On book covers, by contrast, the fight to stand out at the point of sale, and the urgency of being relevant for one very short window of time, are no longer as decisive as they are for a magazine. That allows other rhythms in the composition, a more plastic, playful use of space and of imagery too. In the centre cover, by Roy Kuhlman, rhythm, repetition, hierarchy and clarity are achieved with a very expressive, spontaneous approach.

## Editorial interiors: pacing a sequence of pages

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L47 · 06:29–07:15

The interior pages of a publication present another interesting challenge. On one hand, the space grows and becomes much more horizontal, like a screen — the perceptual forces work differently there, and the information can vary much more in size and length. Balance and appeal operate on a very subtle level. On the other hand, the sequence of pages — the natural narrative of a book or a magazine — also produces compositional moments, as the artist Ulises Carrión used to say. Very much like film editing, the editorial designer has to design rhythm, dosage, explosion and silence, the way a film director does.

## Web design: composing across screens and interaction

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L47 · 07:15–08:42

This same movement and design of moments is also what web designers are used to. Composing the different screens, and the possible paths between them, means projecting the sequence of shapes the user will come across. On top of that, movement, animation and the different resources of interactivity add other factors to the composition that belong specifically to the world of the internet.

Web composition brings the challenge of intelligent shape and space. That doesn't just mean the design becomes responsive and changes its proportions and look according to the device — it also brings new parameters of contrast, clarity and hierarchy. How do you achieve unity between the different kinds of movement?

In these wonderful web-design proposals, you can see how the same parameters we can appreciate in a poster or a book can be applied to this medium too. Of course, it's important to stay alert to the particularities of each visual context.

### Key points (derived)

- An effective composition needs clarity, balance, contrast, variety, unity and visual impact (*pregnancia*) — but each area of graphic design applies these in its own way.
- Logotypes rely on extreme simplicity for visual impact and reproducibility: IBM's repeated horizontal lines give it a distinctive rhythm; ABC's unity comes from its circular letterforms sitting inside a circular container; Westinghouse's unity comes from the plainness of its strokes — and both ABC and Westinghouse get their balance from a precise, tightly fitted symmetry.
- A brand identity repeats a logotype's compositional traits — colour, space, typography, a distinctive mark — across every piece, sometimes touching all of them, sometimes pushing one attribute further (SabineHolland, Parlé Agro).
- Posters use scale and contrast to force a clear reading hierarchy from a distance.
- Magazine covers manage dense information through contrast and hierarchy; book covers, freed from the urgency of the newsstand, allow a more playful, expressive composition.
- Editorial interiors compose a sequence of pages like film editing — rhythm, dosage, explosion and silence.
- Web design adds movement, interactivity and responsiveness to the same compositional parameters used in print.
