## What a grid is

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L63 · 00:00–00:47

Grids (*retículas*) are powerful tools for composition. In this lesson I'll explain what their parts are, and, working from different examples, how they're used in graphic design. Let's take a look.

## Four types of grid: manuscript, multicolumn, modular and hierarchical

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L63 · 00:47–03:02

There are several types of grid, ranging from the simple to the complex, and that's directly related to the kind of work they have to do. Grids come out of content — that's no surprise — and grids that impose an order.

The manuscript grid is a grid that simply sets out a typographic space, a typographic box, bounded by the margins; it has a single column and one leading (*interlínea*). The other elements can either fit inside that bounded space, or try to come close to it.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 01:28](frames/U04L63-F0128.jpg)

On screen: "NEW TYPOGRAPHY / By Jan Tschichold" — "The new typography aims at a clear presentation of typographical images by the immaculate technique and by the use of forms which correspond to the new feeling for space." [rest omitted]; caption "PHOTOGRAM BY MOHOLY-NAGY."  
Shows: A single column of justified body text facing a full-bleed photogram, both set inside generous, unbroken margins.

Multicolumn grids are different from the previous kind in how many subdivisions they have on the horizontal plane; they're more versatile for managing complex content, and that gives them another degree of value. When time is short and there's a lot of work, a grid system can give you more speed and accuracy.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 01:50](frames/U04L63-F0150.jpg)

On screen: "Lewis Moody" — "Lancaster must stick with Laws and Launchbury in the engine room for Argentina game. They were too good to drop." [rest omitted]  
Shows: A sports-page spread, a wide photograph of a rugby match set across a run of narrow text columns with a portrait and pull quotes at the outer edge.

There are also multicolumn grid systems that aren't meant to lay material out from top to bottom and left to right, but rather to give the content different degrees and shades of emphasis.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 02:04](frames/U04L63-F0204.jpg)

On screen: (German-language book spread, a historical essay) "1922 Faschismus in Italien" / "1933 Nationalsozialismus in Deutschland" / "1936 Faschismus in Spanien" / "1938 Nationalsozialismus in Österreich" (1922 Fascism in Italy / 1933 National Socialism in Germany / 1936 Fascism in Spain / 1938 National Socialism in Austria) [rest omitted]  
Shows: A dense two-column book spread with no images at all, the columns used purely to pace a long block of running text.

The most popular are modular grid systems. Within the graphic space they define equal modules and assign each piece of content a plot; combining these small units produces zones, fields that organise information across different hierarchical levels.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 02:28](frames/U04L63-F0228.jpg)

On screen: "AUSTRALIAN INVENTIONS" spelled out letter by letter across the grid, each remaining cell dated and captioned with a short story (e.g. "1954 Rotary Hoe," "1958 Black Box Flight Recorder," "1958 Kiwi Boot Polish"). [rest omitted]  
Shows: A checkerboard poster where every module is either one letter of the headline, a photograph, or a caption, all the same size.

The hierarchical grid, like the cover we designed for this course, isn't built by subdividing the space in a clinical, granular way, but rather by defining areas, points and zones of real interest. The rule of thirds, as you saw in previous lessons, helped us a great deal in generating that hierarchical point and the other elements in the composition. The hierarchical grid is a bit more flexible, but for that same reason it's also a bit more fragile.

## Breaking the grid on purpose

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L63 · 03:26–04:04

What's curious is that a lot of people worry more about breaking the grid than about understanding it, and it's hard to face something we don't understand. There are many ways to step outside the strict organisation of a grid system. One of them can be granularity: imposing on the grid another grid, with a different condition, so that it becomes much more evident. Another strategy is to overlap or enclose graphic elements so that they detach themselves from the context that governs them. And, of course, against a very marked, rigid space, nothing works better than the contrast of a very loose shape, arranged in a casual, spontaneous way, to bring out both the one and the other.

## The anatomy of a grid: unit, columns, gutters and fields

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L63 · 04:04–06:37

For a grid system the minimum unit matters a great deal: defining that minimum unit is going to condition every other space in the project. Usually that minimum unit is the text's leading, which is why the first major subdivision always happens vertically.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 04:25](frames/U04L63-F0425.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A blank spread already ruled with a dense set of thin vertical guides as well as the fine horizontal lines of the leading, before individual columns have been marked out within them.

Columns let us lay out the space for different line widths, so that other kinds of content can share the same page. On top of that, combined with the vertical space, we can assign different photo sizes very solidly — though it would be awkward for the material to be laid out without the distance between elements being felt.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 04:34](frames/U04L63-F0434.jpg)

On screen: "Columnas" (Columns).  
Shows: The same spread with three vertical bands of text highlighted per page, separated by narrower unhighlighted bands — the columns marked out within the leading grid.

That's what gutters (*medianiles*) are for, which also work as flow lines (*líneas de flujo*) — like big connecting avenues between pieces of content. They speed up left-to-right reading and allow for breaks that can also be managed on purpose.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 05:05](frames/U04L63-F0505.jpg)

On screen: "Líneas de flujo" (Flow lines).  
Shows: Three horizontal bands laid across the same columned spread, marking out the reading breaks that run left to right through the whole page.

Combining the spaces between columns and the vertical spaces — the flow lines — gives you modules. These modules have to meet requirements set in advance: I mean that those modules need to answer to something concrete, rather than complicating the space just for the sake of complicating it.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 05:29](frames/U04L63-F0529.jpg)

On screen: "Módulos" (Modules).  
Shows: The intersections of columns and flow lines highlighted as a grid of twelve equal rectangular cells on the left page.

Adding modules together gives you fields, and fields can enclose larger portions of information: spaces for headlines, full photographs, sections running parallel to a piece's central body. The possibilities are huge, provided we've understood the content beforehand.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 05:34](frames/U04L63-F0534.jpg)

On screen: "Campos" (Fields).  
Shows: A highlighted top field spanning three columns of the left page, and a larger, lower field spanning both pages below it, showing fields of different sizes built from the same module.

We also have auxiliary lines, which, although they don't sit inside the graphic space, because they aren't bounded by the margin, are there to offset elements and to carry other kinds of information, such as folios, and so round out the richness of the design.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 06:04](frames/U04L63-F0604.jpg)

On screen: "Lineas auxiliares" (Auxiliary lines).  
Shows: A thin band highlighted just outside the margin, running down both outer edges and along the foot of the spread.

There are grids that get imposed on the whole graphic space, but it's normal practice to set the margins first and then grid the typographic box. That way, elements can coexist in the space — whether they're text elements or photographic ones — connect with each other, and compose with a much more stable logic. And, as we said, that speeds up the process.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 06:37](frames/U04L63-F0637.jpg)

On screen: "UTOPÍAS POSIBLES / Bjarke Ingels" (Possible utopias); "'Si en verdad quieres cambiar al mundo tienes que pensar en algo que sea ecológico y económico'" (If you really want to change the world you have to think about something that's both ecological and economical). [rest omitted]  
Shows: An interview spread, its underlying column-and-module grid switched on, holding a full-bleed portrait, a pull quote and running body text to the same structure.

## A grid needs its own strategy for breaking it

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L63 · 06:37–07:05

Every time a grid needs to manage content, that goes hand in hand with its own strategy for breaking it — so that the one small line that departs from the system is the only one that does.

![5642 · U4 · L63 · 06:50](frames/U04L63-F0650.jpg)

On screen: "EL PLANETA DEL ÉXITO / Jaime Hayón" (The planet of success); "'Fui un mal estudiante, un desastre total. Vivía en mi propio planeta. Debo admitir que todavía lo hago'" (I was a bad student, a total disaster. I lived on my own planet. I have to admit I still do). [rest omitted]  
Shows: A second interview spread on the same grid, its pull quote's black background and the "1" numbered caption chip both breaking past the column lines that hold the rest of the page.

### Key points (derived)

- A grid is a diagram of guides that organises content and lets it be handled with more control, or more looseness, depending on the job.
- Four grid types were named: manuscript (one column, set by the margins), multicolumn (subdivided horizontally, suited to complex or time-pressured content), modular (equal modules assigned to content), and hierarchical (built around areas of interest rather than a fine subdivision, more flexible but more fragile).
- Grids are the natural tool of editorial design but also organise wayfinding and signage systems.
- Breaking a grid on purpose (adding a second, contrasting grid, overlapping elements, or setting a loose shape against a rigid one) works only once the underlying order is understood.
- A grid builds up from one minimum unit (usually the text's leading): leading gives columns, columns plus gutters (which double as flow lines) give modules, and modules combine into fields.
- Auxiliary lines sit outside the margin and carry secondary information such as folios.
- Practice is to fix the margins first, then grid the typographic box inside them, so text and image can share one stable structure.
- A working grid should still carry its own deliberate exception — the one line that is allowed to break it.
