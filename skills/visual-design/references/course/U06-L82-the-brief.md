## Researching the name: an open, personal process

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L82 · 03:26–04:12

As part of the initial creative process, and already having the product's name, what I'm going to do is research every possible piece of data I come across regarding the name of this song I chose. What I do is divide it into these three areas: the area of reason, the area of emotion and the area of attention. And into these three different areas I keep pouring the various data I find in my research — research that doesn't have to be so solemn or so defined, but should start a bit more from your own curiosities.

## Reason: the hard facts behind Kashmir

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L82 · 04:13–05:32

In my case, I found some interesting data: for example, the song was created in 1975 in the United Kingdom; the song's duration, 8 minutes 32 seconds; the origin of the song's name, which has to do with a mountain area in the middle of India, called the Valley of Kashmir — which caught my attention a lot, where its origins come from. It speaks of Kashyap (captions: *Kajad*) and Mira. Kashyap was a sage, and Mira means sea, and from there comes Kashmira. This sage Kashyap — whose name means "the one with the black teeth" or "the one who devours the real like the night" — struck me as a very poetic definition, and I think this song carries some of that magic inside it, in its lyrics and its rhythm. Legend says this sage drank all the water from a lake, and a fish was left exposed; that fish was a demon, and from there, from when this sage drank all the water from the lake, the Valley of Kashmir emerges. There's something there in rebirth, in transforming one thing back into another, which I think also belongs to the song.

## Emotion: how Kashmir feels

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L82 · 05:33–06:08

If we go to the line of emotions, to this circle: it's a song that feels very intense, very repetitive, that has something like hypnosis, a bit of psychedelia, a bit of nostalgia. There's something like patterns that keep repeating over and over. Basically the song is built on a rhythm that repeats and repeats and repeats. It has moments of whispers, it has moments of screams. At times it's a very slow, heavy song; at times it's like a song that feels like a mystical revelation.

## Attention: the details that stand out

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L82 · 06:09–07:41

And going to the triangle's data — data that catches my own attention — I find, for example, that they used a mellotron, an instrument the band didn't normally use, and that already gives me maybe a color note for my project. I find that this song is long by design: they said, let's make a song that's long. I also find some phrases or statements from the singer and the lyricist, who said many times that this song, even though it talks about a place in India, was actually created on a trip through the Sahara, in Morocco. This desert took him through certain experiences and made the author feel that this song was like his definitive song. He talked about this song as his definitive song — it was bigger than him. Many times it was very hard for him to sing this song; in fact, there are several performance mistakes in some recordings, in some videos, in some tapes, that have to do with this feeling that the song was much bigger than its own author and its own singer and performer. According to him, the lyrics speak of sages, of a lost race, of sages lost in time and space. It constantly talks about this, about a journey through time and space.

## Closing: on to the story

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L82 · 08:32–08:43

Now that we have our product's name and know who it's aimed at, we'll start telling our story.

### Key points (derived)

- The brief: a new canned drink (beer, energy drink or apéritif), refreshing and fruity with a touch of alcohol, aimed at curious 18–45-year-olds with an active social life; the differentiator is a collection of three flavors.
- Naming method: pick a band or musician from your own mood board with your heart, not your reason — the teacher picks Led Zeppelin, then the song "Kashmir" (Physical Graffiti, 1975), then apéritifs as the product category.
- Once named, research the choice and sort everything found into three lenses: reason (hard facts), emotion (creative/emotional data), attention (details that stand out).
- Reason data for "Kashmir": 1975, UK, 8'32", the word's origin in the Kashmir valley and the legend of the sage Kashyap.
- Emotion data: intense, repetitive, hypnotic, psychedelic, nostalgic, alternating whispers and screams, slow and heavy, almost a mystical revelation.
- Attention data: an unusual mellotron, a deliberately long song, Robert Plant calling it his "definitive song... bigger than me", written in the Sahara despite lyrics about India, and lyrics about lost sages traveling through time and space.
- Don't leave anything to chance during research — note down anything that catches your attention, then clean and refine the three lists into a tidy final version.
