## A special case: an identity that's meant to change

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L99 · 00:32–01:03

We're going to talk about a special case of image, first because it's based on a real theatre company, and second because we're going to see how this is, in a way, the counter-example of what a corporate image should be. ## Why give up consistency: integration over identity

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L99 · 07:23–09:08

What are we trying to explain in this lesson? Basically that an image for a company, a business, a service, can sometimes actually benefit from not keeping a certain consistency — that having a very marked logotype, or one with a very strong style of its own, can work against it. So in this case, for example, with a theatre production, it's better to do this rather than that: to adapt to whatever we find ourselves working with. We'll see later how we can turn this to our advantage. On one hand it might seem like we're losing identity, that our mark doesn't show up there — but on the other hand, we do manage to get it to integrate better into what we're doing. It's a theatre company, so the performance, the production, matters more than having a logotype that would turn the show into something a bit more vulgar; instead, we're making something that integrates very well. The same goes here: integrating the logotype, creating what's almost a new logotype each time, can work out well for us. In this case, this second option is better than the first. Here too, we can clearly see this works better than that. Here, this also works better. Here, this fits much better with the original shape. And here, clearly, this works much, much better than what we saw before.

## Turning inconsistency into an asset: a mark that grows

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L99 · 09:08–10:23

In corporate work, this can actually be an advantage. We can make something out of it, as they did at the time, with elements like this one: something that keeps changing over time and doesn't create an identity crisis for us — it simply serves to develop the mark and keep changing it as time goes on. Trying to keep this logotype identical across every production would actually work against us. So this is something we can apply in some cases, and it can even be quite interesting: when we don't know what mark to use, we can fall back on this default mark for the stationery, or when we're asked for this logotype for a collaboration or whatever it might be. That is, we can have a base mark, but it wouldn't be a strong mark — rather, in this case, it would be what you might call a weak mark, except that it's strong precisely because it keeps growing over time and adapting. Being flexible is what makes it better.

### Key points (derived)

- The lesson inverts the usual identity rule by design: rather than judging the mark by how consistently it reproduces, here it's judged by how well it disappears into each show's own visual language, and the "T de Teatre" wordmark is explicitly redrawn, in full or in part, to match each show's typeface rather than the other way round.
- Two caption errors turn up in the same short typography passage: "albética noye" for Helvetica Neue (01:36) and "Doberberg" for Adobe Birch (03:16) — both close-sounding substitutions rather than real typeface names, translated here from what the on-screen specimens actually show.
- The stationery/anniversary-notebook payoff (09:08–10:23) resolves the tension the lesson opens with: a company still needs one steady, "weak" default mark for contexts with no specific show to match — the flexibility is additive, not a replacement for having a base mark at all.
