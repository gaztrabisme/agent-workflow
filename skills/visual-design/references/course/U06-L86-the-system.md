## What "system" means

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L86 · 00:26–01:50

A system is a combination of parts that come together to reach a result or form a whole. But talking about systems also means talking about a lot of other systems that have nothing to do with a visual-identity system. One of the best-known, recognised from childhood, is the solar system: a set of planets aligned together, each one holding a relationship with the others that makes it unique. The nervous system is another example, and there are plenty more found in nature all around us, almost without noticing. Thinking about a more general definition: a system is more than a simple sum of parts — it is each of those parts, their individual functions, and their intrinsic relationships. That is, the specific function of each part, and how each part relates to all the other parts of the system, together with each one's specific function.

## The basic elements: logo, typography, colour, image

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L86 · 01:50–03:03

A system is more than a simple sum of parts — it is the parts themselves, their individual functions, and their intrinsic relationships. Broadly speaking, a visual-identity system is a group of elements, several of which come to mind right away. The first is the logo, the brand mark, or the isologo — whatever it gets called: the graphic mark that defines the product or service. Second comes typography, a discipline that runs through design in an almost singular way — arguably the most essential, most distinctly "design" element there is. On this project, for instance, a typeface could be custom-built or simply chosen from a catalogue. Beyond that, colour is also part of what builds the system, and colour is often paired with a fourth leg: image. "Image" can mean photography, illustration, patterns, textures and other elements that make up the picture.

## More elements: concept, composition, tone, support

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L86 · 03:03–04:39

There are other elements that also make up a visual-identity system: concept, composition, tone and support. In a project developed together with designer and illustrator Ulla, it's easy to see what these mean once the discussion moves past the basic elements and into the conceptual depths. That means working out a phrase, a slogan, a piece of writing that comes before developing the graphic visual-identity system itself. Composition is about how all the elements that make up a graphic system sit and relate to each other in space. Tone is about deciding whether the system reads as more adult or more childlike, more cheerful or more sombre, brighter or darker. And then there's support — the material the design actually lives on. Support is something that sometimes gets left for last consideration, but it is undoubtedly very important: without being aware of what the final support or material will be, it becomes hard to design something and then carry it over to a material where it doesn't perform well.

## Complexity: low and high

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L86 · 04:39–06:41

Systems can also be looked at by complexity. A low-complexity system might be one developed for a denim clothing brand, where the only things really doing the work of the system are the brand mark, the isotype playing a fairly prominent role across the pieces, and the colour palette — solid colour backgrounds competing and blending with the isotype's colour. That's a fairly low-complexity system: the rest of the pieces that accompany a bag like this will look very similar, generally with a blue background playing against the typography or isotype in a gold ink. On the other side are higher-complexity systems, such as this visual-identity design proposal for a basketball championship, where the many elements laid out on the page get ordered, grouped and modified depending on the piece and what's left of it. The same blue background, for instance, has to be adapted for each piece — all these textures, miscellaneous elements, gradients and the use of colour and typography adjust to the support depending on what each piece needs to say. One poster uses one composition, another poster uses a different one, the clothing does its own thing, and so on for the rest of the pieces. This higher complexity means the visual-identity system gets stitched together through small cues and gestures that the viewer has to learn to read — which asks for a viewer who is a bit more visually trained or fluent.

## Strategy: formal systems

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L86 · 06:41–07:26

There are also functions, and ways a visual-identity system gets built strategically. One option is a system governed by the formal — that is, the morphological. That's the case in this example, a project for an Argentine cultural centre, where the iconography is pushed to the front and each discipline the centre covers takes over one piece chromatically. The whole system ends up held together and fed simply through form.

## Strategy: conceptual systems

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L86 · 07:26–08:33

## Flexibility: closed and open systems

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L86 · 09:08–10:23

On the system's flexibility, there are two opposite paths. A more closed flexibility means the system is always governed by two or three elements that appear and disappear, but always predictably — their placement is always known, and the viewer isn't left with anything to puzzle over on each piece. On the other side, more open identity systems let the designer's and the product's own decisions mean each piece can carry different ideas or different goals, with the elements that make up the system grouping more loosely or abstractly — appearing one way in some pieces, another way in others. Even so, the system still gets woven together through small decisions that the viewer will learn to spot.

## What makes a system

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L86 · 10:23–11:46

To sum up: something becomes a system simply when there are two or more parts, two or more elements in dialogue with each other, generating a bigger, higher-level idea. A system also needs a certain order and sequence — the sense of gradually discovering the system, while always knowing that something is fixed, or sits in a determined place the viewer can find easily. On top of that, a system should avoid repetition, unless the decision is to build a completely closed system — ideally a system carries a sense of dynamism and fluidity so it never tips into the feeling of automated repetition. Finally: a system always has particular objectives and complementary objectives — each piece has its own particular objective, which it fulfils in service of a bigger goal, while also being complementary to the objectives of the rest of the pieces.

### Key points (derived)

- A system is more than the sum of its parts: it is the parts, their individual functions, and how they relate to each other (compare the solar system or the nervous system).
- The core elements of a visual-identity system: logo/brand mark, typography, colour, and image (photography, illustration, patterns, textures).
- Further elements: concept (the idea or slogan behind the system), composition (how elements sit in space), tone (mood: adult/childlike, bright/dark), and support (the final material — easy to overlook, but decisive for how the design performs).
- Systems vary in complexity (a denim brand's low-complexity system vs. a basketball championship's high-complexity one) and in strategy (formal/morphological vs. conceptual vs. mixed systems).
- Systems also vary in flexibility: closed (predictable, fixed elements) vs. open (looser, more abstract groupings that the viewer has to piece together).
- A system needs: two or more parts in dialogue, order/sequence, avoidance of flat repetition, and objectives that are both particular to each piece and complementary to the whole.
