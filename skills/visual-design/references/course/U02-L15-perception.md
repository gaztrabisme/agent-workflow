## Color works through perception and context

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L15 · 00:00–01:41

After shape and typography, the lesson turns to color. Artists such as Olafur Eliasson and James Turrell use colored light as a central part of their work. Eliasson fills rooms with colored light: a room that appears yellow can affect perception so that a viewer’s hands seem black and white. Turrell also uses colored light to make spaces that seem two-dimensional become three-dimensional, creating apparent hollows or recesses through light alone.

Color has a strong scientific basis, but its use in design is also shaped by experience, culture, and country of origin. Perception is physical and psychological. A color’s full effect emerges in relation to another color, including achromatic colors (*acromáticos*) such as white, black, and gray. A white square on a dark field can seem larger than a black square on a light field. The same medium gray can look light against a dark background and dark against a white background, so judge colors in relation to their neighbors.

## Contrast, harmony, and cultural meaning

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L15 · 01:41–04:16

Color harmony (*armonía cromática*) is often described as something pleasing, but that judgment is subjective. The teacher approaches it through color relationships. A gray square against yellow seems to take on a violet cast, because violet is yellow’s complementary color (*color complementario*). Against orange, the same gray seems bluish; against blue, it seems yellowish. When we stare at one flat color and then close our eyes, the brain tends to imagine its complement, as if balancing the colors.

With more than two colors, the brain also seems to average what it sees. A palette with several colors can balance darks with lights, or use colors of similar lightness, to create a sense of equilibrium. These responses are still subjective and change with time and culture. White may mean cleanliness or peace to the teacher, but in other countries it can signify mourning, bad luck, or death. Designers need to know who and where they are designing for. The teacher’s own view is that colors are colors: they are a powerful design tool to combine with other elements, rather than carriers of a hidden meaning that should bear the whole design. Peace need not be represented with white if the color works with the chosen forms and type.

## Build and read the color wheel

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L15 · 04:16–05:42

The color wheel (*círculo cromático*) helps identify complements. Its three starting colors here are yellow, cyan or blue, and magenta or red—the pinkish color. Place them as the points of an equilateral triangle, with yellow at the top, cyan at lower left, and magenta at lower right. Mixing blue and yellow makes green; yellow and magenta make orange; blue and magenta make violet. Those are the secondary colors. Continue mixing between the spaces to fill out the wheel. Complementary colors sit directly opposite each other: violet and yellow, blue and orange, green and red. The teacher suggests making a painted wheel by hand to explore the mixes.

## Hue, saturation, and brightness

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L15 · 05:42–07:04

Hue (*tono*) is the color itself. Saturation (*saturación*) is how pure it is: a highly saturated color is far from gray, while a desaturated one moves toward gray. Brightness (*brillo*) is its lightness: more brightness moves a color toward white, and less moves it toward black. For example, the green she shows is grayish and therefore not very saturated, but it is bright because it is light. The violet is pure and saturated, but has little brightness because it is close to black. She then asks viewers to judge a third swatch for its hue, saturation, and brightness.

## Make six signal flags

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L15 · 07:04–09:00

Nautical signal flags send varied messages without text, using color and geometric shapes. The teacher likes how they can communicate a specific warning—such as having a diver submerged—without letters. The exercise is to design six flags for messages used in everyday life, with complementary colors.

For her set, she chooses five colors that make two complementary pairs, blue with orange and green with red, then adds yellow to balance the set and allow more combinations. Her flags encode reminders to take out the rubbish, get up after sitting too long, charge a phone whose battery is running low, and avoid interrupting a video call. Geometric shapes are welcome, but the flags should have a similar level of complexity: each can include lines or circles while keeping comparable detail. Viewers are asked to make their own flags.

### Key points (derived)

- Perception depends on adjacent colors as well as cultural context.
- Use the color wheel to identify complements; hue, saturation, and brightness describe different color attributes.
- The flag exercise applies complementary pairs to geometric messages with consistent complexity.
