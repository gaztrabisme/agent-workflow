## Brand colour under RGB versus CMYK

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L103 · 00:00–02:24

Even though brand thinking still defaults to paper, the reality today is that brands are seen on screens far more than on any other support. This lesson looks at some of the things that affect how a brand lives online, starting with colour: on-screen colour is built with the RGB system — Red, Green, Blue — and that can change how a brand ends up looking in some cases.

![5642 · U7 · L103 · 00:08](frames/U07L103-F0008.jpg)

On screen: "Imagen de marca en pantalla" (Brand image on screen)  
Shows: Title card for the lesson.

The most serious case is this: if a brand's colours were designed for CMYK printing, moving to RGB shouldn't in principle cause a problem — a colour built from a CMYK formula barely changes when converted to RGB. The problem shows up the other way round: a colour designed for screen, which looks brighter, loses that quality when converted to CMYK. So not every colour reproduces the same in both systems, and the main problem is going from RGB to CMYK, not the reverse.

Pero al revés, sí." (Colour / On screen, colours are built with the RGB system. / If your image's colours were designed for print (CMYK), moving to RGB won't be a problem. Even so, not every colour reproduces in RGB either. Although RGB covers a wider range, some colours — fluorescent inks, for instance, or even colours assumed to come out fine in RGB that actually don't — fall outside it. A chocolate brand's quite distinctive colour, one that stands out a lot on shop shelves, reproduces very well in flat, spot inks but not in RGB: colours fall outside RGB's range not only in special cases like fluorescents, but also with some pastels and cream tones that don't come out right even on screen. This needs care.

As a conclusion on colour: it isn't a bad idea to build a brand's colours based mainly on wherever they'll be used most.

## Fitting a mark into a small, round avatar

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L103 · 02:24–03:14

On proportion, which also ties into size: an important factor today is how a brand appears in the avatar — the smallest space available, but also the one that's almost constantly present everywhere. It's a round space, or proportionally as wide as it is tall, and ideally a mark should fit that space well. That said, the avatar doesn't have to be a logotype: there are many ways to give a brand presence there without relying solely on the logotype.

![5642 · U7 · L103 · 03:05](frames/U07L103-F0305.jpg)

On screen: "Proporción / La proporción de la imagen del avatar puede ser un condicionante para la imagen. / El avatar no necesariamente debe ser un logotipo." (Proportion / The avatar image's proportions can be a constraint on the image. / The avatar doesn't necessarily have to be a logotype.)  
Shows: Three round avatar examples — a shell-brand emblem, a swoosh-brand wordless mark, and a cropped photograph (a hand holding a ball bearing that same swoosh) used as an avatar in place of a logotype.

This mainly affects size too: the avatar means a complex mark — like some of the examples worked through earlier in this course — simply doesn't work well. So another thing worth keeping in mind when making a mark today is that it should work at small sizes, which means it should be a simple mark.

## Keeping marks simple, and the responsive idea

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L103 · 03:14–04:24

![5642 · U7 · L103 · 03:25](frames/U07L103-F0325.jpg)

On screen: "Tamaño / Las marcas muy complejas no funcionan a tamaños pequeños." (Size / Very complex marks do not work at small sizes.); "Cuaderno de Bitácora / Editorial" (Logbook / Publishing House — the publisher's name)  
Shows: A publisher's full circular emblem (compass-rose graphic plus wordmark) beside its own simplified small-scale avatar version — a plain lower-case letterform on a solid colour ground.

A trend worth knowing about is *responsive* design, applied mainly to web pages or content layout — content adapting to different screen formats and sizes. That trend is also reaching brands and logotypes: some marks now adapt, or appear in more or less simplified forms of their own content, depending on the space they need to fill. This is one of the more fashionable topics in recent years, well covered elsewhere online.

It's actually something that's always been done with the shape of marks — simpler forms. This came up earlier in the course with publishers' marks that appear on book spines: the version of a mark used there tends to be simpler. In a way this already existed; it's just being used in this other, more deliberate way today.

## Designing for changing formats with a complementary graphic element

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L103 · 04:24–05:43

On formats: those used by platforms and networks keep changing all the time, and will keep changing, because formats change, networks change, everything changes. One way to approach a company's image from the start is to design it so it adapts to different formats — horizontal, square or vertical. The complementary graphic element mentioned in an earlier lesson can be quite useful here: it can let a piece grow horizontally, and sometimes vertically. Two examples familiar to Spanish readers make the point well: a striped band and a flag pattern, each working as an extension of a corporate image without being the company's logotype itself. So remember this: a complementary graphic element can be very useful for extending a brand into formats the logotype, so to speak, doesn't cover well.

Now that brands are set up on screen, the next lesson turns to what it takes to keep a brand alive.

### Key points (derived)

- A brand's colours may not round-trip cleanly between print and screen: CMYK-to-RGB is usually safe, RGB-to-CMYK usually isn't, and some colours (fluorescents, some pastels and creams) don't reproduce faithfully in either RGB or CMYK.
- Decide a brand's core colours based on where they'll mostly be seen, rather than assuming one formula serves both print and screen equally.
- The avatar — a small, roughly square-to-round space, present almost everywhere online — is a real design constraint: a complex mark will not survive being shrunk into it, and the avatar need not be the logotype at all.
- "Responsive" branding (an identity that simplifies or reconfigures itself for the space available) is a current label for something graphic design has always done, such as simplifying a publisher's mark for a book spine.
- Plan a brand's visual system for horizontal, square and vertical formats from the outset, since platform formats keep changing; a complementary graphic element (separate from the logotype) is one way to let a piece grow or adapt without redesigning the mark itself.
