## Introducing weight

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 00:06–00:58

Unity in composition was covered already; now the subject is weight (*peso*). This is another important concept for categorizing elements within a composition, for helping the eye know where to look across the compositional space.

To begin with, this weight — this ordering, this categorizing, this way of giving compositional elements different levels of importance — can be achieved through the size an element has relative to the format, its relationship to the format. In this case the materials themselves can't be changed or reproduced, so what can be worked on is reducing or enlarging the format instead.

## Demonstration: format size sets the weight

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 00:58–02:04

Here you can see how these two elements work with this format. If the format is reduced, they immediately take on much more prominence, much more visual mass. Here's another example of how the expression changes when the format is reduced: the prominence grows, the free space left over is much smaller, and the shapes carry much more force.

This can be pushed further in progression, increasing how much of the background gets occupied — until a point is reached where the shapes are suffocated. There's a moment, a point, at which the background space shifts. There has to be a relationship and a harmony between the compositional space and the shapes within it; in this case the space became too rigid. An intermediate size would be the right one. This exercise shows, in a simple and hopefully instructive way, how to arrive at the right weight.

Working with this solution gives a much lower contrast: with the same size of piece, a much lighter weight is achieved. It has far less presence.

## Darkening the background

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 04:40–05:23

Another way to get the same effect, if working with a dark background, is to darken the background itself. Altering the tone of the background alters the weight of the images placed on it: as the tones of the figure and the background are brought closer together and merged, weight is taken away from the shapes. Here, instead, the background tone is increased — that is, darkened — to make the object read as lighter by comparison. This can be pushed further still by working the contrast with a genuinely dark background, which starts to produce something like a negative — that's the effect being built here.

That, then, is the second way of giving images weight: the colour contrast of the object against its background.

## A bad example: chaos in an ad

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 06:39–07:37

As discussed earlier, the mind doesn't like chaos, and this is a clear case of it. On one hand, three things compete for attention, with no clear categorization. There isn't a clear number one against a number two and a number three: there are two number-ones fighting each other for attention, and a number two competing with a number four for prominence too. ## Position in space: centered means stable

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 07:37–08:27

Back to the starting point: weight has been achieved so far through the ordering of elements within the format, and through colour contrast against the background. The other element to take into account for giving an object a different weight is its position in space. As pieces are placed within the space, different weights result. If this same piece is used here and allowed to sit apart, the frame acts as it always does, like a magnet on the object; if it's centred, it can be said to be in a state of stability — the force the frame exerts on these two sides and these two verticals is exactly equal. That is, there's balance.

## Position in space: low is heavy, high is light

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 08:27–09:22

As the object is moved toward one of the sides, it gets pulled with greater force in that direction, and so its weight, or its sense of movement, starts to transform. Generally, when an image sits low in the format, it carries weight — it weighs more than if it were near the top. Near the top it becomes much lighter; the higher it's pushed, especially against the top edge, the lighter it reads; the lower it's brought down, the heavier it becomes — and this with the very same object and the very same contrast. This is the third of the resources for giving a given image weight. Having now explained these basic ideas of the frame's pull and the weight it exerts on objects, it's worth showing a couple of examples of finished work.

## The fourth resource: internal direction

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 10:34–11:56

Three resources for increasing weight have been covered. The fourth is to use internal force — the directionality of the piece itself — to create more or less weight. If an element within the composition carries an internal energy, because of how it's joined, because different angles meet at one point, then the energy of the shape turns toward that end; if the shape is then placed in the upper part of the format, it will rise with even more force. Combine the two — the frame's pull together with the shape's internal force — and the effect works with still greater force.

The same thing happens with this shape, a circle: watching how it behaves, it acts with a force in one direction. Keep supporting that force upward and the shape gives a positive, rising sensation. Work with that same shape pointing downward, and the figure will fall and weigh more. With these four concepts, the right weight for whatever an image needs can be achieved.

## Finding and isolating the shape

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 11:56–13:15

To close, here's a practical example of how one of these pieces was worked. This piece is a fragment of a bicycle tyre found on the beach. Its texture was interesting, its folds were interesting — it was a strange piece, and it wasn't clear how it had been made or how to use it.

So, taking the method discussed from the beginning, the first step was to isolate it: this is a shape of two curves that join and then open apart — joined on one side, open on the other. Then came analysing it, seeing what the shape communicated. Seeing it stopped being about a scrap of bicycle tyre and became about seeing it as a shape. The first step, then, was analysing the shape itself, seeing how it behaved and what orientation it could be worked in. It was moved around in space: face down it reads as a heavy shape, something like a duck walking, with little style — a shape that falls. Turning it upward, though, a different shape appears: one that rises, an arc running from bottom to top that lifts.

## Testing position: heavy versus flying

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 13:57–14:40

Step two was pushing further on that weight, on the idea of flying, relative to the format. So what to do with the piece? It was moved, shifted to one side to give it a certain imbalance and interest, let drop — and the left side pulled at it with a great deal of force; instead of advancing, the image seemed to retreat, and on top of that it weighed a great deal. The conclusion reached was that it had to move toward the left for the image to advance. But lower down it still weighed heavily — the image kept weighing too much. Raising it, though, it suddenly became clear the piece was starting to fly. The format, however, now felt a little suffocating.

## Preview: balance and imbalance

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L75 · 15:40–15:56

The next lesson turns to balance — above all to imbalance, which is of more interest than balance itself. Balance is very straightforward; imbalance is far more complex, and far more interesting.

### Key points (derived)

- Weight can be set through size relative to the format: the same object gains or loses prominence as the format around it is reduced or enlarged.
- Weight can also be set through contrast: lowering contrast against the background lightens an object; darkening the background (or working toward a near-negative) increases it.
- Colour itself can carry weight, independent of size — a vivid element reads as heavier than a pale one of the same size.
- Position within the frame matters: centred is stable; low in the format reads as heavy, high reads as light.
- A shape's own internal direction (set by its angles or joints) adds to or fights against the frame's pull.
- The closing demonstration applies all of this in sequence: isolate the shape, choose its orientation, fit the format, test its position, then add a smaller element for scale and comparison.
