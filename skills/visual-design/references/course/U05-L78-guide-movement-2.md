## Introducing Chema Madoz

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L78 · 00:06–00:35

To close this lesson — the last one — three photographs by Chema Madoz are worth discussing. He's a master, as far as I'm concerned: setting aside his surrealist content, formally his work is simply impeccable. I'd like to show how he has folded several of the principles discussed today into his images.

## The cord that becomes a hand

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L78 · 00:35–01:25

This is an image already shown before. What stands out first, beyond the substitutions he makes between elements, is the clarity of his image's structure, its composition — how he creates such a brutal visual movement. The composition and the whole image's centre of interest concentrate at one point: the space where the vertical line converges, a powerful line that ends in a dark point. Then there's the line entering from the right and rising, and the short but powerful dark mark of the hand. All three are balanced against each other, and all three build the interest of the image through the crossing of the lines and through the space that's left to be completed.

## A branch that becomes a vein

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L78 · 01:25–02:15

Moving to another image, also very clearly organised around its visual path. Notice how the eye is drawn into the image through the triangle on the left — the most important dark mass in the whole picture. It's the image's heaviest, most contrasted shape, and it directs us along this angle; the force pushes toward the hand, and the hand carries us out the other side. Very subtly, the image circles back once more to this same idea: the movement runs left to right, and only then do we notice that the branches are standing in for the veins of the arm. Only later, once that reading clicks, do we look back into the shadow and find the origin of it. The construction is impeccable.

## Four principles in one photograph

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L78 · 02:15–03:06

Beyond that, look at the sheer organisation of the spaces: this pentagon-like shape and the triangle at its base, and these two clean, empty areas — nothing here is surplus. Everything present in the image is essential. The four principles discussed today are all at work here: unity, since the elements are held together by their internal forces; proximity, and the organisation of the spaces. Weight has been very well handled here too, the elements very well judged. There's expression, in how this dramatic shape launches the arm out of the frame on the right. And finally, there's the visual movement just discussed.

## A knife's edge sets the whole table in motion

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L78 · 03:06–04:00

And finally, this other image, which also seems very interesting. To begin with, notice how all the interest is centred in the lower part of the image — how it contrasts, and how it carries the whole action down into the bottom third. That alone gives it tremendous weight. On top of that, this line here converges with the line the knife creates, running from the knife, passing through the glass, carrying that same movement, and ending here. A visual angle forms at this point, and that angle is what gives the image its energy from there. That's the interest in the whole construction Chema Madoz builds: it starts at the knife, runs through the glasses, and arrives here. It's an image that pulls all its weight down into the lower part, and that same downward weight and force is exactly what gives the movement on the left its charge.

## Visual principles belong to every discipline

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L78 · 04:00–05:25

As you can see, this is a genuinely important exercise, and I think using the principles of visual appreciation is an essential tool for anyone working with images in any form. It concerns a photographer, as just seen, just as much as it concerns a cook plating a dish, someone setting a table, or someone laying out a piece or a magazine page. It's something that matters across a great many disciplines that work with the image.

With that, this moves into the final phase: the project. There'll be a review within that final project, going back over everything discussed, and a summary — hopefully this has been of interest. It's also hoped that this introduction to the principles of visual appreciation has served as a starting point. If it has simply opened a door, over the hours spent together in this course, into a world that wasn't familiar before, and sparked an interest in exploring it further, then the course has more than justified itself. Until next time.

### Key points (derived)

- Chema Madoz's photographs are used as a closing case study because a single image of his can carry unity, weight, proximity and visual movement all at once.
- A strong visual path deliberately withholds its full meaning: the eye follows lines and angles before the substitution (branches as veins, a cord as a hand) becomes clear.
- Empty, "unfinished" space in a composition can be as load-bearing as the filled space — nothing in a well-built image is surplus.
- The principles covered across this unit (unity, weight, balance, expression, movement) are not specific to photography: they apply equally to plating food, setting a table, or laying out a printed page.
