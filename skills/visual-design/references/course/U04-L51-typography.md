## Introduction, and the letter as positive and negative form

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L51 · 00:00–00:56

Typography is, without question, a defining element of any area of visual communication design. This lesson explains some key concepts of typographic color and structure, as well as how a typeface family extends across variants, and closes by looking at how to combine typefaces with other elements in space.

A letter is a very particular kind of form for design. It's made of two parts we already know: the positive part and the negative part. Here the term counterform (*contraforma*) genuinely applies. A well-composed typeface balances these two factors — keep that in mind, since it's key to typographic composition.

## The baseline system and the purpose of x-height

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L51 · 01:14–01:37

Pay attention too to the line, or ruling (*renglón* or *pautado*): implicit lines running through every piece of typographic design, measuring its vertical proportions. These heights and depths serve other functions — text typefaces, for instance, need a wider, taller x-height so letters stay legible at small sizes.

## Counters, weight and letterspacing

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L51 · 01:37–02:27

The internal white of a letter defines much of its character: grow it and the letter lightens; shrink it and the letter turns heavy. These counters are tied to the black form and condition the letter's external space — the space connecting it to other signs. The smaller the internal space, the tighter the letterspacing needs to be; generous counters ask the space between characters to open up.

![5642 · U4 · L51 · 01:48](frames/U04L51-F0148.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: Three lowercase "a" letters in increasing weight, light to bold, each paired below with just its counter shape, showing the counter shrinking as the stroke thickens.

Notice the mismatch that can happen: at the top, with a heavier typeface, the internal spaces are much narrower while the letterspacing is far too wide; the same thing happens with the light version at the bottom. There are also spaces between words and between lines, and all of these are related to one another — but they can always be adjusted in search of different visual effects.

![5642 · U4 · L51 · 02:01](frames/U04L51-F0201.jpg)

On screen: "minimun" (repeated as a test word, four times in a thin weight and four times in bold).  
Shows: The nonsense word "minimun" set in two weights (thin and bold) at two sizes each, used to compare how counters and spacing read across weight and size.

Typographic color (*color tipográfico*) is the gray patch (*mancha gris*) perceived when looking at blocks of text. It's easy to see that letterspacing, word spacing and line spacing are all closely tied to this criterion. If these spaces are narrow, the block reads as a darker color; if they're wide, a lighter one. Typographic color works like a texture — an attribute of value for the text that differentiates categories.

Shows: An open magazine or book spread set almost entirely in small dense body text, used as an example of the gray textural patch that a block of typeset text creates on the page.

## Typographic variables: weight, construction and width

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L51 · 02:27–04:21

Typographic variables are alternative alphabets within one and the same family. They keep the structural bond intact, but solve problems of scale and reading level within a text, and also of hierarchy at the macro-typographic level. In principle, variables affect a letter along four axes.

In weight, stroke thickness varies — always make a real jump between steps in a gradation, so a regular reads clearly different from a bold, not just from a medium. In the construction axis — the letter's posture relative to the line — a well-constructed letter also changes its ductus (*ductus*, the path its strokes take) and structure. Width variables, also structural, alter the letter horizontally and change how much space the same text takes up.

![5642 · U4 · L51 · 03:14](frames/U04L51-F0314.jpg)

On screen: "Peso" (Weight), "Postura" (Posture), "Ancho de caja" (Width), "Tamaños ópticos" (Optical sizes).  
Shows: The word "bag" repeated in four labelled rows: a weight gradation from light to bold, an upright-versus-italic posture pair, a set of widths from condensed to expanded, and a row resetting the same word across several optical sizes.

Lesser-known variables are optical ones: fine adjustments that keep a face attractive at different sizes, or make certain sizes simply work better. For example, an optical display variant, built for large sizes, carries a lot of fine detail; a variant from the same family for very small sizes, made for captions or footnotes, changes its contrasts and proportions to optimize that use instead. Using one variable doesn't exclude the others — they can combine — though doing so is usually redundant.

## Classifying typeface styles

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L51 · 04:21–05:01

All the fundamental ingredients of typography dress themselves in different styles, which is how families get classified. But the real difference, beyond whatever criterion sorts the styles, isn't just whether a face has serifs, or whether a script's construction resembles a slab serif (*egipcia*) or a mechanical-style face (*mecana*). It lies instead in subtler differences: the modulation of the strokes, the axis of contrast, or the terminal shapes of its signs.

## Typographic hierarchy

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L51 · 05:01–05:55

Typographic architecture (*arquitectura tipográfica*) expresses the organization of content by emphasizing some elements and subordinating others. It helps the reader scan a text — knowing where to go in and where to come out, but also how to choose between what's on offer. Content can be organized by differentiating it with size, and also by giving certain forms a bit of delight, making them more visible: the most important thing can stand out with a larger size, and the secondary thing with a smaller one.

Shows: Three lines of "Haesg" set in serif on the left, and two lines set in a heavier sans on the right, at contrasting sizes — a plain demonstration of using size and weight to build hierarchy.

Just as any form near the top of a composition finds it easier to convey a certain hierarchy, weight can also take on that job.

## A simple hierarchy from one type family

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L51 · 05:55–06:24

Even so, book design offers a simple method for building hierarchy: handling a complex system with just the limited resources of one typeface family. Whether you use a modest family or an extensive system, always clarify the text with restraint, without piling on too many variables — differences build up little by little, and there's no need to throw every possibility in at the first opportunity.

## Guidelines for combining typefaces

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L51 · 06:24–07:56

Combining typefaces is a major dilemma, but there are a few guidelines to follow to come out of it as well as possible.

First, decide the job the text you're designing needs to do: will it be used to stand out in the design, or to explain something in more detail? Typefaces meant for large sizes grab attention with their shapes and generally set short passages — not the best choice for reading a lot of text. Long-form typefaces, by contrast, have no formal loudness and stay fairly neutral, precisely so you can focus on the content.

If you want to use two or three typefaces, make them easy to tell apart — don't combine typefaces of the same style. Generally, more than two different families won't be necessary, though sometimes they are.

![5642 · U4 · L51 · 07:06](frames/U04L51-F0706.jpg)

On screen: "Título" (Title, repeated as the sample headline); body copy is placeholder text, not real words.  
Shows: Two similar-looking serif display typefaces set side by side under the same heading and body-text layout — an example of two faces that are too alike to combine well.

When combining faces on the same line, the main thing is to match their x-heights: this makes them easier to relate to each other, and lets their other differences read more clearly.

![5642 · U4 · L51 · 07:28](frames/U04L51-F0728.jpg)

On screen: "benguiat" (set four times); labels "altura de x" (x-height), "Arno 92pt", "Kepler 92pt", "Alright 90 pt", "Alright 66 pt".  
Shows: The same word set across three typefaces — Arno, Kepler and Alright (the last at two sizes, giving four samples) — the point size of each one adjusted until their x-heights line up on the same pair of guides.

As mentioned, it's good practice to combine styles — a sans and a serif, for example. Look for signs whose skeleton is similar: an old-style serif face, for instance, with short ascenders and elliptical bowls. Find a point in common so the difference stands out. Whether by linking the forms or by deliberately disagreeing — always on a regular baseline — look for a way to create a pleasing contrast.

## A practical exercise: choosing typefaces for the magazine project

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L51 · 07:56–11:18

For the design of this magazine, several typefaces were tried and combined to find just the right degree of relationship between them. First, since it will appear the most throughout the magazine, came the text typeface: a family with a fairly generous x-height, reasonably wide overall, with open horizontal proportions and a very clear, well-marked rhythm. That typeface, Degular, is very well resolved and looks well suited to those purposes. From there, the search moves on to alternatives that could work alongside it.

This magazine deals with a shift in the human paradigm — a contemporary look at humanity's situation and how a change of direction is needed, toward something softer and less competitive than today's society. So the idea is for the typography — especially the larger display face, seen most quickly — to communicate a bit of that, while contrasting well with the text typeface's characteristics.

![5642 · U4 · L51 · 08:10](frames/U04L51-F0810.jpg)

On screen: "Kansas New" / "New Spirit" / "Degular" / "Quiche" (family names, each shown above a sample block set in placeholder text).  
Shows: An InDesign spread laid out as four panels, each combining Degular's body-text sample with a different display candidate — Kansas New, New Spirit, Degular alone, and Quiche.

First, the shapes of the typefaces are lined up together: Degular's characteristics are compared against Kansas.

![5642 · U4 · L51 · 09:13](frames/U04L51-F0913.jpg)

On screen: "Hamburg" (a classic type-specimen word, set bold above regular).  
Shows: A close-up crop of the Kansas New panel, showing the word "Hamburg" set bold and then regular, enlarged to compare the letterforms directly.

They share quite a few similarities, but Kansas has a softer color and shape, contrasting nicely with the text typeface's rigidity. Degular alone could also work — its forms feel organic — but on its own it still reads as too mechanical; something more sensitive is needed, and that sensitivity could come from contrast, which produces something like an emotional effect. A strongly marked contrast in the page's negative space is appealing for that reason, but it drifts toward fashion and becomes very wide and very geometric.

Another alternative, quite similar to Kansas, is called New Spirit — appealing for meeting the same conditions, but differing in a few respects. Look, for example, at the back — the slanted stem — of the A: that gives the letter another kind of dynamism, versatility and speed.

![5642 · U4 · L51 · 10:40](frames/U04L51-F1040.jpg)

On screen: "amburg" (word cropped at the frame edge, set at large size).  
Shows: A cropped New Spirit specimen, "Hamburg" repeated in four weights and styles at large size — the closest available view of the letterforms the teacher points to, though the frame does not isolate the A's stem in its own close-up.

These subtleties help it feel more dynamic and contrast better. It also has a condensed variable, among others, that could suit a highlighted phrase at these sizes, adding more typographic richness. With these alternatives on the table, it's time to decide which direction to take the project's typography.

### Key points (derived)

- A letterform is built from positive and negative space together; the negative space (the counterform) is as much a design decision as the black shape itself.
- Letters have named parts (crossbar, bowl, ear, neck, waist, arch, shoulder, tail, stem…), and a shared vertical guide system (ascender, cap-height, x-height, baseline, descender) — knowing both helps you compare and describe typefaces precisely.
- Counter size, weight and letterspacing all move together: bigger counters read lighter and want looser spacing; smaller counters read heavier and want tighter spacing. Mismatching them looks wrong even when each looks fine alone.
- Typographic color is the gray value a text block reads as from a distance, driven by letter, word and line spacing rather than by the letterforms themselves.
- A family's variables move along four axes — weight, construction/posture, width, and (less commonly) optical size — and can combine, though combining too many at once is redundant.
- Hierarchy can come from size, weight, or both, using either one family's own built-in range or a size/weight ladder within it — no need to use every option at once.
- When combining typefaces, match x-heights, keep them easy to tell apart, and prefer contrasting styles (e.g., serif with sans) that still share a similar underlying skeleton.
