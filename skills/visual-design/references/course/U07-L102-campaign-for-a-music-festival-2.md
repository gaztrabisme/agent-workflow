## Setting the performer's name in Knockout, with colour rules

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L102 · 02:08–03:13

Following the scheme already worked out for the festival's general image and its lettering, the same approach applies to each performer banner: the same play with Knockout's different weights, with coloured rules (*filetes*) added — rules that later get carried over to the main image too.

![5642 · U7 · L102 · 02:27](frames/U07L102-F0227.jpg)

On screen: "VÍCTOR BOCANEGRA"  
Shows: The reframed portrait above two blue rules, with the performer's name set in two weights of Knockout between them, underneath.

A small problem shows up here: an accent can collide with these rules. It isn't only rules that cause this — tightening the leading as recommended for these typefaces can also make characters collide with each other. The fix, without any qualms, is simply to redraw the accent, or whatever element is colliding, so it fits the piece properly (the lesson's typography recap, near the end, shows this exact fix). This is a traditional graphic-design habit worth keeping: capitals do take accents in Spanish, and in Portuguese too — the excuse that they don't is not a reason to drop them.

## A first complete proposal across the whole series

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L102 · 03:59–05:07

If this system feels a bit flat on its own, colour accents can be added, both to the main banner and to each performer, and applied across the whole series of acts — making the system feel more genuine through how the typography, colour and photographs are handled together. ## Variation 2: cut-out photography and a retro wood-type look

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L102 · 05:07–07:33

Keeping the logos lined up at the bottom, this variation uses slightly thicker rules and treats the photographs differently: instead of converting them to black and white and reframing them, the photographs are cut out instead. Cutting out the photograph, and giving it a bit of texture, sets up a play that lets the performer's photo be coloured — closer to how this kind of poster was traditionally made, with wood type alongside metal type and cut-out figures, for a slightly retro look; notice the use of rules here too.

![5642 · U7 · L102 · 06:00](frames/U07L102-F0600.jpg)

On screen: "NO RADIO ... 'THE FIGHT' ... NO HOME TV / MADISON SQ. GARDEN / MON. MARCH 8TH / 15 ROUNDS FOR THE HEAVYWEIGHT CHAMPIONSHIP OF THE WORLD / JOE FRAZIER vs. MUHAMMAD ALI / PRICES: RINGSIDE & LOGE $150.00 ... [rest omitted]"  
Shows: A vintage boxing poster combining wood type, metal type and cut-out fighter photographs — the historical reference for this variation's look.

This same play continues with Knockout's different weights, but now set vertically: it still reads perfectly well, at a very large size. This isn't text meant to be read up close — it's meant to be seen and read very fast on a street banner, not sat down and studied — so the vertical treatment can be combined with horizontal type. A touch of colour on the main banner balances it against the others, otherwise the type would stay dark grey throughout; half-rules close off this section, and the small text is set in an ordinary typeface rather than Knockout, since Knockout doesn't work at small sizes.

## Recap: colour, images and typography for a wood-type system

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L102 · 09:03–12:04

As before, the lesson closes with a review of the most important points, given here as three on-screen panels alongside a live example of each.

For colour: outdoor communication needs contrast — the original example clearly had too little of it. Working in black, or another dark colour, on white clearly gains far more contrast and makes a piece much more visible outdoors. The colour of the support itself can be used as the background, an interesting strategy worth considering even when it isn't white — with coloured paper or other materials, that's sometimes a better choice than painting on a coloured background. And for logo placement: where possible, use the monochrome versions of any logos that must sit together, so they coexist far better.

Shows: The blue and white versions of the "CASTELO MUSIC FESTIVAL" name side by side, illustrating the contrast point live.

![5642 · U7 · L102 · 09:53](frames/U07L102-F0953.jpg)

On screen: "Color / Para comunicación exterior, busca el contraste. / Utilizar el color del soporte como color de fondo es una buena opción. / Si puedes, usa las versiones monocromas de las imágenes corporativas que tengan que convivir." (Colour / For outdoor communication, look for contrast. / Using the support's own colour as the background colour is a good option. / If you can, use monochrome versions of the corporate images that have to coexist.)  
Shows: The colour recap panel, with the four sponsor marks in monochrome shown underneath as the live example.

For images: don't use generic images that compete with the specific cases at hand — the festival's original saxophone symbol, besides being a cliché, didn't make much sense and fought with the other images. Unify criteria so the images can coexist: they can't be used exactly as supplied. Reframe them if that's possible, change their colour if that's possible; in any case, fitting them into a system means reducing them to a common minimum denominator, one option among others — cutting them out, as seen, is another way of unifying the criteria. On sponsor-logo sizes: equalise them optically, not by matching a horizontal or vertical measurement, but by checking how they read together as the same size.

![5642 · U7 · L102 · 10:48](frames/U07L102-F1048.jpg)

On screen: "Imágenes / No utilices imágenes genéricas que compitan con los casos particulares. / Unifica criterios para hacer que las imágenes convivan. / Reducir al mínimo común es una buena opción. / Iguala los tamaños de imágenes corporativas de forma relativa a su lectura, no a su geometría." (Images / Don't use generic images that compete with the specific cases. / Unify criteria so the images coexist. / Reducing to a common minimum is a good option. / Match corporate image sizes relative to how they read, not to their geometry.)  
Shows: The images recap panel, with the four sponsor marks shown at their optically balanced sizes underneath.

And finally on typography, specifically wood type: to work well it needs large sizes — it doesn't work at small sizes — and the leading needs pulling in, since the automatic leading most typography programs default to leaves far too much space; body can be set right on body, even at negative leading. These faces work well when weights are combined and played with, as with a family like Knockout that has different weights and widths, and they tend to work best set in capitals for this kind of typographic play. Lastly, still on wood type: letters — especially accents and other elements that rise or drop — need retouching so they don't collide; there's nothing wrong with custom-drawing an accent, or any other element that would otherwise be distorted.

![5642 · U7 · L102 · 11:51](frames/U07L102-F1151.jpg)

On screen: "Tipografía / Utiliza los tipos de madera tal como se utilizaban en su orígen [sic]: / Para cuerpos grandes. / Con menos interlínea. / Combinando grosores. / Peferentemente [sic] en mayúsculas. / Retoca letras si es necesario." (Typography / Use wood types the way they were used originally: / For large sizes. / With less leading. / Combining weights. / Preferably in capitals. / Retouch letters if necessary.); "NELSQN CORAÇÃO"; "NELSON CORAÇÃO"

### Key points (derived)

- When source photographs vary wildly in quality, decide on one unifying treatment (greyscale, reframing to a shared crop, or cutting out) rather than presenting each photo as supplied — a single weak photo drags down the whole set.
- A rule-and-Knockout system (coloured rules separating name, date and logos) can carry from a single performer banner to the main poster, giving a consistent frame even while individual colours vary.
- Tight or negative leading on wood-type-style faces can make accents or descenders collide with rules or with each other; redrawing the offending element by hand is normal practice, not a compromise — and capitals still take their accents.
- A coherent graphic language (consistent type, rule, and colour system) can carry a festival's identity across many pieces without needing a single festival logo — photography still leads, but the system unifies it.
- The same brief can be answered with genuinely different proposals: unified grayscale photography with colour accents; cut-out, colour-tinted photography with a retro wood/metal-type feel; or hand-sourced textured lettering standing in for photography entirely, useful when a usable photo is missing or unwanted.
- Wood type needs large sizes, tight leading, and weight-mixing to work as intended, and reads best in capitals; a plain sans (here, the geometric Google Fonts face Just) should carry the small supporting text instead.
