## Lesson introduction

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 00:00–00:27

In this lesson we'll talk about iconicity (*iconicidad*): the different levels of graphic representation available to us for visualizing shapes or figures, going from more literal places to more abstract ones, always keeping in mind what we want to convey.

## Defining iconicity: appearance versus referent

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 00:28–00:50

Talking about iconicity, or levels of iconicity, we're talking about the relationship of appearances between the image itself and its referent. We're talking about how much information we can strip away from, or re-signify in, the original image, until we bring it to its minimal expression, or generate a visual code of its own that represents it.

## Three degrees: realism and iconicity

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 00:51–01:12

On this scale we'll have three levels that indicate how represented the original image is: the more realistic, the more iconicity; the more abstract it appears, the less iconic it is.

## Level 11: the natural image

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 01:13–01:33

We can then talk about 11 levels of iconicity, level 11 being the natural image — that is, the image itself, which carries every property that appears in the design, and the highest level of information. If we were standing in Egypt, next to the pyramids, we'd be at iconicity level 11.

## Level 10: the three-dimensional model

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 01:34–01:56

The next level, number 10, is the scale model or true-size 3D model. These designs are practically identical representations of the original; they can be actual size, or a larger or smaller scale of it. The design can easily be identified with the original, but it is not the original image.

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 01:57–02:17

At level 9 we find holograms, which can be made in either black and white or color. These are images that reproduce the shape and situation of the objects they represent in another time and space — above all, space.

At level 7 we find black-and-white photography, which has the same characteristics as color photography, except the image appears represented in black, white and grayscale. That is, from the previous level to this one, we lose the color data, but we're still looking at the same image.

## Level 6: realistic painting

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 02:51–03:10

At level 6 we find realistic painting: painting that represents the original image on canvas with a very high degree of fidelity, and in which the two-dimensional spatial relationships show a reasonably faithful match to the real image.

## Level 5: non-realistic figurative representation

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 03:11–03:33

At level 5 we find non-realistic figurative representation. Here the original images can still be perceived and identified, but their similarity to the original is fairly low, and the points that make up each of its elements can sit in a different place than in the original.

## Level 4: pictograms

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 03:34–03:53

At level 4 we find pictograms. Here, the design being evaluated carries every characteristic of the original image, but represented abstractly, except for its shape — which is what lets us identify what appears in it.

## Level 3: motivated schemas

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 03:55–04:28

At level 3 we find motivated schemas — that is, designs whose characteristics are all abstracted from the original. Their only similarity to the original is that they keep a certain organic relationship with it. For example, in map or plan design, we see the relationships, proportions and distances visually represented, but they may not be the real proportions or the real distances.

![5642 · U6 · L80 · 04:24](frames/U06L80-F0424.jpg)

## Level 2: arbitrary schemas

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 04:29–04:55

Reaching level 2, we find arbitrary schemas: designs generated in a completely arbitrary way, showing no perceptible characteristics in common with the original. As for the relationships between the elements that appear in the design, they follow no logic — as happens, for example, in many traffic signs.

## Level 1: non-figurative representation

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 04:57–05:17

And reaching the last level of this iconicity table, we find non-figurative representation. As its name indicates, its properties are entirely abstracted — both the perceptible properties and those that relate its elements to one another.

## A simplified three-level model

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 05:18–06:02

For a more basic and general understanding of the scales, or levels, of iconicity, some theorists establish only three fundamental levels: the representative, where the receiver's direct perception is set from a low level of coding; the symbolic, where the image's representation becomes simpler; and the abstract, where the image directly reduces itself to its most basic visual components and the level of coding is very high. Naturally, between one level and another, various degrees of iconicity occur.

![5642 · U6 · L80 · 05:24](frames/U06L80-F0524.jpg)

Shows: Left, a text panel listing the three-level model (Representative, Symbolic, Abstract); right, Picasso's eleven-image series "The Bull" (*El toro*), progressively simplifying a bull from a detailed drawing down to a single continuous outline.

## Picasso's bull: finding the minimal unit

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L80 · 06:03–06:39

It's interesting to look at this exercise by Picasso, which doesn't strictly relate to the levels of iconicity, but which I want to bring in as part of this course's visual culture: a drawing exercise of a bull, which keeps losing detail and losing this sense of realism until it reaches its minimal unit. What is the minimal unit for representing a bull? That's how we move across the different levels of iconicity, and now we're ready to experiment with them.

### Key points (derived)

- Iconicity measures the relationship of appearance between an image and its referent: more realistic means more iconic, more abstract means less iconic.
- The scale runs 11 levels, from the natural image (11) down to non-figurative representation (1): natural image, 3D model, hologram, color photo, B&W photo, realistic painting, non-realistic figurative representation, pictogram, motivated schemas, arbitrary schemas, non-figurative representation.
- Motivated schemas (maps) keep an organic relationship with the original without matching its real proportions; arbitrary schemas (like traffic signs) have no logical relationship with it at all.
- A simplified model collapses this into three levels: representative, symbolic, abstract — with many in-between degrees.
- Picasso's eleven-image drawing exercise "The Bull" demonstrates the search for an image's minimal unit, stripping a realistic bull down to a single continuous line.
- The course will lean toward more iconic images going forward, aiming for stronger, more well-defined symbologies.
