## The brief: an image that must share space

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L101 · 00:00–00:47

This is an image made of images: a festival where different artists from different genres will perform. It's an image that has to compete in an urban environment, so the logo or imagotype has to be fairly prominent, since people will mostly be looking at the show itself. On top of that, the identity has to support and boost another image: that of the sponsors. And as if that weren't enough, the piece has to read both close up and from a distance, and it has to adapt to different supports (surfaces and formats). This lesson works through the identity of a music festival.

## The base poster and the banderola system

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L101 · 00:47–02:40

A music festival's image raises a fairly common problem: how far the festival's own identity should dominate, sit above, or instead be coordinated with the separate adverts for each act on the bill.

Take this as the main image for a music festival: it carries a symbol — fairly obvious, and the lesson comes back to it — with the lettering set dynamically at an angle, and at the bottom, the sponsors' logos, both public and private. That's very common, and worth remembering. Alongside this poster sits a street banner (*banderola*); a mock-up of it in place comes later.

What gives the festival unity here isn't so much the musician placed on that first banner, but the colour and the typeface: each piece individually carries its own photograph, and that photograph doesn't come from the festival — it comes from the musician, the promoter, or whoever supplies it. ## Questioning the festival's own symbol and its type

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L101 · 02:40–03:59

The lesson starts by questioning the festival's own mark. It doesn't work well to have a mark that competes with, or gives away in advance, what visitors will actually find at the festival — either because that kind of musician isn't on the bill, or because the mark ends up competing with what is. On top of that, the saxophone is a cliché that doesn't really tell us anything, or that might tell us something slightly wrong: a saxophone brings jazz to mind, and this festival isn't only about jazz — it covers other genres too. So this is a symbol worth dropping as the general image, especially since it isn't even being applied across every format.

![5642 · U7 · L101 · 03:21](frames/U07L101-F0321.jpg)

On screen: "Castelo MUSIC"; "GERTRUDIS"  
Shows: The saxophone silhouette poster crop next to the Gertrudis banner.

The other problem is the lettering itself: it mixes forms inconsistently, sometimes capitals, sometimes lower case ("Castelo" next to "MUSIC FESTIVAL" in the poster already shown). That should be fixed — one or the other, not both. Settling on capitals, one option is to also line the pieces up on a more ordered, non-slanted grid.

## Choosing a typeface: from wood type to Knockout

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L101 · 03:59–07:48

The letter used so far is Helvetica Neue Condensed (captions: *albética noye*) — a cut of Helvetica that barely looks like Helvetica any more, and doesn't keep much of the spirit seen in Helvetica's other uses. The original intent was probably closer to what's normally done with what are known as wood types (*tipos de madera*).

![5642 · U7 · L101 · 04:25](frames/U07L101-F0425.jpg)

On screen: "Helvetica Neue Condensed / GREAT MUSIC TONIGHT" and, below, "Imapct [sic] / GREAT MUSIC TONIGHT"  
Shows: The same phrase set in Helvetica Neue Condensed above, and in a bold display face labelled with the on-screen typo "Imapct" below.

What are wood types? They're the letterforms that appeared in the late 18th or 19th century, once printing was becoming industrialised — no longer just making books, but also things meant to be read from a distance, like posters and gazettes. Larger letters started to appear, and they're called wood types because they were bigger than traditional metal type, and because — as the photograph shows — they began to be cut in wood: cheaper than metal, and lighter, easier to handle. That's when sans-serif letters (*tipos de palo*), Egyptian (slab-serif) letters, and other display styles start appearing in print — styles that still give us a look we keep in typefaces today, Impact among them. Impact imitates, in its way, those old wood types, even though it's a 20th-century letter. Many condensed letters in use today are recreations of those period styles under different commercial names, less famous than Impact — for example Gill Sans, or one the captions render as *el Bético* (unclear; the English captions give "Betis" — likely a mistranscribed type name).

![5642 · U7 · L101 · 05:48](frames/U07L101-F0548.jpg)

The typeface chosen for this campaign, seen already in two examples, is Knockout — flagged in an earlier lesson as one to come back to. It appears in these Rolling Stone cover titles, and in some of the lettering on the poster on the right, by Paula Scher (captions: *Paul Asher*), combined there with other letters in the same style.

![5642 · U7 · L101 · 06:12](frames/U07L101-F0612.jpg)

On screen: "Rolling Stone", "Britney Spears", "Inside an American Tragedy" [rest omitted]; "THE PUBLIC THEATER", "BRING IN 'DA NOISE," "BRING IN 'DA FUNK" [rest omitted]  
Shows: A Rolling Stone cover set in a bold condensed display face, next to a densely packed yellow theatre poster using mixed weights and widths of a related style.

This style of sans-serif letters is meant to work mixed together, which is really how they were used originally — hence Knockout. Although it's a wood-type letter, Knockout has been developed systematically, like a modern typeface, in the way Frutiger and Univers later organised a whole type family by weight and width, giving a very wide range to draw from. That wasn't the case originally: in the wood-type era, sizes were only sold at fixed cuts, without the range available now. A revival like Knockout, made by Hoefler, is an excellent letter well suited to this piece.

![5642 · U7 · L101 · 06:51](frames/U07L101-F0651.jpg)

On screen: "Knockout"; "Junior"; "Regular"; "Full"  
Shows: Five rows of the Knockout family increasing in weight and width, columns labelled Junior, Regular and Full.

Carried across to wood type's own logic, these weights and widths can be combined with each other, keeping something of the spirit the original letters were made with. One thing to watch when using this kind of letter: give it less leading (line spacing) — even negative leading. Wood type filled the whole body height of the block, so lines were set close together, without the gap seen in ordinary text setting. Here the leading is tightened almost to the same distance used for the space between words. This example has no multi-word phrases yet — how the spacing works with those will be seen later — but in principle this tighter setting works better.

Another fix, and one of the flaws in the very first version shown, is gaining more contrast: black on blue doesn't work well. There's also an interesting move here — using the colour of the support itself. Since the main application is canvas banners, and the canvas is white, using that background colour is a good idea: it gains contrast, and colour is added back in later.

## Unifying and balancing the sponsor logos

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L101 · 08:57–10:26

A detail worth watching is how the sponsor marks along the bottom are placed. Where possible, they should be unified by choosing each one's monochrome version — almost every mark available here has one — so the marks stop fighting each other, which also reads better in the long run.

Where it's allowed (not always possible), it also helps to compensate the marks' sizes. Giving every mark the same width looks fair, and no one could complain, but in practice they clearly don't work together: a set width doesn't mean a mark reads as bigger or smaller, because every mark is built differently. What's actually needed is an optical adjustment for each one individually.

![5642 · U7 · L101 · 10:18](frames/U07L101-F1018.jpg)

On screen: [rest omitted]; sponsor marks "Câmara Municipal de Castelo" (Castelo City Council), "Câmara de Comércio e Indústria" (Chamber of Commerce and Industry), "união dos bancos nacionais" (national banks union), "NEWSTAR"

These optically balanced marks can also be applied to the other banners that didn't have them, strengthening this part of the system: the earlier banners already switched to a white ground gain a good deal from it too. That closes the question of the marks — next comes the photography that arrives from outside.

## Applying the system to Toni Ricart's portraits

Anchor: 5642 · U7 · L101 · 10:26–12:06

As already noted, these images — as happens at many such festivals — aren't generated by the festival itself: they come from the promoter, from the musician, and so on, whoever supplies that personal image. They're elements the designer doesn't control but has to include, because that photo of the musician or performer is what draws attention.

The three images used here have different origins. One worth starting with is the work of a photographer, Toni Ricart (transcript: *Tony Ricart*) — himself also a graphic designer — whose portrait collection, in this case as a photographer, covers people from his own circle across different fields: professionals, artists, and others.

![5642 · U7 · L101 · 11:09](frames/U07L101-F1109.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: Two of Toni Ricart's portraits used on the banners — a man seated at a piano, and the three members of Gertrudis on a pink ground.

That collection is interesting because it's a kind of portrait where the photographer interacts gently, so to speak, with the sitter: no costume, no posed "working" shot, just someone doing something. The hands play an important role throughout, alongside the face and the gesture; the sitters are the artist or the person themselves — writers, scientists, whoever they are — but they're somehow interacting with the photographer, giving the series a very distinctive quality.

### Key points (derived)

- A festival identity has to work at both close and far range, adapt to many supports, and support (not fight) sponsor logos and the acts' own promotional images.
- Question a mark that competes with or misrepresents the content it identifies — a jazz saxophone icon for a mixed-genre festival is a cliché that also isn't applied consistently across supports.
- Fix inconsistent capitalisation before anything else; a switch to a plain, orderly grid can fix consistency but still read as bland — that's a cue to look at the typeface itself, not just its arrangement.
- Wood type (19th-century oversized letters, first cut in wood for economy and weight) is the ancestor of many display sans faces still used today (Impact, Gill Sans, and systematic revivals like Knockout); it was set with tight or negative leading because the letters filled their full body height.
- A systematic type family (varying weight and width, as with Knockout, Frutiger or Univers) lets faces be mixed the way wood type originally was, while giving far more range than the original fixed-size wood fonts ever had.
- Gaining contrast can be as simple as printing dark type directly on the light colour of the support itself (white canvas) rather than fighting it with a background colour.
- Unify sponsor logos to their monochrome versions so they stop competing, then adjust each one's size optically rather than to a common width — equal width is not equal visual weight.
- When photography is supplied by outside parties (musicians, promoters) rather than commissioned, unity has to come from the system around it — colour, type, layout — not from the photography itself.
