# Lesson 17 · Color palettes

## Choose colors beyond literal realism

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L17 · 00:00–01:19

After covering primary and secondary colors and color modes, the lesson turns to palettes (*paletas de color*) that can make a design feel consistent and attractive. As with typography, a color choice can be too obvious: nature does not have to be represented with greens and browns. The designer can play with the elements instead of matching every object’s real-world color.

Historically, art often used colors faithfully, and beginners are commonly told to color things as they appear in nature. Surrealists opened another possibility: Man Ray’s painted blue bread is one example. The teacher invites viewers to imagine a blue tree or an orange sky and see what those choices might do.

## Monochromatic palettes in painting and film

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L17 · 01:19–02:36

A monochromatic palette (*paleta monocromática*) starts with one color and varies its shades, making them lighter or darker while staying with the same color family. The lesson refers to paintings built from one blue and to grayscale palettes, which still use many light and dark values. Sports brands use that black-to-white range; the Nike poster is one example. A monochromatic palette can also vary the hue, as in examples that shift through purple or blue.

Film often uses a dominant palette to create an atmosphere. *Moonrise Kingdom* revolves mainly around yellow or ochre, even though it includes other shades. Another film is *Her* [captions: “Ager”], where red creates a similar surrounding atmosphere.

## Use complementary colors selectively

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L17 · 02:36–03:30

Complementary colors (*colores complementarios*) sit opposite each other on the color wheel. Palettes built from opposites can be striking and attractive, so the teacher advises using them sparingly. They can highlight an element on a poster or, for example, make buttons stand out on a one-color web page. Complementaries also appear in children’s books and illustrations. The teacher points to a famous poster by Fredun Shapur [she says “Fredon”] and notes that complementary palettes are common in contemporary design.

## Analogous colors and collected palettes

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L17 · 03:30–05:28

An analogous palette (*paleta de colores análogos*) uses colors next to each other on the wheel. Choose a dominant color, then use its neighbors for contrast. Because the shades are close, the contrast is not strong; the result can have a calm, consistent atmosphere. The teacher points to landscapes by Monet as an art-history example.

She also shows a designer who collects monochromatic blue palettes from skies found in magazines, newspapers, the wider world, and on travels. The resulting collection includes many shades of blue from places such as Budapest, St. Petersburg, and Santander. Collecting color this way is an exercise in looking carefully and understanding how many different blues exist.

## Read the palettes around you

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L17 · 05:28–06:52

For the exercise, observe the palettes in five familiar things: a favorite film or cartoon, clothes in the wardrobe, a favorite dessert, the place where you live, and the cover of a favorite album. The teacher’s examples show how different the results can be. *Monsters, Inc.* uses an analogous set of blues, purples, and greens. Her wardrobe uses the whole color wheel rather than a monochromatic, analogous, or complementary scheme. Cheesecake gives a nearly monochromatic palette of browns in lighter and darker shades. The La Latina neighborhood palette is analogous, moving through nearby ochres, oranges, and some greens. Her favorite album cover pairs blue and gray with accents of orange, a complementary relationship.

Record the colors by painting, cutting out pieces, or making rectangles on a computer; the method does not matter as much as paying close attention and saving what you see. Some apps can identify colors for you, but the teacher asks viewers not to use them for this exercise. When finished, classify the palettes as analogous, monochromatic, or complementary. The next lesson applies color choices to the Instagram posts.

### Key points (derived)

- Monochromatic, complementary, and analogous schemes offer different ways to organize a palette.
- A palette can depart from an object’s literal colors while still creating a coherent atmosphere.
- Build color awareness by collecting and classifying palettes from familiar parts of daily life.
