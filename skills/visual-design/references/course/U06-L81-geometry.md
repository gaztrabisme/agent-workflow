## Defining geometry

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L81 · 00:26–00:50

If we talk about geometry, we can say it studies the properties of figures in the plane or in space. It's one of the oldest sciences, and since ancient times it has formed a body of practical knowledge about the lengths, areas and volumes of every figure around us.

## Geometry offers a process, not a concept

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L81 · 01:44–02:13

Geometric organization doesn't, by itself, generate a concept or spark inspiration. But what the creative concept does offer is a compositional process: a means of linking and interrelating shapes, and a method for reaching a certain visual balance — a system for relating elements so they form a fairly coherent whole.

## Geometry as organizer of design

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L81 · 02:14–02:38

In design, geometry works as the organizer of the elements we're going to use, taken naturally as an essential function in how they're laid out on the plane. It orders things, and above all acts as an effective structure in space, managing to convey, through its proportions, a fitting aesthetic function.

## The circle: mother of all forms

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L81 · 03:50–04:03

The circle is considered the mother of all forms — literally, metaphorically and mathematically speaking. It's the archetypal form from which the other forms are drawn. The circle is a single cell, and in nature it's the basic component of living beings — that is, the shape of the Earth, the shape of a planet, the shape of the eye. The circle has been the basis of several symbols, and having no beginning or end, no interruptions or restrictions, it represents the endless cycle of life, the universe that surrounds everything.

## Geometry in signage: three functions

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L81 · 06:31–07:06

And finally, to close out this lesson, we're going to dive fully into the use of geometry in communication and in getting messages across. In signage, the use and the different functions of each of these figures is very noticeable. The square, generally blue, serves to convey informative content. The circle, generally white or red, lets us convey regulatory signals — that is, what can and can't be done. And finally, the triangle, generally yellow, puts us on alert.

### Key points (derived)

- Geometry studies the properties of figures in the plane or space, and appears both in human-made design and in the proportions of natural forms (fish, butterflies, murmurations).
- Geometric organization doesn't create a concept on its own, but it supplies a compositional process: a system for relating elements into a coherent, balanced whole.
- Several art and design movements built vital use of geometry into their work: Art Deco, Cubism, Futurism, Op Art, and typography, whose letterforms are built from the square, circle and triangle.
- The circle: mother of all forms, single-cell archetype, represents infinity, perfection, unity, wholeness, and the endless cycle of life.
- The square: order, resistance, stability, strength — but tipped onto a vertex (a diamond), it instead signals temporariness and uncertainty.
- The triangle: the number 3 and the Trinity, the sacred and divine, the elements and the sexes; point-up it is sun/fire/masculine, inverted it is moon/water/feminine; in ancient Egypt a symbol of divinity, and in literature and Dante's Inferno the shape of hell as an inverted, nine-level cone.
- In signage, the square (blue) is informative, the circle (white or red) is regulatory, and the triangle (yellow) is a warning.
- The course will use these three shapes as a creative method: square/reason (the informative), circle/emotion (the regulatory), triangle/attention (personal vision and periphery) — equalized differently at each stage of a project.
