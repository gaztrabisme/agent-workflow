## Communication needs a core message

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L46 · 00:00–01:20

Now I'm going to tell you what criteria you have to consider to judge your composition as effective. Also, what considerations to keep in mind to evaluate your layout, and how to look at other compositions to draw inspiration from. Let's see what it's about.

The task of graphic design is to communicate. Whether it's affecting a piece of knowledge, transforming attitudes, or modifying people's behaviors, a communication comes to exist because someone wants to transform an existing reality into a different, desired reality. For good communication, it's imperative to focus a design project on those ideas and concepts that sum up the core of the message, its DNA. This leitmotif has to be present with different visual characteristics. Understanding the communicative intention or concept means selecting the traits that give the message its identity. It is the content that dictates the look we should design.

## Clarity: precision and simplicity

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L46 · 01:40–02:56

One of the key goals of a composition is to clarify the message. The structure we design has to give access to the main ideas without confusion. It works like a filter that settles what matters most and places it prominently. At the same time, it keeps the structure from being ambiguous. A composition is precise when, without a doubt, its elements are or are not centered, when its lines are or are not orthogonal, or when we can tell clear differences between the size of its elements. When a composition is hesitant, it becomes insecure, and we can't work out what the scheme is trying to tell us. Clarity has to do with the simplicity of the structure. To process all the information it perceives, the human brain needs to discriminate it. So as not to feel overwhelmed, we look for simplicity, we group things, we reduce what we see to the simplest or easiest possibilities. A composition that simplifies the visual message is effective because it makes it clearer and more accessible.

## Balance: compensating perceptual tension

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L46 · 02:57–04:35

Our way of standing up, of moving, of keeping our balance, are important factors for how we receive and interpret the visual environment. We see through a movement from top to bottom and from left to right. Onto what we see, we impose imaginary axes to adjust the balance, and a structural map to understand its compositional elements. Visual experience is dynamic, not static. In every medium or graphic support, we perceive tensions between the forms that make it up. We judge these perceptual forces by the criterion of balance (*equilibrio*) — but not a physical balance, a visual one. Balance is the compensation of perceptual forces. There are factors like size, color, or direction that contribute to visual balance, and that have nothing to do with arranging objects on a vertical or horizontal axis. Much less does it require doing it symmetrically. It doesn't matter whether what's most prominent sits at the top, bottom, left, or right of a composition. Balance is achieved because the tensions each fragment generates are in dialogue with one another, in such a way that it doesn't seem possible, nor that it could be. So, balance can be achieved both with a composition at rest and with a dynamic one that suggests movement.

## Contrast: rhythm, variety, and hierarchy

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L46 · 04:36–06:14

Few things are more insipid than a design without contrasts. It becomes very hard to access information when it's presented without variety among its parts. Besides, there's nothing less seductive than something monotonous — so uniform that it's not just predictable, it's boring. The noticeable difference between some of a composition's elements enriches the graphic composition and gives you resources to design with. As we saw, rhythm (*ritmo*) is not the exclusive property of music. Graphic elements also suggest a sequence, but in space. Whether it's a regular or a progressive rhythm, contrast helps season it, giving it different accents and cadences. Variety gives a design nuance. It enriches it. It can emphasize certain parts of a composition and distinguish elements so they read as belonging to different categories.

What's most important? Where should I start reading? Which are the main parts and which the secondary ones? All of those are questions our brain asks itself in nanoseconds every time it looks at a piece of graphic material. Simplifying and clarifying is also hierarchizing. A composition needs gradations to organize information. This makes it easier to appreciate some fragments and to be surprised by discovering others. What comes first, what follows it, the division into smaller, more digestible parts, are all compositional results of dosing.

## Unity: proximity, alignment, and repetition

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L46 · 06:15–07:07

Thanks to contrast, I assign moments of experience to my design. I prioritize and guide the content, and along that path I prepare my viewer for visual pleasure. When the composition reveals the ideas in the content, when every fragment has a role to play, when every visual element is in dialogue with the others and with space, the phenomenon of aesthetic experience takes place. The pleasure of plastic form, what seduces and moves us (captions: *que no seduce y conmueve*, likely a caption error for *que nos seduce y conmueve*), is finding the unity (*unidad*) in the composition — one that holds up solidly and stands with security and discursive coherence. Of course, a good composition captures the attention of the person it's aimed at.

## What makes a composition ineffective

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L46 · 07:40–08:48

So, what does an inadequate composition look like? It's disorganized. It doesn't seem to have a clear logic for how it should be read. It's confusing and ambiguous, because it has no contrasts or hierarchies. It seems accidental or transitory. The tension between its elements is off-balance, or simply doesn't exist — it's unbalanced. It's also boring, or lacking in visual appeal: it fails to make its material qualities catch the recipient's attention. If our job as designers is to spark someone's interest so a piece of content reaches them, being timid, ambiguous, or inconsequential will never meet that goal.

Now that you have a clearer idea of what an effective composition is about, in the next lesson we'll see how you apply these criteria to different areas of graphic design.

### Key points (derived)

- Start from the message's core (its "DNA"); the layout is right only when it honors and emphasizes that content.
- Clarity comes from precision and simplicity; balance is a compensation of perceptual (not physical) forces, achievable at rest or in motion, symmetrical or not.
- Contrast drives rhythm, variety, and hierarchy; taken together they produce the perceived unity and salience that capture and hold a viewer's attention.
- A bad composition is disorganized, ambiguous, unbalanced, boring, or inconsequential — it fails to establish visual contact with its audience.
