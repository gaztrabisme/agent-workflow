## Directing the viewer’s eye

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L20 · 00:00–01:56

Now that we have looked at balance in composition, I want to share a few schemes that can make designs more visually interesting. When we compose a book, poster, or website, we should decide where we want the viewer to look. Advertising in the 1960s often used one high-contrast object to fill the composition so no one could miss it.

Lucian Bernhard used this approach for Faber-Castell pencils. Rather than showing a happy person writing or drawing, he removes the human figure and makes the pencil enormous. Green and red provide complementary colors. A dark background can add contrast and focus attention. His Manoli cigar advertisement similarly makes the cigars the main element against black; outlined letters strengthen their contrast.

Color can also direct the eye. In Pablo Amargo’s poster, most of the image is nearly monochrome, while the cat’s whiskers are colorful. The text in the lower-left corner also balances the cat so the composition does not fall to that side.

## Diagonals add movement

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L20 · 01:56–03:37

A diagonal can make a composition more dynamic when everything else is aligned on horizontals and verticals. In Rubens’s painting, the figures turn around a clear diagonal. The main figure is dressed in red, which also attracts attention and centers the eye.

Paul Cox uses a diagonal tightrope to speak about balance—or imbalance. An American Airlines poster turns a diagonal into an arrow. Its parts are not aligned on one vertical and horizontal, but they still balance: the sun has substantial weight, matched by the information carried in the arrow’s flags.

Two posters about the telephone show different uses of the same idea. In the first, nearly everything is diagonal, while the circular globe stabilizes the composition. Years later, the same company used a more restrained version. Most of the second poster is symmetrical, vertical, and horizontal; the telephone cable is the small diagonal that breaks the pattern and adds movement.

## The rule of thirds and simplifying

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L20 · 03:37–04:36

The rule of thirds (*regla de los tercios*) is widely used in photography and film. Imagine dividing an image into thirds vertically and horizontally. The lines create four intersection points, and placing important elements near those thirds can make a composition more interesting. Direction matters too: if a subject or portrait is placed in the right third, the person should look toward whichever side of the image has more empty space, leaving room for their gaze within the frame.

## Exercise: photograph one object three ways

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L20 · 04:36–06:12

Practice centering the eye, using a diagonal, and applying the rule of thirds. Choose a small object and photograph it three times, using one scheme for each picture. Try adding text, such as a brand name or an invented word, so you can see how it works with the composition. You can do this with a phone camera.

I use an eraser. For the eye-catching photo, I choose a dark background so the eraser stands out, then use text with strong contrast to draw the eye further. In the diagonal version, I angle the text too; with few elements, I can let everything have a more chaotic rhythm. For the rule of thirds, I use a vertical composition, place the eraser in the first third and put the text in the second.

Now make the three photographs with an object of your choice, and show them to us if you like. If you post them on Instagram, you can tag me. We are nearly ready to apply these ideas to our project and make the posts work together through composition.
