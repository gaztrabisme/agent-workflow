## No figure without ground: notan and Japonism

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L49 · 00:09–01:48

There's no figure without ground, and no ground without figure. Negative space is just as important as positive space. In this lesson I'll explain all the details behind it. Let's see what it's about.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 00:08](frames/U04L49-F0008.jpg)

On screen: "JAVIER ALCARAZ" / "Espacio negativo" (Negative space); "Life", "FORBES TRAVEL ISSUE" on the mock-up magazine cover.  
Shows: The lesson's title card beside the same Forbes Life travel-issue mock-up used to open other lessons in this unit.

In Eastern cultures, harmony between light and dark areas is a cornerstone of their aesthetics. From a deep philosophical standpoint, opposites complete each other — you can't understand light without dark, or fullness without noticing emptiness. One of the key concepts in Chinese art, developed with real mastery in Japan, is notan, an anglicized term for the word *nōtan*, where *nō* refers to what is thick, dense or strong, and *tan* refers to what is watery, scarce, white. Artists use this concept to explore arrangements of light and dark elements in a composition, without the distraction of other attributes like colour, texture or finer detail.

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A Japanese ink landscape painting, pavilions and pine trees balanced between dense dark brushwork and large areas of untouched paper.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 01:02](frames/U04L49-F0102.jpg)

On screen: [none]

Notan found huge acceptance and influence among the European Japonists (captions: *sionistas*, "Zionists" — clearly a mistranscription of *japonistas*), who adopted it along with other innovations of the Japanese print: its lack of perspective, its flat areas of vibrant colour, the freedom of composition that came from placing subjects off-centre, organized along diagonal axes, and the energy the background took on.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 01:31](frames/U04L49-F0131.jpg)

It's important to understand that this isn't a lighting effect, but a balance between light and dark shapes. This approach is radically different from how we're used to thinking in the West, because it raises space to an active position in the composition, rather than relegating it to a leftover, a mere consequence of using shapes.

## Negative space as an active, dynamic element

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L49 · 02:06–03:47

Once you conceive of negative space as a dynamic component, you can give it visual attributes. It's possible to shape it and aim for that shape to be as forthright, clear and simple as possible. The space surrounding the figures holds enormous aesthetic potential. It isn't simply a void, or the absence of content — it's a deliberate space, a breath that lets you bring out the other parts of the piece, or even create new meanings.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 02:23](frames/U04L49-F0222.jpg)

On screen: "man on earth", "Shown above are the three most distinct pictures of the history of man's years on earth..." [rest omitted]; "Ladislav Sutnar", "R. Buckminster Fuller" [rest omitted].  
Shows: A Ladislav Sutnar spread pairing dense red-brown text with three small blue diagrams of Buckminster Fuller's stages of human history, most of the double page left empty.

By also affecting its size, we manage to separate elements and groups, or emphasize the design's positive elements. Following the principle of totality, the white areas are part of the whole, and of every detail — they need to be considered as part of the overall composition.

According to Japanese philosophy, this space is full of energy and induces a contemplative state. Orientation can emphasize the visual movement of the positive elements. Its position lends weight and pause to how the composition unfolds.

Making the white shapes resemble each other helps set the positive marks apart from one another. When counter-forms are brought close together, continuity results — they join up and become larger spaces, which more clearly defines the figure, the text, the different zones of information in the visual message.

## The figure-ground principle

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L49 · 03:48–04:52

Our perception won't let us read an object as figure and ground at the same time — instead, each figure emerges from the ground through a defined contour, through contrast in value, or because its surface area is generally smaller than that of the space around it. This is a foundational rule of Gestalt psychology: the figure-ground principle.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 04:08](frames/U04L49-F0408.jpg)

On screen: "Composición para el diseño gráfico" [Composition for Graphic Design] / "Javier Alcaraz" [rest omitted]; "Contorno" (Contour), "Contraste" (Contrast), "Superficie" (Surface area).  
Shows: A large white circle containing two smaller circles, one outlined only and one filled grey, illustrating how contour, value contrast and smaller surface area each let a figure separate from its ground.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 04:16](frames/U04L49-F0416.jpg)

On screen: "Figura-Fondo" (Figure-Ground) / "— principio de percepción —" (principle of perception).  
Shows: A definition card naming the figure-ground principle.

Spaces function as illusory shapes, and are directly related to the principle of closure. Remember, since we tend to perceive objects as complete, our perception fills in a shape's missing parts.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 04:34](frames/U04L49-F0434.jpg)

On screen: "Wondering About Social Distancing?" [rest omitted]; "TEH FOREST" [sic].  
Shows: A dense newspaper page beside a poster where a forest silhouette doubles as a woman's profile — the misspelling "TEH" left uncorrected on the design itself.

Other attributes also help distinguish figure from ground, such as convexity — bulges or angles pointing outward from the shape push it forward. The opposite effect is achieved with concavity. Likewise, a symmetrical structure favours the figure standing out, while asymmetry makes the ground clearly perceptible.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 04:52](frames/U04L49-F0452.jpg)

On screen: "Moullinex / OPEN HOUSE (U.S. TOUR)" [rest omitted]; "09.23 GOOD ROOM / BAD ROOM / DJ NATURE · BLT" [rest omitted].  
Shows: Two Braulio Amado posters side by side, their bulging yellow shapes pushing forward as figure against the flat black and orange grounds behind them.

## Emphasis, symmetry and rhythm in negative space

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L49 · 04:52–06:43

Beyond the possibilities for visual effects, negative space lets us define what stands out in a piece. We manage to give emphasis to certain shapes and figures, make the space's boundaries invisible, speed it up, give it character within the piece — all of which unsettle perception and activate the viewer's mechanisms of interpretation, giving your audience a less passive role.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 05:04](frames/U04L49-F0504.jpg)

On screen: "SHIGEO FUKUDA: May 23 to 28, 1975" [rest omitted]; "54.40".  
Shows: A row of identical high-heeled legs whose repetition turns the white gaps between them into a second, ambiguous row of shapes, beside a green poster where oversized, cropped "54.40" numerals share the same photographic band pattern as the ground behind them.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 05:19](frames/U04L49-F0519.jpg)

On screen: "THE MOTELS / SHOCK"; "THE WATER RESOURCES COUNCIL", "WATER" repeated in a band across the bottom.  
Shows: A bright pop-art album cover beside a pale blue poster where a single black droplet is emphasized against a field of tiny repeated type.

In graphic design we can't neglect the gaps, the visual pauses between figures. We can't leave them to chance, because the relationship between shapes is only understandable if the spaces separating them are as carefully defined as the figures themselves.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 05:37](frames/U04L49-F0537.jpg)

On screen: "MANOS DE CACAO" (Cacao Hands), "OU70 ORIGEN ÚNICO" (Single Origin), "REGIÓN MADAGASCAR" (Region: Madagascar) [rest omitted].  
Shows: A chocolate packaging system where loose black, pink and green paint marks pick out each box's stamped region and origin details.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 05:46](frames/U04L49-F0546.jpg)

On screen: "emigrefifty!", "thinkinkinkink".  
Shows: An Emigre magazine cover, its wordplay title set alone on a mostly white page beside a solid blue spine.

Negative space organizes content. It can relate elements to each other, or separate them. It can prioritize some parts of the piece and relegate others, offering a path through the piece and, in that way, pacing how it's read.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 06:05](frames/U04L49-F0605.jpg)

On screen: "Paul Dresher Ensemble in Concert / Friday, May 31, 8 p.m." [rest omitted, mirrored and rotated text].  
Shows: A concert poster where overlapping circles trace a rhythm across a photograph of a bald head, the surrounding text set upside down and mirrored.

![5642 · U4 · L49 · 06:14](frames/U04L49-F0614.jpg)

On screen: "MANUFACTURERS OF THE WORLD FAMOUS BERKEL HAMS", "P. VAN BERKEL Ltd, Rotterdam-Holland" [rest omitted].  
Shows: A Bauhaus-style letterhead built almost entirely from empty space and two thin red rules, placed beside the Paul Dresher poster for comparison.

Counter-forms produce intervals — small or large pockets of breathing room — to generate a sequence, a controlled succession of shapes seeking aesthetic delight. This resulting rhythm, alternating figure and alternating ground, achieves different effects by varying its intensity, its strength and its duration — that is, its extent across the graphic space.

### Key points (derived)

- Negative and positive space are inseparable: no figure exists without a ground, and vice versa.
- Notan (light/dark balance, without colour or texture) is a core East Asian compositional concept that shaped European Japonisme through flat colour, off-centre subjects and diagonal axes.
- Treating negative space as dynamic — not leftover void — means giving it shape, size, orientation and position on purpose.
- The Gestalt figure-ground principle is reinforced by closure, convexity/concavity, and symmetry/asymmetry.
- Negative space organizes a piece: it groups or separates elements, builds hierarchy, and paces the sequence in which it's read, producing rhythm through the gaps (counter-forms) between figures.
- A project mood board can also be built on Pinterest: search, create a secret board, save and re-find related pins, then download chosen images into a named local folder.
