## Why simplicity matters

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L53 · 00:09–01:09

In this lesson we'll see how simplification helps us organize elements in space and also helps improve the viewer's experience of them. I want to explain how to use proximity and alignment to organize elements into less complex shapes, so you can get more out of your design. Let's look at what this involves.

As we saw before, our brain tends to filter the information it receives so it can process it. A simple composition captures attention and is transparent. I'm not saying complexity can't be aesthetic, but you shouldn't go beyond what your purpose requires. Also be careful, because oversimplifying can reduce the content to something too elemental — a design that's too obvious or boring. In graphic design we can't show everything; we have to reduce the number of elements in the piece, keeping only the main components.

## Techniques for reducing complexity

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L53 · 01:09–02:56

Simplifying means reducing the shapes or attributes of the elements. How many different frame styles do your photos have? How many different type sizes do your headlines use? Ask yourself whether two or more objects couldn't be made into just one. We can also hide parts and dose out information — this is obvious in interface design, where advanced functions sit under navigation menus.

Reduce your colour palette (*paleta cromática*) whenever you can, and apply the 60-30-10 rule we saw when choosing colour. A categorisation system based on colour works fine for 5 or 6 colours, but with more than that it becomes hard to remember and recognise. Put your photos through a colour filter that evens out their tones, and settle on a few camera angles and framings. Diversity is fine when it's a key concept you need to communicate.

As I already said, two type families can handle almost any job well. If you want stylistic range, reduce some other factor to unify the piece. Aligning objects along a few reference lines consolidates the composition and makes it solid. Remember the principle of proximity (*proximidad*)? Bringing things closer together or moving them apart creates groups that simplify the piece. On top of that, combine the groups you've created so there are as few as possible — without losing coherence, of course. Grouping the spaces to make simpler areas also keeps you from leaving whimsical, arbitrary white space. With these considerations, and whatever you can add from the forum, it'll be easier to make the ordinary extraordinary through simplicity.

## Simplifying through thumbnail sketches

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L53 · 02:56–05:07

Now, in the InDesign project, I want to show you how I started simplifying from the sketches. I use a sketching technique that consists of making very small thumbnails, so that I can't focus on detail. Sometimes I make them extremely tiny — when I say tiny, I mean around 3 by 1.5 centimetres. But by keeping these miniature elements very small, it becomes much easier to simplify the shapes and the structures, and to concentrate on them.

![5642 · U4 · L53 · 03:18](frames/U04L53-F0318.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A grid of dozens of pencil thumbnail spreads, each only a few centimetres wide, exploring rough placements of text blocks and image shapes.

As you can see, I started out making a shape that was too complex, with very strong angles, trying to build a very solid group — maybe too jarring, not simple enough. I kept trying from there, but the groups I was generating were too many. It was genuinely hard to try to produce a clean, simple structure. Every time, I ended up adding more elements instead of simplifying and reducing the shapes. So I went back to zero and started making the counter-shapes (*contraformas*) much more concrete.

I also wanted the text blocks to stand out much more clearly — that is, differentiated from the figures and the other elements on the page: photographic shapes on one side, and another group for the text. The text should always have a fixed place, so that the whole design layout becomes much simpler.

From there I realised some elements could be grouped to make them a little more present on the page. And since this article is about the new rural habits of people who move out to the periphery, there are a lot of elements — I don't know if you remember there are a lot of little plants and lots of tiny thumbnails — and that was a dilemma for the next spread. Until I arrived at a composition that seemed fairly simple and clear to me on that front.

I think working with very simple structures at a small size also helps you find things quickly — like in a notan (*notan*), if you remember that concept we covered with counter-shape: you see the clear structure right away, and from there you start adding different nuances and extra elements.

So, once I'd done that, I'm going to show you how I moved forward from there.

### Key points (derived)

- Simplicity aids attention and processing, but oversimplifying can make a design too obvious or boring — reduce only to what your purpose needs.
- Merge repeated shapes or attributes (frame styles, type sizes) into one where possible, and hide secondary information behind navigation, as interfaces do.
- Keep colour palettes small: apply the 60-30-10 rule, and cap colour-based categorisation at 5–6 colours.
- Two type families can usually carry a whole project; trade off stylistic variety against some other unifying factor.
- Use few alignment reference lines, and use proximity to group and then merge groups, to avoid arbitrary leftover white space.
- The designer's own process: tiny (~3×1.5 cm) thumbnail sketches force simplification by removing the ability to focus on detail; counter-shapes (*contraformas*) and a fixed text position keep the layout legible even with many elements.
- Across the three sample spreads, the recurring moves were: removing an unneeded subtitle, pushing elements toward the page edge, grouping images into three (an odd number), and adding or duplicating an element to create alignment (e.g. a cross) and a visual centre.
