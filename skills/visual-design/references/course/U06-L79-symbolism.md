## Unit introduction: symbology through geometry

In this unit we enter the territory of theory and foundations. We'll talk about symbology (*simbología*), and you'll understand that beyond the visual language used — in our case, simple geometry — what matters is finding what we want to say, and how we'll go about building those meanings.

## Defining sign and symbol

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L79 · 00:30–01:19

Talking about symbology, I'd like to start with a line from the author Fernando Zamora, from his book *Filosofía de la Imagen* (Philosophy of the Image): every sign (*signo*) has symbolic potential, and every symbol (*símbolo*) is a sign that has overflowed its senses and opens itself to other possible senses — to every possible sense.

## From sign to symbol: reason and emotion

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L79 · 01:34–01:55

## Symbolum: religion as the root of symbology

## Primitive symbolic thought: reading nature

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L79 · 02:47–03:31

Without doubt, the primitive human mind already grasped the most essential things around it — both dangers and benefits — and expressed them through very simple graphic signs. Its mental task was to give visible form to what exists, from its own point of view, using natural tools and supports. Its connection with nature was unique: it did not limit itself to copying what it saw, but revealed the other, inner life behind things — that is, their essence.

## Redefining the human: the animal that creates symbols

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L79 · 04:21–04:34

Perhaps, then, it would be worth slightly modifying the definition of man, or of the human being, and saying instead that we are the only animal endowed with the creative capacity to generate symbols — and therefore, to be an artist.

## Closing: from symbols to iconicity

Now that we've talked about symbology, we'll move on to our next lesson and talk about the different levels of iconicity (*iconicidad*).

### Key points (derived)

- Zamora: every sign has symbolic potential; every symbol is a sign that overflowed its senses.
- The word "symbol" comes from the Greek *sym-ballein*, to join or bring together.
- Signs can be verbal, visual, auditory, tactile or non-verbal.
- Sign = intellect and reason; symbol = sign plus emotion, culture, religion and other forces.
- *Symbolum* originally meant a community's set of religious beliefs; every religion has its own signs and symbols.
- Fandoms (Star Wars, Led Zeppelin) function like "religions" with their own shared symbolic code.
- Zamora's second definition: a symbol is a sign that has grown, without ceasing to be a sign.
- Primitive humans depicted essence, not just appearance, using simple geometric signs made with natural tools.
- Expression moved from primary needs (hunger, danger, fear) to complex emotions (joy, tenderness, sadness, wonder at birth or death).
- The sun is one of history's most frequent symbols, rendered very differently across cultures.
- Proposed redefinition: the human is the animal with the creative capacity to generate symbols — an artist.
- We can change signs freely, but not symbols, without changing ourselves; the peace symbol's persistence across decades illustrates this.
