## Elements that evoke a whole figure

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L74 · 00:07–00:47

Another way of creating unity is when elements visually stimulate the viewer or recall something figurative. An oval shape with two short lines that simulate a head is one example: it's a powerful resource for uniting forms. Whenever we evoke a whole figure, the pieces that make it up — however they are arranged — immediately read as one block.

Another example is a typographic piece built as a Christmas tree using only type. The double reinforcement comes from the typeface: it's the same type size (*cuerpo*) throughout, and the only thing that changes is the vertical zone, which simulates the trunk of the tree.

## Loose parts unified into one figure

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L74 · 01:07–01:54

There's also this figurative element from *grafía callada* ("quiet script/graphism"), where the separate branches have nothing to do with each other, yet the mind sees them as one figure rather than three loose branches. This is the concept of unity and simplification that the mind always creates.

The same thing happens with these human figures made of wood scraps. Or with Picasso, and his famous bicycle saddle and handlebars that become his bull. Or with Matisse and his nudes, from the cut-paper collages of his final period. Or with the Futurists, working with the human figure and pushing the use of angles as their main resource for creating a figure in movement — for creating movement itself, which was, in a way, their underlying drive (captions: *sublame motivo*).

## Summary: four ways to create unity

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L74 · 09:30–11:13

The really important thing to take from this lesson, I think, is to come away with a few clear concepts. The importance of creating unity when talking about a piece of work. The importance of composition, when trying to order something within the compositional space. And the four ways that, as I understand it, I use to achieve this unity, which have been covered here, can be listed as: one, proximity and similarity — things placed one on top of another, and things that formally resemble each other, unite immediately. The second is the grid — that is, having the image sit inside a grid, a grid or structure that pulls all those forms together. The third is figuration — when these forms suggest a figurative shape, even if the forms themselves are separate, if they suggest a figure they compact immediately. And the fourth is the ordering of space.

With these four resources, these four ways of composing, of using these resources to compose, a great deal can be done. In the next lesson the aim is to explain, once this concept of unity is clear, weight — that is, the prominence an image should have within the format, how to categorize and order these pieces, and how to arrive at expressing the ideas we want to convey a little more clearly.

### Key points (derived)

- Unity can come from evoking a whole figure: once a shape reads as a recognizable figure, its parts read as one block regardless of how they're arranged.
- Found or assembled materials (wood, wire, twigs, stones) can read as figures or creatures through the same principle.
- Distributing and dividing space is itself a unifying resource — both filling all of it (Miró) and leaving almost all of it empty (Chillida, Calder) can create unity.
- Weight, introduced here in passing through several images, is the subject of the next lesson.
- Four resources for compositional unity: (1) proximity and similarity, (2) the grid, (3) figuration, (4) ordering of space.
