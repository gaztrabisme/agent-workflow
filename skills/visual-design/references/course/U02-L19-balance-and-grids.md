## Why arrangement matters, and how ambiguity disrupts it

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L19 · 00:00–02:16

So far we have looked at the separate parts of a design; now we need to put them together. Composition may seem easy, but it is essential. Imagine a house with beautiful, comfortable furniture, except one piece blocks a window, another prevents a door from opening, and another has a broken leg. Composition has the same role: even when the elements are well designed, how we arrange them in a space matters.

The worst thing for a composition is ambiguity. Draw a vertical line inside a square. If it sits a little to the right of center, it is neither centered nor far enough to one side to read as intentionally eccentric. A slight tilt can also disturb the eye. We look for patterns and tend to prefer things that rest clearly, just as we stand upright: perfectly horizontal or perfectly vertical. This is why designers often use geometric, symmetrical structures.

The Parthenon illustrates how design can correct for perception. Seen from a distance, its columns could look curved or concave, so they were made slightly convex; from afar, they appear vertical. Text needs the same care. The three lines on this card do not share a perfectly straight left edge. That small curve feels unsettling, so align elements to one vertical. They can be deliberately offset, but the intention should be clear.

## Balance as a dance of visual weights

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L19 · 02:16–04:28

Ed Cheverton’s collage uses horizontal and vertical alignments, supported by graph paper. Although those lines can seem dull, he balances pieces of very different sizes and shapes. A heavy red element in the lower-left corner is balanced by the upper-right corner, so the composition does not fall to one side. Balance is more than lining things up: it is a play among weight, size, and color.

Malevich’s abstract painting removes anything unnecessary and focuses on balance. The dark square near the top would carry a lot of weight by itself: things higher on the page feel heavier, and dark colors add weight. Small yellow squares attract attention and begin to counter it. If the composition still seems to fall left, elements in the lower-right help restore balance. Their slight diagonal keeps the arrangement from becoming rigid and adds movement.

Isidro Ferrer’s poster gives another example. The brick at the top carries visual weight, and placing it above the text makes it seem ready to fall. The brick is shown in perspective rather than straight on, which gives the image depth and movement.

## Using a grid to make work consistent

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L19 · 04:28–06:59

A grid (*retícula*, also called *rejilla*) is a system of lines that acts as a template. It gives a design places to align against and helps keep repeated work, such as a magazine, consistent. It is like the lined sheet we put behind paper at school to help us write straight.

Grids usually have a margin around the page or website and columns. We often make more columns than we expect to need, which gives us flexibility: with four columns, a paragraph can span two, three, or all four. A gutter (*medianil*) is the space between columns; it keeps adjacent paragraphs from touching, so we choose a width for it. Horizontal lines help text and images align across the page. Newspapers and magazines often use between six and twelve columns, and websites also use grids. Pinterest makes its image columns easy to see.

Grids can also keep icon drawings consistent. Otl Aicher’s Munich Olympics pictograms use a grid of 90-degree and 45-degree lines so their diagonals follow the same system. The captions transcribe his name as “Otel Eiger.” Comics are another familiar example, including work by Julia Huete and Begoña García-Alén.

## Exercise: balance shapes on a grid

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L19 · 06:59–08:52

Try composition from a purely visual point of view: notice the weight of colors and shapes, then arrange them in a gridded space. Use graph paper, or another sheet that helps you keep horizontal and vertical structure. Cut shapes from magazines, colored paper, or whatever is around the house, and place them on the page. If one shape is very large, try balancing it with smaller ones. In my exercise, one form seemed to fall, so I tried to rescue it by placing a red square above it.

Move the pieces around creatively and try combinations. I also tried putting the elements near the top of the page and arranging some along a diagonal, which adds movement. After making several compositions, you will have a small library of arrangements to draw on for later designs. Now it is your turn: gather some cutouts and colored paper, and show us what you make. In the next lesson I will explain some composition rules and how they can help these schemes work.
