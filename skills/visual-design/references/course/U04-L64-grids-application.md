## Choosing the number of lines before the leading

## Growing the margin, then computing the exact leading

To make it 65 lines, I'll have to grow this margin, and for that I'll need to go back to my proportional scales. The simplest thing to do is go to the margins, always selecting the master pages, and here, on the bottom one, all I'll do is add — or rather multiply — by 1.25, and there we have it. So it'll be a little less than eleven; it'll come out as a decimal, ten point something, and that's fine. ### Key points (derived)

- A grid is planned from the general to the particular, but built from the particular to the general: fix the overall content plan first, then work out columns, gutters and fields from it.
- Twelve columns is the practical choice when a layout needs to alternate between three and four columns, since 12 is the lowest common multiple of both.
- The vertical unit normally comes from the leading, but when the format's own proportion (here, a rabatment of a 1.25 ratio) won't divide evenly by the "natural" leading, it's better to fix the number of lines you want and let the space dictate the leading instead.
- Fitting a whole number of fields (here six, at ten lines each) can mean growing a margin — using the same proportional scale as the rest of the design — before the leading is even calculated.
- The exact leading is the usable column height (converted to points) divided by the target number of lines; that value then goes into the baseline grid's increment and into every paragraph style's own leading and "align to grid" setting.
- A grid's horizontal and vertical rhythms are adjusted separately: here the gutter was widened by multiplying it by the golden ratio (1.618) once the columns felt too cramped.
- A dedicated guide-creation tool can lay out fields and flow lines automatically, snapped to the margins, once the leading and gutter values are known.
- Fitting real content (photos, headlines, bylines, captions) to a finished grid is iterative: usually only an element's first line needs to snap to the grid for the rest of that style to follow.
- The measure of a finished grid is functional (how versatile and fast it makes the design) and aesthetic (whether it lets the parts of the design connect) at once.
