## Setting a text hierarchy

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L89 · 00:07–01:09

A good typographic selection will make your visual identity system take off in a different way. In this lesson I'll go deeper into this appealing world, giving shape to the project's textual side.

When it's time to get into the typographic universe of the project — I'd already been getting a little ahead of myself with the visual proposal at the color level. I optimized the colors a bit, gave them more life, more light, added a few more miscellaneous details, and kept giving more shape to the whole structure.

What matters now is identifying, out of all our textual content, what's most important to highlight and what's least. Clearly the brand name will matter most and should carry the highest hierarchy. Its category can sit at a middle point, and the technical data can be a third level of information.

## Building a typography mood board

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L89 · 01:09–02:56

When it's time to start working with this typography, we can search different portals for families or references that interest us. The way I see it, the key move is an intermediate stage: putting together a new mood board that's purely typographic references.

What I looked for here were references tied to the decade we're working in, the seventies — record covers, posters, different graphic pieces that are purely typographic, where we can also see how colors and shapes combine with each other.

We can also look at the band's own universe: its very distinctive logo, and some typographic treatment from the album. We can also look at London's own graphics, since we're talking about London so much, and see what was happening there — this idea of plainer, simpler sans-serif faces mixed with more elaborate typefaces.

Once we've made that decision, ours will be exactly that: a somewhat elaborate typeface for the name, and a more rational, simpler typeface for the technical data. Let's start placing the content and see what happens.

## Choosing Cako Black

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L89 · 05:23–06:29

Let's bring it into this scene and see if any of the fonts I have on my computer might work. We'll try this one, called Cako Black (captions: *Kako Black*). As we can see, it brings together some of the qualities of the mood board we'd preselected, and we're going to fully commit to this typeface, so we don't drive ourselves crazy searching and searching.

As for how well this typeface fits the shapes we're generating, it has this quality of gestural marks, but also this idea of hard, rigid figures. And at the same time, because of the name I'm choosing, it lets me create a kind of symmetry where the H sits right in the middle — three letters to the left and three to the right. This typeface can also work for writing the category name.

## Typography reshapes the whole system

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L89 · 09:52–10:36

This also has to do with how, once typography comes in, the whole system gets woven together differently — there's almost no design without typography. So you have to go back and make decisions that touch both the formal and the color side of things. That's how some elements end up changing scale: something that used to be a miscellaneous detail, like this symbol, becomes something more central. All of it in service of finding a framing and a final design for this back side, which tends to be pretty boring — especially once you have to add a lot of iconography around legal matters, like whether the can is recyclable, whether the ingredients contain something that could affect certain people, and the barcode.

### Key points (derived)

- Set a clear text hierarchy first: brand name, then category, then technical data.
- Build a second, typography-only mood board from period references — record covers, posters, the band's own logo and album type, city ephemera — before picking a typeface.
- Pair an elaborate display face with a simple, rational one, or the reverse, rather than using one typeface for everything.
- Test typefaces live on foundry sites before buying; screenshotting a test and tracing it in Illustrator works as a stand-in, though it introduces small tracing errors.
- Typography reshapes the whole system: once it's in, colors, scales, and even which elements read as primary or secondary may need another pass.
- The back of a package usually carries the more generic text and the required legal iconography: recyclable mark, allergen info, barcode.
