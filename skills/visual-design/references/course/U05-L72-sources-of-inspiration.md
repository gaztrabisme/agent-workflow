Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L72 · 00:49–01:47

These notebooks date from around 2000 to 2003. At the time, he would look at images and elements that interested him and simply keep storing them. Each one interested him for a different reason — the "ordered chaos" of a particular page, for instance.

## Figurative studies: Chillida, Picasso, Matisse, and drawing left-handed

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L72 · 02:31–03:25

## Mondrian: valuing and distributing space

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L72 · 03:25–04:45

Having looked at this kind of material in overview — and he encourages doing the same with anything at all, from kitchen plates to the most unlikely source — he turns to examples of what particular artists have given him. Mondrian has been a very important artist for him. Mondrian's great contribution to graphic design, in his view, lies in how he values and distributes space: Mondrian never leaves a single space wasted. For Mondrian a composition is a kind of puzzle in which what is used matters as much as what is left aside — which is very close to page layout. Later, when laying out a page, the same principle applies: every part of the space must be considered good, must add up with no residue left over. Everything in the composition has to be essential.

![5642 · U5 · L72 · 04:04](frames/U05L72-F0404.jpg)

On screen: "P M 15" (artist's monogram and date, lower right)  
Shows: A Mondrian grid composition in black lines on white, with a blue block top-left and a yellow block bottom-left.

He shows another example of how this space has been worked — a piece perhaps not as serene in its distribution of space as Mondrian's, more unsettling, but still interesting, perhaps even more expressive than Mondrian's own work.

## Weight begins: an unnamed piece and Calder's rising mobile

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L72 · 06:08–07:27

Here the discussion turns to weight. The teacher shows a piece by an artist he names as "Mojolinari" (the name is unclear in the source and could not be verified). With just two very simple elements — practically a strip and a dot — the piece manages to narrate a story: a dot placed at the top, right at the edge of a springboard, which, propped by pale yellow and pinkish lines, reads as tilting, as if falling. With so few elements, it tells you how the greater weight sits at the top yet is supported by the red line, so that this point presses down on the springboard and looks about to fall — inciting a sense of falling movement.

![5642 · U5 · L72 · 06:38](frames/U05L72-F0638.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A Suprematist-style composition: a large black circle resting on a horizontal band of red, orange and yellow planes, crossed by a thin diagonal line.

Calder does something similar. He begins to set things in motion, to lift them, working the weight so that the eye travels upward along a very interesting visual path — simplifying and repeating the form, reducing it until it almost disappears into infinity, starting from the ground and losing that line, that path, in the far distance.

![5642 · U5 · L72 · 07:27](frames/U05L72-F0727.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A black Calder stabile-mobile: an angular base of triangular fins supporting a branching wire arm strung with black discs of decreasing size, rising toward the upper right.

## Weight in architecture and Malevich

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L72 · 07:27–08:09

The same resource appears in architecture, where the upper floors of a building look as if someone were pulling them from above: instead of pressing down, the building lifts, appearing on the point of rising into the air entirely.

![5642 · U5 · L72 · 07:42](frames/U05L72-F0742.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A cantilevered apartment building whose upper floors angle progressively outward and upward, as if tilting skyward.

Malevich uses the same solution and the same resource in one of his pieces: all the heavy elements are placed in the upper part, with the frame's upper edge pulling that weight upward while the smaller, simpler parts sit below. All the weight shifts upward, like a magnet gathering all the metal elements toward the top.

## Miró: weight and story in three simple lines

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L72 · 10:52–12:07

Then there is Miró, who has an enormous capacity to convey emotion from the simplest thing imaginable — an admirable artist, a hugely important figure for the teacher. He has seen these pieces in person: they are enormous, panels two metres by two metres, so that the whole mural must run six or seven metres or more. Beyond the textured ground, Miró gives these lines a tremendous energy simply through where he places them: one opens downward, another opens to the right, and the third is more open still and set much higher. Each carries a completely different sense of weight. The one on the left is an absolute heaviness — a thing that weighs — and its red dot sits outside the circle it forms, whereas in the middle panel the dot is centred within it: he has placed that line, with its own expressiveness, with the dot centred with respect to the format. On the right, much more open, much smaller, set much higher, the line feels as if it is rising. Between the three pieces there is a whole cluster of visual interest, and together they tell a story.

![5642 · U5 · L72 · 11:35](frames/U05L72-F1135.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A triptych by Miró: three panels on a lightly textured ground, each with a single black curved line and one coloured dot — red on the left, blue in the centre, yellow on the right.

## Closing: from method to inspiration to practice

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L72 · 17:29–18:03

That covers, first, the working method, and now the sources of inspiration — how he sees certain artists having used these principles of perception, principles he finds very important and very interesting. Next comes the point of applying and working through each of the principles discussed. He will see everyone again shortly, in the next lesson.

### Key points (derived)

- The teacher treats his own notebooks (2000s onward) as raw material, not archive: pages mix typography deconstruction, figurative sketches after Chillida/Picasso/Matisse, and his own calligraphy, all kept for a line's expressiveness rather than its subject.
- Recurring reading across very different artists: value/distribute all of the space with nothing wasted (Mondrian, comparable architecture); load visual weight toward one point or edge (an unnamed Suprematist piece, Calder, Malevich, Art Nouveau/Deco, a cantilevered building); exploit oblique-line tension for depth (an unnamed painting, the Library of Alexandria, a photographer's shadows, Cassandre); use fragmentation to invite the eye to complete a form (a wedge-shaped chair); deform the circle for expressive tension (Arp across relief/architecture/sculpture/furniture, Picasso's Guernica studies); use repetition to depict movement (chronophotography, the Futurists, Miró); build a deliberate visual path through a work (architecture, Chema Madoz, Miró); and play figure against ground (Chillida, Calder).
- A single line or a single dot, placed correctly, can carry a whole composition's weight and narrative — the recurring example is doing more with fewer elements, not more.
