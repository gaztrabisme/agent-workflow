# Lesson 66 · First experiences

## An invitation with no time and no tools

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 00:00–01:15

Pepe Gimeno opens with two framing questions put to him: what his experience at the University of Madrid was, and then what his own path with waste materials has actually been, hand in hand with these materials. It really began with an invitation from Raquel Pelta and Paco Rayón to take part in the "Sin Límites" (Without Limits) exhibition, held in Zaragoza. He was invited in July and had to submit the work in October — bad timing, since he already had a lot of work on, and on top of that he was going on holiday in August. He realised that if he wanted to take part, he would have to use his beach holiday to produce something for the show. That meant he would have no technology available to him at all: no computer, no printer, nothing.

## Combining three interests: waste material, layout and perception

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 01:15–02:10

At that point he considered two or three directions and finally decided to bring three concepts that interested him at the time together into a single project. One was waste material, which he did not yet know how to apply, although he had already had some experience with it — he had worked pieces of shell into plasticine, shown on screen. The second was layout: he had always been very interested in composition, had studied and worked with it, and enjoyed it a great deal. The third was a long-standing interest in the principles of perception — something he had always enjoyed playing with and testing, but had rarely had time for, though it was always on his mind.

## Two reference books on visual perception

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 02:10–03:02

He names two reference books in this field. The first, and for him the great discovery, was Kandinsky's *Point and Line to Plane*: a gentle, easy-to-read introduction that he recommends to everyone who has not read it. The second is closer to a bible for him: a copy he bought many years ago, now battered with a loose cover from heavy use. He has never read it start to finish, only in pieces and chapters, in no particular order — he thinks it cannot be read in one sitting, because it holds so much information that the brain would burst; it has to be absorbed gradually. It is Rudolf Arnheim's *Art and Visual Perception*.

## The method: from the storm-washed beach to a notebook layout

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 03:02–04:45

With those three ideas in place, he began collecting waste material on the beach. His working method was, first, to collect after storms — the days when the sea deposits every kind of material on the sand. He gathered material and simply washed it, removing the sand, without trying to manipulate or alter it: that was one of the rules of the piece. He kept it as found, and above all he collected broken material — he was not interested in pretty shells or pretty stones, only in damaged material. He then left it to dry, and once dry, sorted it by type into trays: stones in one, long reeds in another, and so on. His stretch of beach was close to a marsh, so reed material was abundant, glass was scarce, but there was plenty of reed and wood.

After sorting, he worked in parallel in a notebook, sketching approximations of how this kind of material could be arranged: joining pieces, seeing dots and dashes, then substituting lines for actual pieces, and so on, accumulating one layout after another.

## Composing and gluing onto a white support

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 04:45–05:00

Once he had accumulated these layouts, he arranged them on a white support — a 70 x 100 cm sheet — and once he had found the arrangement he was looking for, he fixed the pieces down with glue. That, he says, was simply the whole working process.

## Nine large finished pieces, and a page from the catalog

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 05:00–06:21

He then shows the results. The first piece he made surprised him a great deal — it seemed to hold so many possibilities, and interested him so much, that instead of stopping after the single piece he had originally planned, he made a large number of them: nine in total. When he had finished, he saw the pieces were large — each one measures 1.40 by 1 metre — and called Paco Rayón to tell him he had produced far more material than planned and didn't think Rayón would be interested. Rayón asked for a few days to work out whether it would all fit, then called back to say it would, and to send everything, so Gimeno ended up sending the complete set. The work pays close attention to the spacing and organisation of each "page," treating blocks of material like blocks of text of different sizes, simulating different type sizes.

## Neighbours, bin bags, and the "Diogenes syndrome" joke

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 06:21–07:25

Once the whole project was finished, he had a series of very strong feelings while making it. For one thing, it was very entertaining, because he felt he had a touch of Diogenes syndrome: his neighbours, seeing him get into the lift with plastic bags full of what looked like rubbish, would stare at him, alarmed. Nobody said anything outright, but they clearly started to look at him strangely — helped along by the fact that he and his family were new to the building. After a few days he had to start inviting people over to show them the project, which surprised them enormously: "how funny," they would say, and shortly after they started bringing him material themselves, calling him up to say they had found him something interesting.

## A trip back in time: what could be made with nothing at all

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 07:25–07:57

He also found it exciting in another way, almost like time travel: the question of what he, with a contemporary graphic culture, would do with no tools and no technology at all — as if he had found himself in nature, stripped down, a primitive man. What would he do in those circumstances, making that trip back in time? He could make exactly this kind of thing: an ordering, a set of compositions — not much more than that.

## The redemptive, "alchemist" feeling of turning rubbish into form

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 07:57–08:53

He also came to notice something he found beautiful: a redemptive quality to the work, a little like feeling an alchemist. He would come up from the beach with rubbish, and gradually, once he started ordering it, it already took on a different character; but once he laid it out on the cardboard and composed it, something that had been unbearable to look at suddenly had visual interest. That transformation gave him a real charge — a sense of having an enormous capacity, of being a kind of alchemist. Those were very powerful sensations, and he believes they were part of what encouraged him to continue, and what pushed him to keep working in this way.

## Separating content from form — and what came next

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L66 · 08:53–10:02

As for the concepts this experience gave him, he singles out one as fundamental: the ability to separate content from form — how it laid structures bare, how pure structure could be worked with using these materials, because there is no image, nothing to distract; you work with pure structure. He thinks this is one of the most interesting things about this kind of work: it lets you set narration aside and leaves only bare structures.

He closes by saying he will go on, little by little, to other projects that grew out of this first one, out of this first "Sin Límites" experience — which even went on to become part of another project that has mattered a great deal to him: Grafía Callada (Silent Writing).

### Key points (derived)

- The "Sin Límites" piece began as a forced experiment: an exhibition deadline that fell during a technology-free beach holiday.
- It combined three long-standing interests — waste material, layout/composition, and the principles of visual perception — informed by Kandinsky's *Point and Line to Plane* and Arnheim's *Art and Visual Perception*.
- His method had eight steps: collect (after storms, broken pieces only), clean, dry, classify by type, sketch layouts in a notebook, compose, and glue onto white supports.
- What began as one planned piece grew into nine large (1.40 x 1 m) works, all accepted into the exhibition and catalog.
- The project's central lesson: working with unaltered found material strips away image and narrative, separating content from form and leaving pure structure — a discovery that fed directly into his later project, Grafía Callada.
