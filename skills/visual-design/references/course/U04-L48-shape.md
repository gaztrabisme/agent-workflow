## What is a shape: totality and simplicity

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L48 · 00:07–01:51

Design, when you get down to it, is organizing shapes in space. But what do we mean by "shape"? In this lesson I'll explain what shapes are in graphic design, how to work with them, and how to generate some of them out of groups. Let's take a look together.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 00:08](frames/U04L48-F0008.jpg)

On screen: "Javier Alcaraz" / "Forma" (Shape); "Life", "FORBES TRAVEL ISSUE" on the mock-up magazine cover.  
Shows: The lesson's title card beside a Forbes Life travel-issue cover mock-up, a photograph of Moroccan doorways used as decoration.

To talk about shape, we need to agree that we mean any visual object — point, line, plane, geometric or organic. A shape is perceived in the human mind following certain perceptual principles, defined by the psychologists of the Gestalt school. Without question, two of those principles are more influential than any of the other considerations we'll see: the principle of totality and the principle of simplicity.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 00:59](frames/U04L48-F0059.jpg)

On screen: header "Composición para el diseño gráfico" (Composition for graphic design) / "Javier Alcaraz"; "DOMESTIKA.ORG"; "Totalidad" (Totality) / "Simplicidad" (Simplicity).  
Shows: A dotted-outline square between the two names, introducing them as the section's two key principles.

The first has to do with the famous phrase, the whole is more than the sum of its parts. That is, the overall structure of a scheme dominates our perception.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 01:09](frames/U04L48-F0109.jpg)

On screen: header "Composición para el diseño gráfico" / "Javier Alcaraz" [rest omitted]; "Totalidad" / "— más que la suma de sus partes —" (more than the sum of its parts).  
Shows: A definition card naming the principle of totality with its tagline.

We can't conceive of isolated fragments of a piece of design — each graphic unit plays the role of a part that props up the whole.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 01:20](frames/U04L48-F0120.jpg)

On screen: "MAN HAT TAN" on the record sleeve; "Lorca" traced in sand, "3 Tragedies", on the book cover.  
Shows: An abstract Mondrian-like record sleeve beside a collage book cover — both read as one whole rather than as separate marks.

The principle of simplicity tells us that the mind, in its drive to survive external stimuli, always looks for simple shapes and configurations that have regular characteristics and approximate pure geometric forms. Faced with a visual experience, the brain asks itself two questions: what is the simplest shape I can find? And also, what is the simplest way to organize the structure?

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 01:33](frames/U04L48-F0133.jpg)

On screen: header "Composición para el diseño gráfico" / "Javier Alcaraz" [rest omitted]; "Simplicidad" (Simplicity) / "— reducción de rasgos esenciales —" (reduction to essential features).  
Shows: A definition card naming the principle of simplicity with its tagline.

A shape identifies itself by its boundaries. The contour of a figure is what helps us recognize and tell it apart from others. The simplicity of that contour is fundamental both to perception and to our understanding of it.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 01:50](frames/U04L48-F0150.jpg)

On screen: "BEYOND BEAUTY", "THE REVOLUTION OF DESIRE", "Natalie Portman"; "WONDERLAND", "TECHLEAP BRAND IDENTITY", "Accelerating the tech ecosystem of the Netherlands".  
Shows: A perfume-brand website pairing large translucent pink circles with a still-visible close-up portrait behind oversized lettering, beside a dark tech-identity website built from a few plain shapes.

## Structural axes, symmetry and the law of Prägnanz

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L48 · 02:05–03:15

As we saw before, structural axes — inherited from our experience of space — are imposed on everything we see, which is also why they define the identity of shapes. The basic structure of a square, a triangle or a person is what lets us recognize it, even though we can vary its direction and position.

Increasing an object's symmetry increases its simplicity, because it reduces the number of structural features — there are fewer variations around the vertical and horizontal axes.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 02:26](frames/U04L48-F0225.jpg)

On screen: "Fontana Modern Masters / Editor Frank Kermode / Lukács / George Lichtheim" and "... / Marcuse / Alasdair MacIntyre"; "VOL. 5 / NO. 4" on the type-collage cover.  
Shows: Two symmetrically striped paperback covers beside a cover built from tumbling, near-symmetrical block letters spelling "BROOM".

But watch out: increasing its asymmetry also simplifies the object, because that clarifies its difference from the vertical and horizontal axes. Both tendencies — the one that levels out and the one that sharpens — are the same strategy: making the structure as clearly defined as possible. The key, then, is to eliminate ambiguity. The Gestalt psychologists called this the law of Prägnanz, from the German for concise, precise or exact.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 03:10](frames/U04L48-F0310.jpg)

On screen: "L. Moholy-Nagy / 60 Fotos / 60 photos / 60 photographies / Fototek 1"; "PENGUIN BOOKS / THE GREAT GATSBY / F. SCOTT FITZGERALD / FICTION / The tale of a man who built himself an illusion to live by".  
Shows: A cropped, diagonally sliced photograph on a Moholy-Nagy monograph cover beside the plain red-and-cream Penguin cover for The Great Gatsby.

## Bertin's visual variables

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L48 · 03:16–04:56

Visual objects have different attributes that we can alter for our own purposes. The French cartographer and father of modern graphic representation, Jacques Bertin, defined these attributes as visual variables.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 03:21](frames/U04L48-F0321.jpg)

On screen: header "Composición para el diseño gráfico" / "Javier Alcaraz" [rest omitted]; "Atributos formales" (Formal attributes).  
Shows: Bertin's own diagram of shape, size, colour, value, texture and orientation, arranged as branches around a small white square.

Objects, then, can vary in shape, which — as we said — is perceived through its contours and structural axes. In size, which refers to the object's dimension and depends on comparing it with other elements or with its context. In colour, which we'll develop in another lesson, and which accounts for a shape's hue. A further, dependent variable follows from colour: value, or luminosity, a gradation of tone where light is added to it or taken away, making it darker. Texture, or grain, refers to filling a figure with some repeating pattern, producing an effect that can range from dense to sparse. Orientation, which refers to an object's position and direction relative to the vertical and horizontal axes — there are shapes whose orientation can't be perceived, like a circle, or ones that are too complex.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 04:24](frames/U04L48-F0424.jpg)

On screen: "Forma / Tamaño / Color / Valor / Textura / Orientación" (Shape / Size / Colour / Value / Texture / Orientation).  
Shows: The finished diagram: each of the six visual variables illustrated by a row of five icons, from a square through five sizes, five colours, five darkening reds, five stripe densities and five rotations.

And position in two-dimensional space, which, although it isn't a property of the shape itself, is decisive for achieving organizational effects between the units of a composition, because, as we said before, we see the overall image of an object before we perceive its details.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 04:55](frames/U04L48-F0455.jpg)

On screen: "DE BEDRIJFSZEKERHEID VAN ONZE ... rmaalkabel" (The reliability of our ... normal cable), "\"H T SP TŠ\"", "NEDERLANDSCHE KABELFABRIEK - DELFT" (Dutch Cable Factory, Delft) [rest omitted]; "FEININGER / GEMÄLDE AQUARELLE GRAPHIK / 9. OKTOBER – 2. NOVEMBER 1927 / KUNSTVEREIN JENA" (Feininger: paintings, watercolours, prints; 9 October – 2 November 1927, Kunstverein Jena) [rest omitted].  
Shows: A Dutch cable-factory advertisement built from repeated plug-and-socket roundels, beside a German exhibition poster whose type blocks are stacked at different positions on the page.

## Grouping principles: similarity and proximity

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L48 · 04:57–06:27

Taking these attributes, or visual variables, into account, the Gestalt perceptual studies establish five principles that are key to handling shape in composing a design.

Similarity, and consequently difference. Under this principle, we tend to group objects with similar attributes. Any aspect — shape, colour, luminosity, position in space, direction, movement — can trigger this grouping.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 05:14](frames/U04L48-F0514.jpg)

On screen: "Semejanza" (Similarity); "a millionth anniversary", "Meridian Books is a little less than three years old, but we're already celebrating our millionth anniversary..." [rest omitted].  
Shows: A red brochure spread whose bold headline "a millionth anniversary" is printed across rows of black-and-cream dots, with body copy and a mailing address in the corner.

But they can also be separated: by giving them distinct characteristics, we manage to detach parts of the composition from each other. Remember, though, that similarity is a precondition for noticing differences — comparisons, connections and separations between disparate things aren't made unless there's a baseline of regularity against which to make the difference stand out. You can emphasize an element, make it stand out, draw attention to it, by altering one or more factors of similarity. Similarities in these attributes bind together elements that are far apart from one another in space.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 05:49](frames/U04L48-F0549.jpg)

On screen: "383 Madison Avenue, New York, N.Y. 10017", "Holt, Rinehart and Winston, Inc."; dot-pattern poster with a small black cross, "the pipe" [rest omitted].  
Shows: A poster built from a tree of scattered letters, one letter singled out in red, beside a grid of identical black dots with one black diamond marker breaking the pattern.

But spatial position is also, by itself, a factor of grouping. Proximity, or closeness, produces visual clusters, a relationship among elements. What is close together presents itself in our perception as backed by some semantic condition.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 06:15](frames/U04L48-F0615.jpg)

On screen: "EROS / Summer, 1962"; "Jeff Keedy / CalArts Graphic Design / Current faculty & past program director / Thursday 1pm / January 18th 1996" [rest omitted].  
Shows: A yellow magazine cover with its masthead in red beside a poster whose many typeface sizes cluster into readable groups purely through their spacing.

Once again, if you want to emphasize an element through this principle, you need to increase the distance, the remoteness, the space between it and the design's other units.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 06:28](frames/U04L48-F0628.jpg)

On screen: "Igni / Full-service web studio / We illuminate the dark web space" [rest omitted].  
Shows: The "Igni" web-studio homepage, its menu items listed vertically along the left edge at mid-page, set apart from the large illustration and headline.

## Continuity and closure

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L48 · 06:28–07:55

Continuity combines ingredients from the principle of closure, since it seeks to complete pauses or empty spaces, and from the principle of good form, or Prägnanz, in choosing the simplest, most emphatic shapes. The eye follows a line, a path, a route through shapes or spaces, and relates them in the simplest way possible. By linking two or more points through continuity, the designer can establish visual movement.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 06:58](frames/U04L48-F0658.jpg)

On screen: "novità Nebiolo: Indici direzionali, Frecce fantasia, segni turistici, segni dello Zodiaco" (Nebiolo novelties: directional indices, fantasy arrows, tourist signs, zodiac signs) [rest omitted]; "産経観世能" (Sankei Kanze Noh theatre programme) [rest omitted, Japanese poster].  
Shows: A red Italian type-specimen sheet of repeated arrows and zodiac marks beside a poster where a single continuous black line describes hair, brow and jaw across four colour blocks.

Likewise, a break in continuity creates an anomaly that stands apart and distinguishes itself. Use this principle to your composition's advantage.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 07:09](frames/U04L48-F0709.jpg)

On screen: "weniger Lärm" (less noise).  
Shows: A black-and-white photograph of a face turned upside down, with the diagonal red type running along the same line as the raised arm.

This next principle says that we see incomplete shapes, or ones only suggested by the arrangement of other elements, as whole — we perceive the general shape even when some parts are missing.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 07:22](frames/U04L48-F0722.jpg)

On screen: "10×", "100×", "fotografías desde el confinamiento" (photographs from the lockdown), "Frine Ortega y Rafael Cano".  
Shows: A plain black book cover where the title and two numbers sit along a circle whose outline is never actually drawn.

The more coherent and precise the shape we manage to complete, the more easily it will stand out from its surroundings.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 07:30](frames/U04L48-F0730.jpg)

On screen: "A conversation about type with Karrie Jacobs, New York, 2/20/1990" [rest omitted]; "It's not what type you use, it's what your intentions are and whether it matters to you that people read or not."  
Shows: A text spread beside a page where a red ring, mostly hidden under a photograph of a hat, is completed by the eye as a full circle.

Understanding this principle lets us construct shapes without needing to fully configure them.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 07:36](frames/U04L48-F0736.jpg)

On screen: "Kaepa." on each box.  
Shows: A stack of coloured shoeboxes photographed at an angle, their running-shoe illustration continuing legibly from box to box even where a box is turned away from the camera.

## Figure and ground

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L48 · 07:56–08:37

Because our brain prefers simple solutions, it needs to separate the figure from the ground it sits on — we can't read an object as figure and ground at the same time. This relationship is also known as form and counter-form, or negative space; we'll develop it in detail in the next lesson.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 07:56](frames/U04L48-F0756.jpg)

On screen: "WWF" and the copyright/registered-trademark marks.  
Shows: The WWF panda mark, its white body formed entirely from the gaps left between the black patches.

Colour or tonal value have very little influence if we don't take the visual object's context into account.

## Hierarchy, proportion, and the composition as a whole

Anchor: 5642 · U4 · L48 · 08:38–09:02

Understanding these perceptual principles lets us build a structure in our design through relationships that group or segregate shapes in space. But beyond that, as Jacques Bertin proposes, some visual attributes can produce hierarchy effects — size, position in space, or a colour's value.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 08:47](frames/U04L48-F0847.jpg)

On screen: "Più veloce dell'aereo" (Faster than the plane); speed labels "250 km/h", "300 km/h", "320 km/h", "350 km/h" [rest omitted].  
Shows: An infographic where concentric arcs, sized and coloured by distance and speed, rank the world's high-speed rail lines against one another.

In the same way, and also thanks to the dimensions of objects and where they sit on the page, we can show proportions or quantities relative to one another. But what's important to understand about shape in graphic design is that a composition, for any piece, acts as a global shape, and consequently follows the same principles that govern visual perception.

![5642 · U4 · L48 · 08:57](frames/U04L48-F0857.jpg)

On screen: "$7.95 issue # 27." / "+ David Carson" [rest omitted].  
Shows: A dark, halftone magazine cover in the RayGun style, its small credit line the only clear text against a wave-like silhouette.

### Key points (derived)

- A shape is any visual object, read through two dominant Gestalt principles: totality (the whole outweighs its parts) and simplicity (the mind seeks the plainest reading available).
- Symmetry and asymmetry both simplify a shape, by making its relationship to the vertical and horizontal axes unambiguous — the Gestalt "law of Prägnanz".
- Jacques Bertin's six visual variables — shape, size, colour, value, texture, orientation — plus position in space, are the attributes a designer can alter.
- Five Gestalt grouping principles organize shapes in a composition: similarity, proximity, continuity, closure, and figure/ground.
- Visual attributes such as size, position and colour value can also build hierarchy and show relative proportion.
- A whole composition behaves as a single global shape, governed by the same perceptual principles as any smaller one.
- Building a project mood board starts with reference images: search generically, then by a known reference figure, then by uploading an image to find visually similar ones.
