## Redundancy and restraint

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L12 · 00:00–02:12

Small choices in typography can spoil a design. The first crime is obviousness, probably the mistake I encounter most often and one of my favorites. As we discussed earlier in Unit 2, image and text should coexist rather than say the same thing, because that would be redundant. A birthday card that uses a cake, balloons, confetti, and a very festive, multicolored typeface repeats the same birthday signal several times. In the first invitation, every one of those elements competes for attention. A quieter design chooses one cue and lets the other elements work with it.

In the cleaner birthday example, a candle and a hand make the number 10: the candle is the 1 and the hand forms the 0. The type itself does not announce the birthday. Instead, a simple face and lively arrangement make the phrase feel cheerful, and the design uses only two colors. The cake, number, and text already establish the occasion, so festive colors are not needed as another signal.

Paula Scher’s Public Theater poster for *Love and War* in New York uses one typeface. The forms around the words carry the ideas of love and war; two different typefaces are not needed to label each idea. The type and image can complement one another without spelling out the same message twice.

## Use one typeface family with purpose

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L12 · 02:12–03:02

Another common mistake is putting many typefaces and styles together. Before adding a second typeface, ask why it is needed. In most designs, one typeface and its styles—such as italic or underline—are enough. If another face is useful, decide what it contributes; too many unrelated choices can make the design look messy and purposeless.

A typeface family with several weights offers a way to emphasize selected words while keeping a coherent system. Lighter versions, usually called light, and heavier versions, usually called bold, can create a typographic dance (*baile tipográfico*) and direct attention without introducing a new face for every phrase.

## Preserve letterforms and choose the right width

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L12 · 03:02–04:44

Do not squeeze or stretch a typeface to make it fit. Letters are drawn for particular proportions, and changing their width distorts their design. Now that you know how to search for typefaces online, look for a condensed or extended face when text does not fit a design or when the typeface you are using does not fill the space. Some families are variable: they offer widths from very condensed to extended. The example of Dropbox uses a grotesque typeface with both kinds of forms.

Very narrow signs can also be hard to read when every letter is stacked underneath the one before it. That is especially difficult on the street, where people need to read quickly. A condensed typeface can help; another option is to rotate the text 90 degrees. MoMA uses rotated lettering, which is easier to read than a vertical stack even if the reader has to turn their head.

## Exercise: design as badly as possible

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L12 · 04:44–05:12

For practice, make a birthday invitation as badly as possible by using all the mistakes from this lesson. Show it to us. If the birthday person gets angry, the teacher apologizes. The next lesson moves from these crimes to practical tricks for making text work well.

### Key points (derived)

- Let image and type complement each other instead of repeating the same cue.
- Keep a clear reason for each typeface and use the family’s own weights and widths.
- Preserve designed letter proportions; choose a condensed or extended face, or rotate text, when the layout calls for it.
