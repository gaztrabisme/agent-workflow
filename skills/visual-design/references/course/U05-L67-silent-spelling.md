## A book about writing with not one written word

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 00:00–01:59

Continuing from the previous lesson, Pepe Gimeno explains that the obvious next step for this layout project was to make a book. If he was already producing a layout, why not a book? He originally thought the subject would be writing and the sign (*el signo*), later widening it to other forms of representation, even painting. The book he planned was a book-object (*libro objeto*) containing not one single written word — a deliberate paradox: a book about writing that holds no writing at all.

Beyond the paradox, he was interested in a double reading of the project: on one hand, a book lets him order individual pieces into a sequence, giving the whole collection a single reading; on the other, he was interested in the original pieces themselves — each 1.40 by 1 metre — whose surface textures and material presence cannot be read from a sequence of small reproductions. The material itself is very powerful, and one of its most interesting qualities is the texture, and what can be seen and read in its surface.

## Grafía Callada's origin: an exhibition at IVAM

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 01:59–02:45

As he has already told, Grafía Callada (Silent Writing) grew out of a personal project — one that set out with no fixed goal, but ended somewhere he could never have anticipated: an exhibition at the IVAM, the Valencian Institute of Modern Art, here in Valencia. That exhibition presented the project much as he had conceived it: on one side, the book-object as a sequence; on the other, the individual pieces themselves.

## A chaotic order, the hard lesson of plastic, and a cover that inverts Magritte

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 02:45–05:14

He then explains how the book is structured. He did not work from a finished plan and then order the book afterward; he built sections one after another, expanding as he went, so the result is partly ordered and partly chaotic. He did not fully know, when making the first pages, what direction the book would take — something that shows toward the end, because his experience with the materials had changed enormously by then.

At the start he worked only with what he considered "noble" materials — wood, natural things, reeds, stones, seaweed — and barely touched plastic. Plastic, especially the broken and ugly pieces he chooses, is with its garish colours and clinging dirt very ungrateful to work with; it is also a material made to look new, shiny and perfect, always well finished and rounded. He found himself facing something he could not master, and it took him a long time to overcome that unpleasant feeling, that instinctive resistance the material gave him.

The book has all the traditional parts of a book. Its cover is a single reed leaf, and it always reminds him of Magritte's famous painting *This Is Not a Pipe*: the cover works in the opposite direction to what Magritte was doing. Magritte painted a pipe and said, "this is not a pipe, it is a representation of a pipe." Here, the leaf really is a reed leaf, but it also represents a vertical sign — the most basic mark there is. If the book was going to talk about writing and about the sign, what better way to begin than with that most basic vertical stroke?

## Traditional parts of a book, made strange: dedication and credits

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 05:14–06:24

After the cover comes the endpaper (*guarda*) — he has always enjoyed decorating a book's endpapers, almost never leaving them plain — then a half-title and title page, where the book already begins to work by suggestion: throughout the whole project he is always evoking a writing that is not really there. None of the "writing" that appears anywhere in the book has any scientific rigour; it is all approximation, closer to painting the idea of writing than writing itself.

The dedication page carries a short dedication and a cascade of stones falling like a waterfall, recalling memory — the author looking back and thanking someone for their support or help. The credits page is illustrated with a fingerprint made from cigarette butts, which, even after their time on the beach, still smelled of tobacco.

## An index, and a nod to classic book design

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 06:24–07:31

Next comes a multiple index, grouped by content, with two other possible orderings behind it. Then the classic opening image every book has — the author's photograph, or a photograph of an important project — followed by a page paying homage to the layout of a classic book, reproducing its structure and text block (*mancheta*). The presentation itself is written as a serious, rigorous text. Then comes the opening of the first chapter proper: a title and a suggestive text with an illustration, moving into the body of the book with type set at different sizes and weights. A little further on is another index — an index of materials — curious because it only lists the materials he had used up to roughly the halfway point of the book; the range of materials expanded considerably after that.

## Counting, then a tour of invented and world scripts

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 07:31–09:09

The first real "writing" in the book, he explains, is counting: he believes the earliest writing was a way of counting people, animals and so on. From there the book moves through a long, deliberately non-chronological sequence of invented and evoked scripts, going backward and forward rather than in any historical order: an approximation of cuneiform writing (never a strict reproduction, always just a suggestion of it); a gestural script; tests in a Mesopotamian style; vertical writing, twice over; entries into the ideogram; an oriental-style script; a script for the blind, read by touch; "the sign" as such; a medieval codex; Arabic script; a page of notes on the left; a contemporary calligraphic hand; and a binary script. He is not making a scientific survey of writing systems; it is a formal collection, and he deliberately puts these scripts side by side so their nuances and textures contrast with and set each other off.

## The chapter of signs: a nod to Mondrian, and the value of blank space

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 09:09–10:53

He then names the recurring contrasts he was building toward, and moves into the chapter of "the sign" (*el signo*) itself. Several of these pages, he says, pay homage to Mondrian: the balance of white space matters enormously here, and a page holds together more through what is left blank than through what is filled in. The chapter runs through a collection of materials and a sub-index; the sign itself; the sign as an element of an alphabet; an expressive sign; the sign and composition, with a dynamic text on the right; the sign as understood by Arab, Oriental and Japanese calligraphers; the sign as art; the sign in Aztec (he corrects himself — not Mexican, Aztec) writing systems; and, on one page, a deliberate joke: streets of white space running through a block of text, which is exactly what one should never do in typesetting — a wink, a piece of play.

## A sign as an alphabet element, built on a diamond grid

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 10:53–11:44

The sign as decorative element leads to an alphabet built from a diamond (rhomboid) grid. This piece, he notes, really is battered and worn — but that, he says, is simply how he found it: one of the rules he followed throughout the book is that materials are never manipulated, always used exactly in the form and condition they were found. The one exception in the whole book is a different piece: a large net, which he did cut down to size. Not manipulating the material is, he says, one of the most important challenges of the whole approach, and one of the reasons he finds this kind of material interesting — it is what this course is centred on, and what it allows. But it also demands real discipline: working with unaltered material is hard, and after this series of projects he eventually had to go back to the brush, to making a mark at whatever size he needed, in order to express himself, because working only with found material is very rigid.

## The idea of texture and network in writing

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 12:54–13:44

Working on writing, he found himself returning again and again to the same recurring idea: the concept of grain or texture (*trama*), the general greyness every script produces. Every writing system aims for a continuous mark with a movement of its own — some rigid, some freer — but a sense of texture always sits behind it. This chapter gathers those constants. He was also reflecting on the idea that writing is a network (*red*): once you link words together in a sentence, you can no longer pull them apart without changing the message's form and content. A piece of writing, he says, really is a net.

## Random order, and the first piece to use plastic

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 13:44–14:51

There is also something interesting in how very disparate elements, once ordered and sequenced, suddenly suggest that whoever arranged them is saying something. He points to a page built on a random, aleatory ordering, using very diverse materials — one of the first pieces where he finally let the "infamous" plastic in. It reads as powerful and harsh; he worked with a great deal of white space, trying to neutralise the clash between the different pieces. Ordering and sequencing things, he adds, is always suggestive: the human mind's constant habit is to try to find order and make sense of what it sees, and it is in that sense-making that creativity happens — you can play, suggesting rather than stating, and let the viewer complete the work in their own head. That suggestion, he says, is one of the most interesting things in any piece of work.

## What every alphabet has in common: a lollipop sticks, and caps dropped into a grid

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 14:51–16:21

He turns next to what every alphabet has in common: certain letters always share the same basic elements — an O or an A or a B always has a circle, and always a vertical or horizontal stroke that finishes defining its base. One page speaks directly to that idea; on its left side, an entire alphabet made from lollipop sticks (*palos de chupa chup*) in different colours, showing that tilting a single repeated stick is enough to generate a whole alphabet. Another page amuses him particularly, mixing two concepts at once: a grid (*retícula*) — ordering and composing within a structure — and a random form. Bottle caps sit within the grid, even where pieces are missing, so the grid reads as emptied out, while the background is made of branches that look as if they had simply been allowed to fall, even though they are in fact carefully finished. On the facing page, order and sequence are read the ordinary way: left to right, left to right, left to right.

## Other ways to represent: geometry and colour

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 16:21–17:37

The book then leaves writing behind for other systems of representation. First comes geometry, which can visualise concepts that would be very hard to explain and picture through writing alone. Other languages follow: music, astral charts, silence and sound, and colour. Painting, he says, is the representation system par excellence — perhaps the most descriptive of all. He draws a comparison here between European art, where the whole universe is packed inside the single piece, and the suggestion typical of Oriental art, which is always only a fragment of cells — you never see the whole scene, always a part of it. Perspective is fundamental too: there are two ways to create depth, either through perspective or through overlapping (superposition).

## Figuration, abstraction, and a closing signature

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L67 · 17:37–18:54

The book closes its content with figuration and abstraction. Abstraction, and the avant-gardes, he says, are the reason the book can exist at all — without abstraction, this book simply would not exist. He shows the limit of figuration: with only two marks you can suggest a landscape, and already see one. And, finally, painting itself. That closes the book's content. He always ends with the silhouette of his studio, which appears somewhere in almost every book he makes — here it is barely visible, but the motif is there.

### Key points (derived)

- Grafía Callada began as the natural next step from the "Sin Límites" layouts: turning a sequence of pieces into a book-object with no written words, built around a paradox (a book about writing with nothing written in it).
- The book was built section by section, not planned end to end, and documents a real shift in the artist's practice: starting with "noble" natural materials only, and only gradually learning to work with the harder, "ungrateful" material of plastic.
- Every traditional part of a book — cover, endpapers, dedication, credits, index, presentation — is remade using found materials and pure suggestion, never literal writing.
- The book surveys invented and real-world scripts side by side (cuneiform, Arabic, ideograms, braille-like writing, binary) purely for their formal contrast, not as a scientific record, before moving into a dedicated chapter on "the sign."
- Two rules run through the whole project: material is used exactly as found, without manipulation (with one deliberate exception), and ordering/sequencing disparate objects always suggests meaning, because the human mind cannot help searching for sense in what it sees.
- The book ends by leaving writing behind for other representational systems — geometry, music, colour, painting, figuration and abstraction — closing, as always, with a hidden silhouette of the artist's studio.
