## How Gestalt explains perception

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L09 · 00:00–01:56

*5642 · U02 · L09 · 00:00–01:56*

Designed images use basic elements such as points, lines, outlines, direction, color, texture, dimension, scale and movement. The question is how to make these elements make sense to the viewer instead of looking like a jumble. Silvia introduces Gestalt, a school of thought that used psychological experiments to study how people perceive images. Its approach treats an image as many small parts that can be seen separately, while also recognizing that changing one small part affects the whole.

The first law she discusses is the law of pregnance (*pregnancia*). It says that images with fewer and simpler elements are easier to remember. A complex image is more likely to leave only one or two details in memory. Silvia briefly shows one image and then another; viewers are likely to recall more about the second even though it was visible for less time. The Bauhaus took this law into account. The familiar design maxim “less is more” is connected to the same principle. The unit resources include links and material for reading about the many other Gestalt laws.

## The mind closes incomplete forms

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L09 · 01:56–02:53

*5642 · U02 · L09 · 01:56–02:53*

The brain tends to complete images and close gaps, filling in details that are not actually visible. This is the law of closure (*cierre*). In the poster for *The Lobster*, Colin Farrell appears to be embracing someone even though no other person or complete embrace is shown; we see only cutouts of his face and arms, and the brain supplies the rest. A book cover can have separate geometric pieces that the viewer closes into one whole and reads as text in negative space. A poster by Paul Rand works similarly: its pieces are abstract on their own but become one image when seen together.

## Nearby elements form groups

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L09 · 02:53–03:45

*5642 · U02 · L09 · 02:53–03:45*

Silvia shows four groups of four squares and asks whether they look like four groups or 16 individual squares. The mind groups things that are close to one another. This is the law of proximity (*proximidad*), and she says it is a frequent source of design mistakes. Hotel signs can place several pieces of information together without making clear which belong together. In her example, a visitor looking for room 205 may not know whether to go left or right. Separating the information into horizontal or vertical groups would make it easier to find the room instead of ending up at the gym or pool.

## Figure and ground compete for attention

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L09 · 03:45–04:50

*5642 · U02 · L09 · 03:45–04:50*

The law of figure and ground (*figura y fondo*) says we cannot see both at once: one becomes dominant, while the other recedes. They can exchange roles, so what was figure becomes ground and vice versa. A black-and-white pattern of birds and fish lets viewers notice that either set can take the lead. The Carrefour logo similarly hides an arrow inside its C. The same idea appears in FedEx: an arrow sits between the E and X, although the captions call this the “Eiffel” logo. As with Carrefour, the viewer has to choose whether to see the letters or the arrow as the figure.

## Build a mark by switching figure and ground

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L09 · 04:50–08:17

*5642 · U02 · L09 · 04:50–08:17*

For a more involved exercise, create a shield using the initial of your name and build a figure around it. Silvia chooses S and explores what shape and background can emerge from the letter. In her first drawing, she leaves the shapes open at the top and bottom so the surrounding space has more room to work. She then tries other letters. A J suggests the handle of an umbrella, which could become an emblem for her friend José, who lives in northern Spain where it rains a lot. With another letter, she finds a little house by turning her head; that could be a logo for her friend Esther, who has just bought a house.

Figure and ground may not always be easy to distinguish, but the letter J gives Silvia a clearer result. Try your own initial, or a friend’s, and see what objects or forms it suggests. The task instructions are in the lesson activities. The point is to use the letter and its surrounding negative space together, applying the Gestalt law rather than treating the letter alone as the whole mark. The next lesson begins the project, putting the basic image-making concepts into practice.

### Key points (derived)

- Gestalt principles explain how viewers simplify, complete and group visual information.
- Figure and ground cannot usually be read as equally dominant at once, but they can switch.
- A letter and its surrounding space can combine into a memorable emblem.
