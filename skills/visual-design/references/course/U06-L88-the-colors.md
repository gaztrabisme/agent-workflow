## Sampling colors from the mood board

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L88 · 01:46–03:45

Of everything I see here, some colors clearly interest me more than others, and some feel like they'd work better than others. Logically, a color often comes to you from the flavor of the drink, the market, or the style — but let's run an exercise to identify new palettes and new colors we can use.

There's something in this green shirt that I find interesting, which we can also find in this dress — I keep touching on different shades of green. There's something in this pink that I find very seductive and enigmatic. Maybe yellow can be one of our colors too. There's something very interesting in the red of this London bus — here it came out a little dull, but we can cheat slightly and push it closer to red, the way it really is. There's something in these blues that run through these two references, which I find an interesting detail. Yellow shows up again here, a stronger yellow. There's something in this sand color too that could work for some of our tones. That's how we keep selecting different colors that could serve the project.

## Wrap-up

Anchor: 5642 · U6 · L88 · 06:38–06:48

Now that I've defined the color palette, I'll continue in the next lesson, where I'll move on to typography.

### Key points (derived)

- Revisit the mood board to source colors from what's already gathered, rather than inventing a palette from scratch.
- Online tools such as Adobe Color (monochromatic, complementary, analogous rules) generate quick starting combinations.
- Sample directly from your own references: a shirt, a dress, a bus, a tiled mosaic, a poster background.
- It's fine to push a sampled color toward its true real-world hue (e.g., a truer red) when the source photo came out dull or muted.
- The same base design elements can carry different palettes; each combination shifts the feel toward modern, retro, or vintage.
