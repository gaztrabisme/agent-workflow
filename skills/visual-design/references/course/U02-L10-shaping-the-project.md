# Lesson 10 · Shaping the project

## Define the project and choose a tool

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L10 · 00:00–01:30

*5642 · U02 · L10 · 00:00–01:30*

The final project is to create Instagram templates (*plantillas*) that give your own content—or content for a company or a friend—more personality. The goal is to put what the course has covered into practice by making nine images. Silvia will use the exercise for her own design studio, patio, whose work is playful. The first step is to develop the concept and imagery. Geometric shapes can represent ideas that are very abstract or very concrete, so use them for whatever suits the project.

The software itself is not the main concern. Choose the program that is easiest for you. Silvia will demonstrate Figma (captions: *Afigma*), which designers use for digital projects. It is free and runs in a browser without a download. The lesson resources also list alternatives, including Canva, if Figma seems complicated.

## Set up a page and learn the artboards

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L10 · 01:30–03:11

*5642 · U02 · L10 · 01:30–03:11*

When Figma opens, the recent files appear. Silvia already has a file for her studio’s posts; a first-time user can choose **New** and create a design file. She likes Figma because it is free, versatile and widely used by designers, while pointing to Canva in the resources as another simple option.

The interface has tools across the top, each of which opens more options. An important tool creates artboards (*mesas de trabajo*): small screens inside a page. A page in Figma is an infinite canvas, so you can create as many artboards as you need and move around without reaching the edge of a fixed canvas. Pages are listed below the tools. Silvia has one called Figma for demonstrating the program, and another for inspiration. To add a page, click the plus sign and give it a name.

## Collect varied visual references

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L10 · 03:11–06:57

*5642 · U02 · L10 · 03:11–06:57*

Silvia plans a system of about nine templates, enough to make a versatile Instagram feed. They do not need to contain the final content yet; it is enough to think about the kinds of posts she might publish. Some can be photography only, some text with graphic elements, and some a mix of photography and text. She wants this variety so she is not limited in what she can post.

She has gathered references for different ways of arranging text and photography. One example sets text in a circle; another combines text and photography subtly. Other examples are not close to patio’s desired look but still suggest ways to arrange words or keep a consistent color system. A further group inspires her through cheerful shapes and color. The studio’s projects are playful, so its graphics should feel playful too. She plans the same variety for her own nine posts: some text-only, some with text and images, and some using graphic elements to complement text without needing a photograph.

Silvia also looks for references that are not literal matches for the project. For patio, she is drawn to playful images: a ball pit at Color Factory in New York, which suggests sport and the familiar circle of a ball; semicircular playground structures where children interact with a geometric form; and art or painting that places circles in a more structured arrangement. These references help her start choosing both a graphic element—the circle—and a bright palette (*paleta*) of primary colors. Blue, green, white and red will be useful when she develops the colors later.

## Create nine Instagram artboards

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L10 · 06:57–08:18

*5642 · U02 · L10 · 06:57–08:18*

Silvia switches to her test page, renames it **Posts**, and chooses the artboard tool. Preset formats and resolutions appear in the right panel, including options for phones, tablets and computers. For this project, she opens **Social media** and selects an Instagram post, which is 1080 × 1080 pixels. The resulting square artboard is what she will later export as an image and upload as a post. She creates nine of these and arranges them in three columns so the page resembles an Instagram feed. She leaves this Posts page in place and will start a new page to explore shapes.

## Explore circle-based graphic elements

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L10 · 08:18–10:45

*5642 · U02 · L10 · 08:18–10:45*

Patio makes playful projects, so Silvia expects the circle to be useful. On a new page called **Formas**, she starts experimenting without deciding sizes or placing forms inside the Instagram posts yet. She creates a circle and watches how Figma adds an entry for it as a new layer (*capa*) in the layer list. A shortcut she uses often is holding Option, which shows a double arrow and duplicates the selected shape; copy and paste works too. She can also draw another circle.

The first possibility is to use a circle as it is. Another is to draw a rectangle and round its corners, making a useful container for text or other content. She also overlaps oval forms to create shapes that are not circles but still feel related to circular imagery. Finally, she can repeat a small circle many times, like confetti or the ball pit from her references. These four sketches give her starting points for the posts. In the later composition lesson, she will assign them a sense and order within the feed. For now, the forms remain loose and their sizes are not yet settled. With the posts planned and a style and geometry chosen, she next turns to typography.

### Key points (derived)

- The project starts with nine square Instagram artboards and a varied content system.
- Use references to find a visual direction, including references that are not literal matches.
- Explore graphic elements freely before sizing and arranging them in the final posts.
