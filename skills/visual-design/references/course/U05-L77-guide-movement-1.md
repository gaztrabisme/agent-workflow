## An unstable wheel that seems to advance

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L77 · 00:06–01:25

This last lesson takes up something that follows on from the previous topic: imbalance. Imbalance essentially creates energies, creates forces, and those forces in turn create visual movement. This can be applied, for example, when shapes carry an internal energy that sets up a particular relationship with the rest of the elements and creates a distinctive kind of movement.

In this piece, the visual centre is the wheel — an unstable shape, a kind of balance scale. On the right sit the wires; on the left, a straight piece that weighs a great deal and pulls with force in that direction, fully released. Visually it reads as a kind of contraption: something that seems to weigh as much on one side as the other, yet the relationship between the two gives the sense that it's advancing, that little by little it's moving, slowly progressing. There's a slight sensation of movement, provoked by this imbalance of shapes and by the internal forces the shapes themselves carry.

## Marey, repetition, and movement across history

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L77 · 04:09–05:03

Direct everyday visual experience generates movement, as discussed — but repetition, reiteration and the translation of shapes across a space create movement too. The photographer Étienne-Jules Marey, a French doctor fascinated by photography who experimented with it constantly, worked with movement, capturing how the human body moves through repeated exposures that traced and described the whole motion. That was a powerful, far-reaching experience with movement.

Movement has been a hugely important resource since ancient cultures — from Greece to the Futurists, by way of the Baroque. Achieving that sense of movement has been a fundamental pursuit throughout. This French doctor-photographer's experiments had a major impact on the avant-gardes that followed, starting with the Futurists.

### Key points (derived)

- Imbalance creates internal energy, and that energy reads as movement even in a perfectly still object or image.
- Movement can come from a shape's own internal forces (an unstable "balance," an open angle, a counterweighted arc), from everyday visual experience (wind, growth, the moving body), or from repetition and translation of a form across space.
- Historically, movement has been a central pursuit from ancient art through the Baroque to Marey's chronophotography and the Futurists (Duchamp, Balla), who used repeated, overlapping angles to fix motion on a static surface.
- The same perceptual principles apply across disciplines — bonsai, plated food, photography, sculpture, graphic design and architecture all use the same visual paths, angles and repetitions to move the eye.
- A visual path can be simple and singular (strongest, most immediate) or complex and combined/crossing (more interesting over time, but less immediately powerful).
- In graphic design, a controlled visual path is often used to lead the eye to a small but important element, such as a piece of text.
