# Lesson 65 · Personal projects

## Why personal projects: filling training gaps in a generalist market

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L65 · 00:00–01:13

Pepe Gimeno explains how he has gradually made up for the gaps in his training, through personal projects: work he develops outside any client commission. He has done this for several reasons. One is to get ahead of a future commission: the pace of day-to-day graphic design is so fast that you can never risk researching something for a specific job while the job is running. Everything has to be ready and rehearsed beforehand, so it can be applied the moment it is needed. He always tried to keep a stock of resources and techniques prepared in advance for whenever he wanted to use them.

This was also pushed on him by the market he worked in: the commissions available in Valencia at the time did not call for deep specialization, so a designer had to be able to turn their hand to almost any kind of work.

## Synthesizing drawings into a strong line: the Corts Valencianes brand

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L65 · 01:34–02:10

He also worked on synthesizing images: taking drawings, photographs or other source material and reducing them to a minimum, working with a very strong, clear, geometric line. In 1994 that practice let him take on the Corts Valencianes brand (he refers to it in speech as "Les Corts"), a very complicated job of translating a detailed image into a mark that can be reduced to a centimetre and still read clearly.

## Cut-paper collage: the Marlboro Jeans labels and the Sabrina display

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L65 · 02:10–03:14

Another long-running line of work was cut paper. He began working in collage, with coloured papers — this was 1984, before computers had appeared in the field. The technique let him play with straight cuts, torn edges, and the white patches left where a printed (not solid-coloured) paper tore, so a broken line sat against a hard one; he also found the bright colours very appealing. It pushed him, too, to simplify his images further. He used this technique for a series of labels and later for displays, such as one for Sabrina, also from 1986. Later, colleagues at his studio (*la nave*) even used the same technique for the highway signage that is one of the region's best-known public design jobs — no image of that particular application is shown.

## Working in three dimensions: the 1973 sculpture, music, and the Palacio de Congresos brand

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L65 · 03:14–04:38

In 1973 he also began working in three dimensions, in sculpture — a way of working he finds interesting in itself, quite apart from anything it is later used for. He recalls that studying music theory (*solfeo*) at school, and later playing in a *rondalla* (a traditional string ensemble) on the *bandurria* (which he pronounces as "UD" in the recording), gave him a lasting feel for musical language, even though he no longer plays. Music, he says, deals with time, movement, space and silence, which connects closely with white space in images: music and image share a great deal.

Working in three dimensions has served him in ways he never expected. A sculpture he made in 1973 turned out to be the basis for the Palacio de Congresos (Congress Palace) brand mark he made in 1998: that mark is based on the building's roof, which itself resembled the earlier sculpture, so the sculpture and the mark share essentially the same principle — a piece that rises and seems to float in the air.

## From brush numbers to the Caparrós poster

## Building a portfolio to be trusted with type: the EPP project

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L65 · 05:09–06:32

Personal projects also let him widen the types of work his studio could take on. He never had a typography teacher; he came to type-design self-taught, but he had always wanted to design a typeface and gain access to that world. As he puts it, in this profession nobody will commission work you cannot show you have already done — if you want illustration work, you need illustration samples first. So he set out to design a typeface, which he calls the EPP typeface (labelled on the posters as "Tipografía Pepe" — Pepe's typeface); its whole reason for existing was to add that kind of work to the studio's portfolio. The project won an excellence award from the Type Directors Club (of New York) in 2002 (mistranscribed in the captions as "TAI Directos") — beginner's luck, he says, for a first typeface. From there it opened the door to much more work.

## A commission from an admirer: the Zenobia typeface for Juan Ramón Jiménez

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L65 · 07:00–07:53

Another typeface grown from that beginning was commissioned by the Provincial Council (*Diputación*) of Huelva: a digital version of the mixed, personal lettering that the poet Juan Ramón Jiménez used when setting his own books. He was very fond of typography, and drew letters from three or four different type families interchangeably, sometimes alternating individual letterforms by taste. The studio had to research which families those letters came from, then collect and unify the characters into a single typeface, called Zenobia.

## From a commissioned mural to a personal fragmentation piece

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L65 · 08:20–09:01

Sometimes the relationship between commission and personal work runs the other way. His fragmentation work "In the blink of an eye" (*En un abrir y cerrar de ojos*) grew out of an earlier commission: a mural for a client the transcript names "R&B" [08:41] but whose mural title card spells "RNB", covering a wall of lockers broken up into three or four separate wall sections. Rather than a flat mural, they built a kind of kinetic piece, using the physical breaks between the locker doors to give it movement — an early step toward his later fragmentation work.

## Projects done purely because he wants to, and a closing piece of advice

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L65 · 09:01–10:41

Finally, there is a category of projects that nobody will ever commission — not because he lacks the material or samples to show, but because these are simply never the kind of work anyone commissions at all: pure research, things that interest him, that he wants to make, and that will only exist if he makes them himself for no return — though, he notes, everything eventually brings something else in its wake. The Silent Writing project, which he covers later, is one example.

![5642 · U5 · L65 · 09:47](frames/U05L65-F0947.jpg)

On screen: "Manifiesto emocional" "2009" (Emotional manifesto. 2009)  
Shows: a cardboard relief built from torn sections and short rolled-paper strips in yellow, red, magenta and green.

![5642 · U5 · L65 · 09:50](frames/U05L65-F0950.jpg)

On screen: "...en 59 fragmentos" "2015" (...in 59 fragments. 2015)  
Shows: a small still-life sculpture combining a white rod, an orange sphere, and a coiled length of rusted wire.

His work with waste material — the axis of this course — grows out of this same personal-project habit, starting from sculpture. His advice from this experience: always pursue the thing you secretly want to do. Don't hold back from making a typeface just because you wish someone would commission one from you — doing it yourself opens the path, even if it takes patience. If you start walking in a direction, the encounter eventually comes.

### Key points (derived)

- Personal projects (work done outside any commission) filled the gaps in a self-taught, generalist training and built a portfolio of demonstrable skills.
- Skills developed once — airbrush, cut paper, three-dimensional sculpture, brush calligraphy — resurfaced years later in commissioned work: the Palacio de Congresos brand grew directly from a 1973 sculpture.
- Type design was a self-taught personal project (the EPP/"Pepe" typeface) that won recognition and led to a family of commissioned typefaces (Roca, Lladró, Viu, Zenobia).
- Exhibitions and personal art pieces (fragmentation work, "Manifiesto emocional") are examples of work nobody commissions but that generates further opportunities once made.
- His central piece of advice: do the work you want to do without waiting for a commission; the opportunities follow, even if they take time.
