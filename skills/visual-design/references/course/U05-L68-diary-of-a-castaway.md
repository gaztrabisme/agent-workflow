# Lesson 68 · Diary of a castaway

## Outgrowing a single rigid format

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L68 · 00:00–01:02

The lesson opens with a question — "What happened with the University of Madrid project?" — that the transcript does not otherwise explain. Pepe Gimeno answers that he had no other way forward and could not do more: working with only one fixed format is very demanding, very difficult, very rigid.

When he finished the Grafía Callada project he was still very interested in waste material, but he needed more flexibility, more freedom of action, because he had many ideas and concepts he had not been able to realise. His layout notebook was still full of things he had never carried out.

## A freer project: "Diary of a Castaway"

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L68 · 01:02–02:12

So he began a new project — really a continuation of the previous one, but with different rules. It is less rigid about size: there is no fixed format, though there is a proportion, roughly an A4 or "A-series" ratio, used as a single sheet or a spread. He could even, which felt like a small revolution, start working with coloured backgrounds, something he had never done before. He began using materials that were not purely waste — things found in different places that were sometimes new rather than found. And this time there was no script: no continuous, sequential reading running through all the pieces. That is why it is called Diary of a Castaway (*Diario de un náufrago*) — a diary of day-to-day experience, an accumulation rather than a book with a single argument running through it.

## Two years of work, shown at a 2009 exhibition

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L68 · 02:12–02:40

He worked on this project from 2004 to 2009; what is shown here is the result of the exhibition he held in 2009. The project still makes some nods to text, but is mostly concerned with form: with the stain, the line, the expressiveness of shapes. Compared with Grafía Callada, he calls it less graphic and more artistic — at times even more figurative.

## Line, wire, nails and the "metal flower"

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L68 · 02:40–04:10

He walks through some of the individual pieces. One shows his interest in line: he was drawn to the strength of wire as a drawing tool, which reminded him of Picasso's engravings — that slightly rough, hard line carrying a very powerful inner force. He was equally interested in the power of branches, in the same way as the wire, and in using figurative pieces, such as a plastic leaf that configures the whole image into a kind of flower — he calls it the metal flower (*la flor metálica*). He was working, as always, right at the edge of figuration: an abstraction on the point of becoming figurative.

## Tension, spirals, and materials bought rather than found

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L68 · 04:10–05:20

He was also very interested in line and mark for their own sake: the tension that builds up, for instance, at the top of one piece, or how a simple spiral line carries so much movement that something this simple can hold your attention for several minutes. Other pieces are almost fully figurative, carrying a series of tonal values that amount to something close to a narrative. He liked playing with a concept on the left-hand page that suggests air, or the movement of something transparent, against a right-hand page that simulates a landscape — powerful clouds and waves. He liked the energy of branches, the way they twist, and their interesting inner tension; and he liked nails in particular — how they interact, the movement between them, the way they relate to and shift against each other, creating a kind of extraordinary dance (the nails shown in the wire piece above are an example).

## Text reconsidered: content and mood over alphabet form

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L68 · 05:20–06:25

Toward the end of the project, around 2008–2009, text interested him again, but from a very different angle. More than the text's form, more than the alphabet as such, what interested him now was the content of what these invented texts seemed to say — what they suggested, what they might tell. He cared more about the wrapping (*envoltorio*): how the page was composed and set, its colour and mood, its suggestion, rather than the literal shape of the alphabet.

## The emotional manifesto: text carried by colour and depth

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L68 · 06:25–07:40

These pieces led directly to Emotional Manifesto (*Manifiesto emocional*). Right around 2009 he discovered a way to make text from craft cardboard: stripping away the surface to create blocks of text, leaving the corrugated channels exposed. This continues the same idea as the pieces before it, but he pushed the text further, filling those channels with coloured plastic to give each block its own colour, even using drinking straws in one piece. The result creates movement — he thinks it matters how the movement of these text blocks, which read like flags, is resolved — and the mixture of blacks and colours gives the piece real depth.

Related to this is a piece he calls "the brochure to the microbial worlds" (*el folleto a los mundos microbianos*), where he began working with printed backgrounds — patterns he stamped and mixed himself — worked further with paint over the cardboard. There is always a concept of suggestion running through the whole block: these are empty texts, whose content and message stay implicit in the image itself.

## From a tiring, rigid material to the need for a brush

Anchor: 5642 · U5 · L68 · 07:40–08:32

He closes by noting that this new project, though perhaps shorter in duration, is much more extensive in the body of work it produced, and that its concept is clear enough. It leads directly to a further project culminating in The Green Bag (*El Green Bag*) — a process he finds interesting because his growing fatigue with rigid, physical material that he could not extend or scale up led him to an urgent need to work again with the brush and the paintbrush, at whatever size and in whatever format he wanted. He needed greater freedom, and that is what the next lesson covers.

### Key points (derived)

- "Diary of a Castaway" (2004–2009) loosened many of Grafía Callada's constraints: still an A4-based proportion, but no fixed size otherwise, coloured backgrounds, some bought and non-waste materials, and no continuous narrative — a day-by-day accumulation rather than a single book-length argument.
- Central recurring interests: the expressive power of line (wire, branches, nails) compared to Picasso's engravings, and pieces sitting right at the edge between abstraction and figuration.
- A late shift in the project (2008–2009) moved his interest in text away from the alphabet's literal form and toward the mood and content a page of invented text could suggest.
- That shift produced "Emotional Manifesto": text built from stripped craft cardboard, its exposed channels filled with colour (including drinking straws) to create movement and depth, followed by "the brochure to the microbial worlds," using self-made stamped backgrounds.
- Fatigue with rigid, fixed-size material drove him toward needing to paint again with a brush, at any size or format — the subject of the next lesson, The Green Bag.
