## Setting up a grid for the nine posts

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L21 · 00:00–02:16

Now that we know what a grid (*retícula*) is and have seen composition schemes that work, we can apply them to the project. I select the nine post artboards in Figma and use the Layout Grid control in the right-hand panel to add a guide. It starts as a field of small squares, much like graph paper. Because the posts and images are small, I switch to columns instead.

Figma starts with five columns. I choose six because I like grids that divide cleanly at the center, leaving three columns on each side. That is a personal preference; choose whichever grid suits your design. Figma also lets me choose the grid color. I keep its pale pink at 10% opacity. The gutter (*medianil*) is the space between columns; the default is fine. The margin starts at zero, which I keep because I like text to reach the edge of a post. We could use a larger margin, but this gives us the layout I want.

## Writing content for the templates

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L21 · 02:16–06:40

With the grid in place, I begin adding plausible content, even though these are templates I will change later. One post can show recent projects so I can share them on Instagram. When I put in several lines of text, the line spacing (*interlineado*) feels too open, so I tighten it to 140. For now, I am entering text first and will arrange it later.

## Aligning each design to the grid

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L21 · 06:40–14:00

Now I use the grid to align the posts. With no margins, it brings elements right to the post edge, a look I like for small posts. I turn the “New!” label into a sticker on a circle and rotate it slightly. Diagonals break the regular horizontal and vertical structure and add movement. I align the project photograph to the grid: it starts in the third column and ends along another column. It overlaps some text, which is fine; if I want the image behind the words, I move its layer below the text. The image now fills the third, fourth, and fifth columns.

For the service post, I select the ovals and labels and align them at the center. They were already tidy, so nothing moves. I notice a spelling mistake in another post and correct it. Its text is not centered, so I use the center-alignment control; the red guide confirms when it is centered. I do the same with the exclamation-mark post, which is too far right and has a mark sticking out. The grid also helps make the margins on either side more consistent.

I align the letters in “fun design” to the same height, then size the shapes to occupy consistent columns. I let some colored circles overlap letters like confetti and add a third color for more movement. I also align the F and D so they have a clear gap between them.

For a second project template, I change the text arrangement to give myself another option. With no margins, the text sits along the lower-left edge. I crop the white border from the photograph and make the image span three columns. I add a “New!” sticker like the one on the first project post and rotate it, then make it blue. Blue text did not work on red, but a blue geometric shape does. Across the next posts, I make the circles the same size and align their corners, checking the text against the red and horizontal guides.

One design uses a diagonal photograph to break the horizontal and vertical patterns. I align its text and use “just because” as temporary copy. I make the ovals a little taller and put the text in place. This post is very symmetrical, which gives it balance; the different colors in each word keep it from feeling dull. I align it to the vertical and horizontal center lines.

The final project template gives me a chance to try right alignment. That is often a design mistake, but at this small scale it can add movement. I keep the top and left edges flush with the grid, make the photograph four columns wide, and add a red sticker near the lower-left corner to counterbalance the visual weight at the upper right. The three posts are now composed, and I hide the grid to review them without the guides.

## Reviewing the feed and recalling the process

Anchor: 5642 · U2 · L21 · 14:00–17:46

Without the grid guides, the nine posts use the rule of thirds, the grid, and diagonals. There is also rhythm: some posts have white space, some are fields of color, and others use several colors. If I move a centered post off to one side, its weight pulls the composition over. I could scatter letters around for a playful effect, but centering the text keeps this post consistent with the other text-only designs. A left- or right-aligned version could also work, but I already have other posts with centered text.

Looking back through the project pages, I began with Instagram posts and other sources of inspiration, including places and artworks. Those references helped me choose colors, shapes, and arrangements. I made nine blank post artboards, then explored circles because they suited Patio: a single circle, ovals, pairs of circles, and loose confetti. I chose two typefaces, Circa with serifs and Flexa without serifs. For a post-sized design, the headlines are 200 points and the body text is 50 points. I made a palette that works together; white and a dark color help rescue combinations that otherwise have poor contrast or legibility.

Finally, I added plausible post content and arranged it on a six-column grid with no margins. I aligned shapes vertically and horizontally and used diagonals and the rule of thirds. While reviewing the phrase in the “fun design” post, I notice it is missing two Ds [the captions repeat “a D and a D”]. The posts are ready. I have left mockups and visualizations in the resources section so you can show us how yours turned out. That is the course content; I hope you enjoyed it. In the next lesson I will give you a summary of what we have covered.
