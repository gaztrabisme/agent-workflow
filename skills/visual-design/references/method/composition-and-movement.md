# Composition and movement: direct attention through the frame

Reader: an agent arranging forms, content, typography and images into a readable whole.

## Establish hierarchy before detail

- Composition organizes parts of a visual language into one whole. (5642 · U4 · L45 · 01:08–01:47; 5642 · U4 · L45 · 04:23–05:49)
- Review clarity, balance, contrast, unity and salience as qualities of the composition; the specific way each appears depends on the artifact and context. (5642 · U4 · L47 · 00:09–01:37)
- Choose the compositional register to fit the effect: contrast, hierarchy and diagonals can add energy; symmetry and tighter balance can support contemplation. (5642 · U4 · L44 · 00:49–01:35)
- Decide what is visually number one, two and three. Weight can come from size, contrast, position or a shape's own directional force. (5642 · U5 · L75 · 00:06–00:58, 07:37–08:27, 10:34–11:56)
- One dominant figure with subordinate elements reads more clearly than several competing focal claims. The eye follows contrast, so contrast can be made by adding to the focal element or removing from its surroundings. (5642 · U4 · L55 · 00:08–01:27; 5642 · U4 · L55 · 01:27–02:33; 5642 · U4 · L56 · 00:00–02:00; 5642 · U4 · L56 · 02:00–02:40)

## Make placement intentional

- Resolve ambiguous placement: centre an element exactly or move it clearly off-centre. Slightly unresolved alignment reads as an error. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 00:00–02:16)
- Balance perceptual forces, not only coordinates. A dark or high-positioned shape may need a lower or lighter counterweight; the Malevich example balances a heavy dark square with smaller, differently placed forms. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 02:16–04:28)
- Balance can remain active. Angles, curves, overlap, absence and the placement of forms within the frame add or counter energy. (5642 · U4 · L54 · 00:07–01:16; 5642 · U4 · L54 · 01:16–02:02; 5642 · U4 · L54 · 02:02–03:12; 5642 · U5 · L76 · 04:04–05:03; 5642 · U5 · L76 · 05:03–05:45; 5642 · U5 · L76 · 05:45–06:20; 5642 · U5 · L76 · 06:20–07:44; 5642 · U5 · L76 · 07:44–08:19, 13:52–14:54, 18:10–18:45)
- Symmetry reads as stable and contemplative; asymmetry lets content and negative space move more freely but needs consistency. A thirds grid is a flexible guide, not a compulsory target. (5642 · U4 · L58 · 01:45–02:58; 5642 · U4 · L58 · 02:58–03:45; 5642 · U4 · L58 · 03:45–04:37; 5642 · U4 · L59 · 00:00–00:32; 5642 · U4 · L59 · 00:32–02:14)

## Set rhythm, movement and space

- Sketch several very small thumbnails before choosing the layout. Their small size encourages plain shapes and a fixed text location before detail. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 02:56–05:07)
- Group elements into as few clusters as possible; the course prefers an odd number of groups so the gaps read as deliberate. Treat the preference as a compositional prompt, not a universal rule. (5642 · U4 · L53 · 06:29–07:11)
- Give a sequence rhythm by changing the density and scale of pages rather than filling every page in the same way. Review a set at thumbnail scale and with construction guides hidden. (5642 · U2 · L21 · 14:00–17:46; 5642 · U4 · L47 · 06:29–07:15; 5642 · U4 · L56 · 08:05–10:31)
- A diagonal, repetition, or a shape's internal force can guide the eye. Plan where the path begins and ends; a single path reads quickly, while a more complex path may hold interest longer. (5642 · U2 · L20 · 01:56–03:37; 5642 · U2 · L20 · 03:37–04:36; 5642 · U5 · L77 · 04:09–05:03; 5642 · U5 · L77 · 05:03–05:22; 5642 · U5 · L77 · 05:22–05:33; 5642 · U5 · L77 · 05:33–06:09; 5642 · U5 · L77 · 06:09–06:34; 5642 · U5 · L77 · 06:34–07:05, 09:00–09:22, 14:24–15:03)
- The distribution of space can use a full field, texture, a diagonal or a fragment the viewer completes; a deliberate path can also reroute the usual entry point. (5642 · U5 · L72 · 03:25–04:45; 5642 · U5 · L72 · 08:42–09:21; 5642 · U5 · L72 · 10:24–10:52; 5642 · U5 · L72 · 15:03–15:54)
- One image can carry several perceptual principles at once, and a path can delay its meaning until the viewer completes it. (5642 · U5 · L78 · 00:35–01:25; 5642 · U5 · L78 · 01:25–02:15; 5642 · U5 · L78 · 02:15–03:06)
- A fixed grid supports consistency across related pieces. Choose a grid that can accommodate the actual layouts, then fit content to its columns, margins and gutters. (5642 · U2 · L19 · 04:28–06:59; 5642 · U4 · L63 · 00:00–00:47; 5642 · U4 · L63 · 00:47–03:02; 5642 · U4 · L63 · 03:02–03:26; 5642 · U4 · L63 · 03:26–04:04; 5642 · U4 · L63 · 04:04–06:37; 5642 · U4 · L64 · 01:20–02:55)
- Negative space is not leftover area: use it to group, separate, pace and balance forms. (5642 · U4 · L49 · 02:06–03:47; 5642 · U4 · L49 · 03:48–04:52; 5642 · U4 · L49 · 04:52–06:43)

## Check before moving on

- The intended focal element and first reading are clear.
- Grid decisions are declared where consistency or alignment is required.
- Spaces group, separate or pace elements deliberately.
- The composition either balances around its frame or states its intended off-centre target.
- Run T4–T9. A failed grid returns to stage 4; a focal or path failure returns to hierarchy or concept; an unintentional space problem returns to stages 3–4.

Practice the teacher sets: photograph one small object using a high-contrast focal scheme, a diagonal and the rule of thirds, including text; then make a balanced grid collage from cut shapes.
