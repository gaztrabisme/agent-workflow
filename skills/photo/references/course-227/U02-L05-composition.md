## Choose a photo format

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:15

In this lesson I will give you a few tips to help with the initial fear of taking a photograph and to give you some basic ideas about composition. These are the most basic rules and essential points, but they will help when you begin to approach a photo.

The first thing to choose when composing a photograph is its format: vertical, horizontal, or square. Instagram lets us upload all three. A horizontal photo looks smaller because we usually browse Instagram on a phone, where the screen’s width limits the image. A horizontal photograph therefore takes up much less space than a vertical one, which can fill almost the whole screen. You can still use horizontal photos when you want a more intimate, delicate feel, but I generally recommend vertical or square formats.

## Place the subject on the rule-of-thirds grid

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L05 · 01:15–02:27

The most basic rule of photographic composition is the rule of thirds (*regla de los tercios*). Divide the photograph into nine parts with a grid of three columns and three rows. This works for vertical, horizontal, and square pictures. Many phones and cameras let you turn on a grid in the settings, so check whether that option is available when you open your camera.

What is the grid for? When we take a picture of, for example, our nephew, we tend to put him in the center. That is a familiar kind of photograph, and we are so used to it that it does not make much impression. If we place him at one of the grid’s intersection points, the picture becomes more interesting. The points that attract the most attention are the four places where the lines intersect. Putting our nephew at one of those points can make the photograph more appealing.

## Arrange objects, the horizon, and the frame

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L05 · 02:27–04:10

The same rule applies when we put objects into a composition. If the two most important things are your brand’s notebook and a pen from a new collection, for example, place them at two of the grid’s intersections. Less important objects can go elsewhere. A pair of glasses, for instance, can stick partly out of the frame, or another prop can enter from an edge. The objects placed at the intersections will attract the most attention.

The same idea applies to landscape photographs and any picture with a horizon line. Even the edge of this table is a line: it has less interest when it runs through the center than when it sits on one of the thirds.

There are two ways to make a photograph. In one, you create the scene: set up a background or other base and place objects after you have decided on the frame. The frame is what you see through the camera lens—what appears on your phone’s screen when you open the camera. In the other approach, you move the phone around and adjust the frame until you find the composition you want. One method moves the scene; in the other, you move yourself to find the frame.

## Practise the rule, then use symmetry

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L05 · 04:10–05:15

Keeping the rule in mind does not mean we have to use it every time. I recommend taking lots of photographs with the rule, especially when you are starting. That is how you develop an eye for attractive compositions and learn to break the rule later.

One way to break it is to do the opposite and use total symmetry. This can work when the subject itself is especially symmetrical and seems to call for a more creative composition. A classic example is a typical Nordic house (captions: *San Órdica*). It is perfectly symmetrical and has a very high roof. We take the center as the axis of symmetry, with the same amount of space and the same elements on both sides. If a fence surrounds the house, we should center that too.

## Use odd numbers and leave negative space

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L05 · 05:15–06:59

Another idea that may sound strange but works is to use an odd number of objects when we put several into a picture, especially when we are arranging the scene ourselves. If you are going to use two, use three; if you are going to use four, use five. If there are many things—say, a hundred stones—you do not need to count them. It tends to work when there are only a few.

One final rule, so to speak, is to respect negative space (*espacio negativo*). Putting our nephew on one side of the photograph leaves an open area that makes the picture more interesting. We can use the same idea in other kinds of frame: place him near the bottom, for instance, against a large white wall. All that breathing room above the subject can make an attractive photo. Pictures like this often work well on Instagram when we want to highlight an object without crowding the frame.

We can also apply negative space to an overhead photo on a paper background. Instead of placing the planner or notebook at one of the intersections, push it farther into a corner, with the pen here and the glasses there. The open area—whether it is white or the color of the background—can make a strong image. It also leaves room to use the photo in other places, such as a website or an Instagram ad, by adding some design.

## Try orderly layouts and balanced disorder

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L05 · 06:59–08:25

With these basic rules in mind, here are a couple of kinds of photograph that often work on Instagram and are easy to make. One approach is to arrange everything in an orderly way, like a grid, even if the objects are different sizes. Props and products should fit together like pieces of a puzzle.

I hope these tips help you get started. Above all, keep going: take as many photographs as you can, trying one rule and then another, one tip and then another. At first you may feel that you are copying what I am saying, but little by little you will loosen up and make variations of your own. Now that we know what to do before taking a photograph, in the next lesson I will introduce the brand I will work with throughout the course, and we will begin taking pictures.

### Key points (derived)

- Choose a format intentionally; vertical and square images occupy more of a phone screen.
- Practise placing important subjects at rule-of-thirds intersections, then experiment with symmetry.
- Use odd numbers for small groups of objects and leave open space when the image needs room to breathe.
- Try both orderly grid-like arrangements and carefully balanced disorder.
