## Choose a photograph and set its framing

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L13 · 00:08–01:24

In this lesson we’ll edit the photograph we took on the floor using Instagram itself. I have been using the Instagram app quite a lot lately when I publish, because editing less is increasingly in fashion and phones take better photographs every day. You do not need to edit them as much; a few small adjustments can be enough.

Instagram works well for this because it has the basic tools. To upload a photograph, open the app and tap the plus sign. To make a post, scroll through the gallery and choose an image, or tap Recent to switch albums. You can upload one photograph or several. If you choose more than one, you can make a carousel; we will not make one here. You also need to set the framing. If the photograph is horizontal and you want to post it as a square, tap the icon at the lower left to make it square. Then position it as you like, zooming in to make it larger if you want. When you are happy with it, tap Next to start editing.

## Open the basic edit controls and straighten the image

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L13 · 01:24–02:14

Instagram has filters. We used them a lot when the app first appeared many years ago, but they have fallen out of use. I recommend going straight to Edit. As we have done throughout, tap Adjust to find the perspective controls and make objects appear as straight as possible. For flat-lay photographs, I recommend correcting perspective in Snapseed because it is the best app for that. Here everything is fairly straight, but sometimes you take the photograph at a slight angle and everything looks disorganized simply because your viewpoint shifted. Correcting the perspective makes the arrangement look orderly. With that done, move to the next setting.

## Make small adjustments to brightness, color, light, and focus

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L13 · 02:14–04:45

These are the same adjustments again. Brightness is exposure (*exposición*): you can make it lighter or darker. This one would look a little better slightly brighter. Contrast adds or removes contrast. I’ll leave it as it is because I want to lighten the shadows a little, which I’ll do later. Texture, like Clarity, adds contrast to the texture. Do not overdo it; if you use it, use very little. This photograph does not need it.

Warmth is white balance (*balance de blancos*)—Temperature (*temperatura*). Notice that the yellow background is not the vanilla color we saw before; it looks more blue. That makes the color seem odd. This happens a lot with colored background cards. When a phone photographs a color, it tries to turn that color gray because it is making the photograph automatically, so the color comes out strange. It happens often with pink or orange backgrounds too: you look at the photograph and think it looks ugly, when all you need to do is correct the Temperature or white balance. You can also correct the magenta-green part. Here only Temperature needs changing. As soon as we add warmth, the background returns to the color it really was.

Saturation (*saturación*) makes the colors stronger or removes their strength. Fade (*atenuado*) removes contrast and makes the image very soft. Under Highlights and Shadows (*luces y sombras*), as in Snapseed, you can lighten or darken the highlights and do the same with the shadows. I’ll leave the highlights as they are, but I think the shadows would look a little better lighter. Vignette (*viñeta*) works the same way as before, darkening the edges; I normally do not use it. Blur (*desenfoque*) creates an effect in which the background, or one part of the photograph, looks out of focus. I personally do not like it when I want a natural result. It can work if you want an exaggerated artistic effect and want the blur to look deliberate, but it does not look realistic to me as a natural effect, so I normally never use it. Finally, add a little Sharpness (*nitidez*) if you want the photograph slightly more focused, and it will be ready.

## Finish the post and consider a faster editing app

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L13 · 04:45–06:04

If you want to publish the photograph, tap Next, write the text, and add the tags as usual. With what we have learned about editing, you now have a very good base for editing in whichever app you like, Instagram or another one. I use Instagram when I want to make very few adjustments or work very quickly, as we just did. There are many apps we could use. I would also like to recommend one more that I think is great and suggest that you install it and explore it: A Color Story. Its filters edit the photograph very quickly, so it is useful for an express edit. They are not analog-style filters like VSCO’s; they are designed more for people who do not know how to edit and automatically make adjustments such as lightening the photograph and adding the contrast it needs. Download it, explore it, and choose the app you like best—the one that lets you edit quickly and makes you feel most comfortable.

Now all the photographs are edited and ready to publish. In the next lesson I’ll show you how I publish them, because I do not usually publish them directly. I normally schedule them to work more efficiently.
