## Set the camera to its highest quality

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:27

Editing can feel almost magical: once we have taken and edited our photos, they often improve a lot. Still, the process is much easier when the original picture already has good quality. If we start with poor ingredients, the recipe will not turn out very well; photography works the same way.

First, configure the camera to take the largest picture it can. On Android, open the camera and look for the settings wheel, which in this case is at the top right. In the settings, choose image size (*tamaño de imagen*) and set it to the largest available number of megapixels (*megapíxeles*). Check the dimensions shown on the screen. On this phone, the maximum is 16 megapixels. This phone can also save RAW images (*imágenes en RAW*), so turn that option on too; it gives us the highest quality this phone can produce.

The iPhone steps are a little different. Go to Settings and find Camera. The available options vary between iOS versions. On this phone, open Formats and choose High Efficiency (*alta eficiencia*), because Most Compatible (*más compatible*) would reduce the image a little.

## Prepare the screen and avoid the flash and zoom

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L04 · 01:27–03:07

Just before taking a photograph, always turn the screen brightness all the way up. It is simple, but we have to remember to do it every time. On iOS, open Control Center and raise the brightness to the top. Android works in much the same way: pull the menu down and raise it all the way.

![227 · U2 · L04 · 01:50.00](frames/U02L04-F0150.00.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: Two phones lie on a table; the Android quick-settings panel is open with the brightness slider across its lower part.

Next, turn off the flash. Do not take pictures with the phone’s built-in flash unless it is absolutely necessary—for instance, if you need a picture of something in the dark to send to your aunt. For Instagram, never use it. The flash control is usually easy to find. On Android, it offers automatic, on, and off; choose off. On iOS, choose no rather than auto or yes.

![227 · U2 · L04 · 02:21.00](frames/U02L04-F0221.00.jpg)

Why turn it off? The flash comes from right beside the camera, so it flattens the photograph and removes interesting light. The comparison below shows the same cup and coffee maker with and without flash.

![227 · U2 · L04 · 02:40.00](frames/U02L04-F0240.00.jpg)

Finally, avoid zooming while you take a photo. Phone-camera zoom is generally not optical: it does not actually bring the subject closer, but enlarges the image. We can crop later in post-production and keep more quality in the original file.

## Clean the lens and steady the phone

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L04 · 03:07–05:17

Before taking a photo, clean the camera lens. We touch the back of the phone without noticing and leave dirt on the lens. In a picture, that makes ugly flares, softens the image, and reduces its sharpness. Every time we take photos, we should raise the brightness and clean the lens.

Use a jacket, shirt, or whatever you are wearing. Put a finger on the camera area and rub firmly. It is the simplest movement in the world, and the only one that will let you achieve quality. Once the lens is clean, we are ready to take a picture.

Hold the phone firmly with your other hand so it stays steady in the frame you have chosen. With the hand taking the picture, tap the shutter button very gently (captions: *Alborotón*), while keeping that arm very still. This gives us a hand-held tripod and helps prevent the usual blur from camera movement. To steady yourself further, hold your breath; then you can take pictures even in fairly low light without shaking.

If there is so little light that you need to keep the phone still, or if you want to appear in the photograph yourself, use a bottle of water or another fairly heavy object, such as a glass. Set the phone’s timer and rest it on the object as if it were a tripod. I usually ask someone to help me, but when I am alone, I put the phone on a table, shelf, chair, or whatever is nearby—even the counter of a bar. Set the timer, step away, and take the picture.

## Choose soft natural light outdoors

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L04 · 05:17–06:50

As I said, natural light is always best for phone photography. If there are lights on around us, we should try to turn them off. The light outdoors and indoors is different, so I will explain what works in each situation.

Outdoors, the main thing is to avoid very strong light. Try to take photographs early in the morning or late in the afternoon, when the sun is low and the light is softer. Very strong light creates a black shadow; this is called hard light (*luz dura*).

If we are outside and the light is hard, we can move into the shade of a building or another street, or find a tree. In the shade, the light is more filtered and diffused, and the phone can make a better picture. This does not mean that photographs in hard light are bad. Many work well for what we want to say. The difficulty is that a phone does not capture as much information in the very brightest and very darkest areas. One part of the image may lose detail. It is usually better for the shadows to go black, since we often do not need information there.

## Use hard light deliberately

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L04 · 08:07–09:31

What if we do not want soft light? Hard light can make striking pictures too, as long as we are looking for that contrast. These photographs were taken in full sun, many of them in summer. The shadow can become part of the composition, even the main subject. One photograph would be almost nothing without the shadow, which creates an easy diagonal as the sun begins to go down but has not disappeared yet. Those long shadows give us ways to play creatively with the composition. We can also use shadows to divide the image into areas of shade and areas of light.

## Find the best natural light indoors

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L04 · 09:31–11:20

Indoors, start by turning off every light. Even a small lamp affects the image and can add an ugly yellow cast that shows up in the shadows. Turn off small lights, lamps, ceiling lights—everything.

Next, find the room in your home with the most light. You can do this in a home, studio, shop, or any place where you can turn off the lights. Of course, we cannot switch off the lights in a café. First, though, examine the place where you usually work. Open all the windows, blinds, and curtains so that as much light as possible can enter. Then walk through the rooms. I recommend squinting a little so the room looks blurrier. We may look a little strange, but this helps us see which room has the most light.

Check at different times of day, too, and note which rooms get the most light at each time. Then you will know where to go when you want to take a photograph. If the kitchen gets much better light in the afternoon, go to the kitchen and take the pictures there. It is as simple as that.

If the best window in your home—or the display window of your shop—gets very strong sun, I recommend putting up a thin white curtain, such as a sheer, to soften the light.

## Work with the light in places you cannot control

Anchor: 227 · U2 · L04 · 11:20–12:14

In an interior that is not ours—a café, museum, or any other place where we cannot turn off the lights or move things as we please—we should reduce the effect of artificial light as much as possible. The best way to disguise it is usually to get as close as we can to a window. Then natural light becomes stronger and the artificial light matters much less.

These are the tricks for finding good light that you really need to learn and internalize, so that they become automatic when you take pictures. In the next lesson, I will give you several tips for beginning to compose photographs.

### Key points (derived)

- Set image size and display brightness to their maximum; enable RAW saving when available.
- Turn off the flash, avoid digital zoom, clean the lens, and steady the phone.
- Prefer soft natural light; seek shade outdoors and switch off indoor lights where you can.
- In harsh light, use shadows deliberately and protect detail in the sky.
- Find the brightest room for each time of day, or stand near a window in an unfamiliar interior.
