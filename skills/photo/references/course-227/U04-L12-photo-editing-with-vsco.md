## What VSCO offers for editing and inspiration

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L12 · 00:08–03:14

In this lesson I’ll edit the photograph we took at the bench with the app I like best for filters: VSCO. You already know Snapseed also has filters, but I do not find them as beautiful, and I do not usually use Snapseed for that. I use Snapseed to develop photographs and, above all, to correct perspective. It is my preferred app for perspective correction. For filters, I prefer VSCO.

When we open VSCO, you’ll see that part of it is paid and mainly unlocks more filters. I do not recommend paying for it straight away. I used the free filters for years and they worked very well; the tools are also excellent. I only started paying recently, basically because I’m a photography enthusiast and I work in this field. I recommend making the most of the free features first. If they are not enough, and you love the app and want more filters, then consider paying for it.

The interface is quite like Instagram, especially the similar layout along the bottom. VSCO is not only an editing app; it is also a kind of small social network. It is similar to Instagram, but many of its creators are photographers or videographers, so the content is very carefully made and quite professional. That makes it useful for inspiration. Tap the house at the far left to see a familiar Instagram-style feed, with posts from people you follow and suggested accounts. It is great inspiration, especially for editing, because people there edit very well.

The next icon is the magnifying glass. It works like Instagram’s Explore page, but here it is called Discover. There is a lot of inspiration. Many people post photographs taken with a camera and edited on a computer, but others upload photographs they edited in VSCO itself. If a photograph was edited in VSCO, the app may show a label below it such as “edited with P9.” That tells you which filter was used. I’ll check whether there is one without that label: this one says it was edited with B5 too. It seems that now they are all filtered. For example, this photograph has no filter, so it has no “edited with” label and filter name. When you like a photograph, check whether it was edited in VSCO and which filter it used; then you can imitate it. The filter may be paid, but at least you can look around and see whether you like it before deciding to pay. I strongly recommend looking at lots of photographs; it trains your aesthetic eye, and over time you will edit much better.

## Import a photograph into the private Studio

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L12 · 03:14–04:37

The next part of the app is the central one: Studio. This is where we import photographs to edit. A special feature of VSCO is that photographs stay inside the app as drafts. This part is not public; it is like your phone gallery, but with your VSCO edits. The public part is the face icon, which works like your gallery or Instagram feed. If, for example, I edit a photograph and like it a lot, I can tap Share and choose VSCO, and it will appear in my public gallery.

The last part of the app, on the right, is Spaces. It is an extra gallery feature for people who pay. It has just launched and is in beta, so we do not know whether it will continue. I’m showing it to you; if you like it, fine. I don’t use it. To import a photograph, tap the plus sign at the top. You can select it directly from the gallery or, as I do, find it in one of your albums. Choose one photograph, or select several if you want to edit a group, then tap Continue. The photographs are sent to Studio. Select the one again and either double-tap it or tap Edit at the bottom.

## Compare filters and choose their strength

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L12 · 04:37–08:37

The first thing that appears is the filter section, the star feature of VSCO. The available filters appear at the bottom. I have more because I pay now, but, as I said, I used the free ones for years. A filter changes the photograph’s color, contrast, and brightness, making it look different and, in this case, more beautiful. What is special about VSCO is that its filters imitate old film rolls from analog cameras. The name of a filter may be the name of the film roll it came from; for example, this one is Agfa Ultra 100. Some filters, such as 01 and 02, are not film rolls but continue the same style. The Spanish captions render the name as *HAKFA Ultra 100*; the filter label on screen reads “Agfa Ultra 100.”

When I choose a filter, I look through them and pick the one I like. That may sound absurd, but there is no more professional way to do it; this is how all of us professionals do it. I go through them and see which one suits this photograph. For an image with many details, I like to tap through each one and look closely. With a photograph like this one, which has fewer elements, I prefer to compare them more quickly. Tap the thumbnail at the lower left to change how the filters are displayed. You can move through full previews one by one, or see them smaller for a faster comparison. I never use the very small view because I cannot get a good sense of the image that way, but use it if it works for you. I normally use the larger previews.

I look at the tones to see what suits the image. For example, this filter does not work for how I want to edit this photograph because it makes the wood too green and yellow. To compare the photograph before and after, press and hold at the bottom of the screen, as in other editing apps. When I find one I like—for example, this one gives it a slightly colder, wintry look—I never apply it at full strength. Tap it a second time and lower the strength. At maximum, the effect is too exaggerated. Also, when all the photographs are together in a gallery, full-strength filters can make them look unrelated, as if each one came from a different person. Reducing the strength gives the set a more consistent, professional look.

If I liked the photograph at this point, I could tap OK and export it. I want to edit it a little more with the tools, and show them to you. One last filter tip before that: VSCO’s black-and-white filters are very beautiful. If no filter works for a photograph—perhaps the light is strange, you dislike the colors, or a person walked past in a red shirt that does not fit—you may be able to save it with a black-and-white filter. That does not mean every black-and-white photograph is a way to save a bad photo, but it is a great trick for the ones you like least.

## Set the editing order, straighten, and crop

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L12 · 08:37–11:32

Do not become obsessed with exact settings that someone else used. If you see a tutorial that says they set exposure to plus three or applied a filter at six percent, do not fixate on that. It depends on the photograph. Here I might like plus six, plus five, or plus seven; it is a matter of taste. Tool settings are the same, as you’ll see.

When you tap Tools, VSCO shows them in an order that you can change to suit your own editing workflow. Put the tools you use most at the beginning to work more efficiently. To change the order, tap the three lines with the star at the bottom right and move the tools around. When you are happy with the order—as I am with mine—tap Save.

Now we’ll edit with the tools, beginning, as always, with perspective and cropping. In this app, those controls are under Adjust. First I’ll adjust the perspective a little, because the bench looks tilted, as if it is falling to one side. Under Tilt you will find the perspective adjustment. We already know how to move the horizon this way or that way. The bench is straight, so it shows clearly how the control works. Since its lower corner sits lower, I’ll pull it the other way to straighten it, using the grid as a guide. I would not change vertical perspective in this case. That control is usually useful for buildings and similar subjects, but here everything is fine. If you change something and want to restore the original, double-tap the setting to reset it to zero.

The tilt looks right to me. I’ll crop to 4:5 so the photograph can be posted directly to Instagram. I like the image both this way and with a tighter crop; that would also work very nicely.

## Adjust exposure, contrast, and white balance

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L12 · 11:32–13:10

The next tool, underexposure and overexposure (*sub y sobreexposición*), is for making local adjustments, similar to a paid feature in Snapseed. You can do that in Snapseed, so there is no problem. Next is Contrast. I think I’ll leave it as it is—or perhaps raise it a little. No, I won’t. This is why it is best to try things: sometimes you think one option will be prettier, then try the opposite and prefer it. Keep testing. Adding contrast gives the image more character by darkening the shadows and brightening the lights. Lowering it makes everything softer.

I like VSCO’s White Balance (*balance de blancos*) tool because its correction is very subtle. Other apps can turn a change in tint very pink or green and make it look false. Here, I have noticed from the beginning that the wood looks too green. I want it to have the more orange tone it had originally, so I’ll add pink to counteract the green. I’ll also make it a little cooler with Temperature. The image is already cool; I’m doing this for an artistic touch, to give it a slightly more wintry feeling.

## Refine texture, shadows, skin tone, and grain

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L12 · 13:10–16:31

For me, the photograph is already finished, but I’ll show you the other tools. Saturation makes the colors stronger or grayer; I’ll leave it as it was. Sharpening (*perfilar*), called Define here, adds a little sharpness. I can add a small amount before exporting. Clarity (*Claridad*) adds contrast to texture and small details. When you raise it, texture becomes more visible. Like sharpening, this setting should be extremely subtle—never use too much. Here I would add a little to bring out the wood’s texture, which looked rather flat, but not enough to look false. This tool is forbidden in portraits for me, unless you want to emphasize every detail of someone’s face. Usually the person being photographed does not like that because it is unflattering.

Tone (*Tono*) provides a Highlights and Shadows adjustment, but here you cannot brighten the highlights or darken the shadows. It only lets you darken the brighter parts or lighten the shadows; you cannot push them in the other direction. In this case I’ll raise the shadows a little. I also like the Skin Tone (*Tono de piel*) tool. It changes the skin’s orange tone, and it affects wood and similar colors too. It can make them redder or more orange by reducing or adding red. It is useful if a photograph looks cold and your skin has become too red: move the setting the other way to correct it and get a nice skin tone. This photograph has so many wood tones that you can clearly see the change. I like it a little redder here, so I’ll move it slightly to the left.

Vignette (*Viñeta*) darkens the edges. Personally, I do not like it and never use it. Grain (*Granulado*) adds the grain of old photographs. It looks beautiful, and VSCO does it very well, so I recommend it if you like it. If you have a photograph you do not like or do not know how to edit but want to keep as a good memory, black and white with grain is the perfect trick. Applying grain can take a moment. After making the adjustment, wait for it to render before judging the result; if you move the control up and down too quickly, you will not see the difference. I’ll leave a little grain here because I feel like adding it. That is the problem with looking at every tool: you want to add everything. Check carefully; sometimes less is more. I am adding only what I think suits the photograph.

## Fade, split tone, and save the edit

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L12 · 16:31–18:15

Fade (*Difuminar*) makes the photograph soft overall, lowering the highlights and raising the shadows; it flattens the image as if you had lowered contrast. Split Tone (*Dividir tono*) adds one color to the shadows or another to the highlights. For example, I can add reddish color to the shadows and see the difference, or change them to blue. I do not use it much because it looks exaggerated even when reduced. I prefer VSCO’s filters, which are more complex: they do not put a single color in the shadows but use many tone variations that affect different parts in different ways and look more professional.

I do not use the other paid tools, so if you have the paid app, go in and see whether you like them. The Premium section also has effects, such as other kinds of grain and light effects. If you like those, there are many free apps you can explore too.

I like the photograph as it is, so I’ll save it. Tap Next. There are three options, but I normally use two: Save Draft, which keeps it in Studio but not in the phone gallery; or save it to the phone gallery and as a draft. I usually choose the second because I want to export it for posting. The edited photograph then appears in Studio.

## Copy edits across photographs from the same set

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L12 · 18:15–20:06

One thing I love about VSCO is that you can copy edits from one photograph and paste them onto another. I’ll show you with photographs we made in the Space Ukraine location. Edit one photograph, select it, tap More, and choose Copy Edits. This is useful when you have taken many photographs in the same space or the same set—for example, when you set up a piece of paper and take several photographs. The lighting, background, and other conditions are usually the same, so those settings will work and the photographs will come out nearly perfect. Choose where you want to paste them, tap More and choose Paste Edits. Now all of the photographs are edited. I like these as they are. If you do not, open one and change whatever you want with the tools, adjust the filter strength, or make another change.

Remember, copying and pasting works best when the photographs have the same conditions. If you copy settings from one photograph and paste them onto any photograph with different conditions, it usually will not look as good; it would be unusual for it to work well. That is why I recommend editing each photograph individually. Do not download filters and apply them indiscriminately, because the photograph usually needs other adjustments and has different conditions from the image that the person who made the filter used as a reference.

I use this a lot when I post several similar photographs together in a feed carousel, or when I make a Story with several photographs. I also use it for Reels when I edit several similar photographs and then make a Reel talking about something. That is all for VSCO. In the next lesson we’ll see how to edit your photographs in Instagram itself.
