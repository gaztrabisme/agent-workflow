## Select and open the photograph

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L11 · 00:08–01:38

We will start editing photographs on a mobile phone with Snapseed, an app from Google. I’ll explain the editing process I usually follow with this app and others; I tend to use the same order each time. Before editing any photo, select the images you want to work on. On iPhone and Android, you can open a photograph, tap the heart to mark the ones you like best, and find them later in the Favorites album. I go through the pictures and mark the one I like most from each kind of shot. Once they are marked, I open Favorites; those are the photographs I want to edit.

To open one, tap the plus sign in Snapseed, find Favorites, and choose a photograph. I’ll edit the backlit image we discussed earlier, in which we exposed for the interior. Snapseed has many filters and options, but I especially like its Tools because they include perspective correction, corrections to parts of an image, and selective adjustments. It is a very complete set of tools.

## Correct the perspective first

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L11 · 01:38–04:12

I always begin by correcting the perspective (*perspectiva*). Open Tools, choose Perspective, and first tilt the photograph so the vertical lines are straight. Then start correcting the perspective itself. Choose Rotate and move two fingers in a turning motion. Use the grid on the screen to check that any horizontal lines are level; the architecture in the space can guide you. If there is no horizontal line, use verticals. Try to use lines near the center of the image, because lines farther out are more distorted. Perspective correction is easier when the subject is photographed straight on, such as a wall.

After tilting, open Perspective again and choose Free. Pulling a corner lets you distort the image in that direction until the lines are straight. In this photograph, compare the iron line with the grid: it sits above the grid line in one place and below it in another, so it is tilted. Pull the corner upward little by little. More of the grid appears as you pull, which lets you make the correction more precisely. The upper line was bent too, but there was no nearby grid line to compare it with; after pulling the corner, it becomes parallel to the grid.

When you stretch the image, Snapseed’s “smart” fill invents the missing areas. It has filled in what it thinks should continue at the right, where the lamp appears, but it has not done that well there. Choose white or black for the fill so you can crop away the extra area later. Press OK once the horizontal and vertical lines look right. The perspective is now corrected. I leave the black part until the end, because I always try to crop after all the editing, in case I want to change something or remove a correction.

## Develop the image with curves and basic controls

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L11 · 04:12–06:57

Once the perspective is corrected, develop the photograph by adjusting how light or dark it is, its contrast, and the other settings that make it lighter, darker, more contrasted, or less contrasted. Open Tools, and I’ll quickly explain Curves. When we edit in Photoshop after taking a photograph with a camera, Curves is a tool we often use. It has a graph: moving the curve up or down makes the photograph lighter or darker. The upper part of the line represents the lighter areas, and the lower part represents the darker areas. Raise the whole curve to increase the exposure. To add contrast, raise one part to make the lights lighter, add a second point, and lower it so the darks stay dark. This S-shaped curve is the classic contrast curve in photo editing. I could leave the image developed this way, but I’ll cancel and show you another method.

In Tools, choose Tune Image. In most Snapseed tools, swiping up or down reveals the available settings; after choosing one, adjust it by moving left or right. Brightness changes the exposure: moving right makes the photograph lighter, and moving left makes it darker. Snapseed is quite sensitive, so gradual movements produce subtle changes and allow for a more precise edit. I would lighten it about this much and make the rest of the changes with contrast, perhaps a little less. Holding a finger on the photograph shows the before and after: this is how it looked, and this is how it looks with the adjustment.

I tapped OK, but I did not need to. Inside Tune Image I could have swiped down to Contrast and moved straight to the next setting.

## Adjust contrast, color, light, and detail

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L11 · 06:57–10:04

In Contrast, moving right adds contrast and moving left reduces it; I’ll add a little. Saturation makes the photograph grayer or increases the strength of its colors. The red in this image is interesting because nearly everything else is black and white, so I’ll strengthen it a little. With interior photographs, desaturating often looks good because it removes visual noise—that is, the amount of things and information in the photograph. This particular image is almost monochrome, apart from the greens and that red, so a little more color works well here.

Ambiance adds contrast to the small details rather than applying general contrast, making those details stand out. You can see more contrast on the right and less on the left. At the far left it looks as if a halo is left behind. For photographs of interiors and similar spaces, a small amount works well, but too much creates a pronounced effect. Highlights affect only the strong light, the whites: they can lighten or darken the whites. They can sometimes help recover a nearly blown-out sky; if it is already blown out, the tool can only turn the white into light gray. Shadows do the same for the dark areas, blacks, and shadows. When I lighten a photograph a lot for Instagram, I often lower the shadows to deepen the blacks and give a light image some points of contrast. Here, the blacks are already dark enough, so I will not lower them further. I never adjust Warmth here because Snapseed has a dedicated White Balance tool that works very well. Warmth makes the image yellower and warmer or bluer and cooler.

Next, open Details. Structure adds contrast at an even smaller scale, mainly bringing out texture. For example, it can strengthen the texture of a table. Sharpening makes the photograph look more focused and crisp. As with the other settings, add only a little: it can make the image look higher quality, but too much makes the processing conspicuous. The captions render her last phrase here as *se nota la lengua* (“the tongue shows”), which does not fit the explanation; she is warning against an obvious, overdone result.

## Set the white balance

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L11 · 10:04–11:36

To edit the white balance (*balance de blancos*), open White Balance. It lets you make a photograph warmer or cooler. In addition to Temperature, which shifts between yellow and blue, Tint shifts between green and magenta. To get a correct white balance, adjust both values. You can move Temperature manually. Manual adjustment is also useful when you want an artistic effect: if you are telling a wintry story about being very cold while drinking coffee, for instance, lower the temperature to create that feeling.

Here I want a correct white balance, matching the material’s real color. The eyedropper samples a neutral tone in the photograph, such as a white wall, gray floor, or anything black, white, or gray without a color cast, and makes it completely neutral. I’ll put it on this light gray floor. That completes the white balance; press OK.

## Make local corrections

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L11 · 11:36–14:31

Snapseed’s local editing (*edición por zonas*) is one of its great tools. It lets you apply a setting we have seen—saturation, contrast, or exposure—to one area of the photograph. It can help when one area is too dark and you want to lighten it, or when artificial lights create a yellow patch in a part of an otherwise pleasing image; you can select that area and lower its temperature.

There are two ways to make these local adjustments: Brush and Selective. Brush lets you choose exactly where the adjustment applies, but I find it stronger and less subtle. Selective uses a gradient, so the local correction is less noticeable. With Brush, choose the setting—exposure, temperature, or saturation—set whether to raise or lower it with the arrows, and paint over the area you want to affect. I’ll cancel because this photograph does not need it, but that is how the brush works for exposure, saturation, or whatever you need.

For the Selective adjustment, tap the plus sign to add a point. The point fades outward, and you can pinch to change its size and choose the area to edit. The center is the middle of the selected area. The useful thing about Selective is that it behaves like a smart layer mask: it selects the area without crossing into a nearby area with a very different color or tone. Here I am selecting the wall without affecting the adjoining windows. Set the area you want to affect, then swipe up or down to choose Brightness, Contrast, Saturation, or Structure. For example, lower the saturation to make the area gray instead of slightly blue, or increase it in the opposite direction. I’ll cancel because this photograph does not need that adjustment either.

## Remove distractions, crop, and export

Anchor: 227 · U4 · L11 · 14:31–16:48

Another important Snapseed tool is Healing (*Quita Manchas*). It removes a part of the image that you do not want. I would not use it on this photograph, but I’ll show how it works before using it on a second image. When you zoom in, use two fingers to move around; a single-finger touch acts on the tool. To remove the lamp in the corner, paint over it with one finger and Snapseed removes it. The dark gradient that appears comes from the area we corrected in Perspective and will crop out, so it does not matter. Apply the correction and the lamp disappears.

The editing adjustments are finished; only reframing and exporting remain. Choose Crop to select the format you need. For an Instagram Story, the format she identifies here is 16:9, which is already selected. Cropping it first lets you upload it without letting the app crop it wherever it chooses. For a vertical Instagram photograph on Android, I recommend 5:4, because Instagram will also crop it to 5:4. On iPhone, photographs are already made in that format, so it is not necessary. Instagram Stories works the other way around: it makes the photograph in 16:9, so you do not need to crop it for a Story. You can also choose a horizontal or square format; square is Instagram’s classic format. I’ll make this one square. Make sure the black area from the perspective correction is not in the crop; reduce the framing a little and accept. When finished, tap Export and tap Export again to save the final photograph to the phone’s camera roll.
