## Reduce the first batch and preserve variety

Anchor: 669 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–03:14

In this lesson we’ll work on selection. We’ll decide which photographs to keep and which to discard from all the ones we’ve edited. Let’s begin.

Here are all the images we exported from Lightroom, already edited. There are a lot of them: 216. The idea is for this first selection to leave us with about 80, or a little fewer. That gives us the best photos from each space. When we look through them again or want to choose one or two from a particular space, we’ll have a much smaller group to choose from. As time passes, we’ll fall in love with different ones, until we arrive at the ten favorites from the whole session.

I think it’s important to give this process time and leave room for the images to cool off; we shouldn’t look at them for a few days. Today we’ll make the first selection, which we can work from for whatever purpose we use the photos for. I’ll look through the photographs and put a label on the ones I like best.

This first one works for me, so I’ll give it the orange label. We’ll work with this color. I also like that one. In each space, I try to choose very different perspectives of each scene, from very close to very far away, and many kinds of gestures. This is where we may find a story behind the photographs. Together, all the photos will tell us much more than one photo alone.

These work very well; I’ll mark this one. I noticed that the smoke shows much more clearly in the other photo (the Spanish captions read *huevo*, “egg”). In the end, this is purely intuitive. You can see that her eye is very well defined here. In the other photos, the smoke comes through especially clearly. Once we see them all and compare them with the rest, we can judge them better. I love this one because it looks as if she has the teeth of a skull. I like this one a lot too.

Now we’ve finished the window photos. We’ve chosen about ten, far fewer than we had. Next are the photos through the glass.

## Compare the glass and reflected-light photographs

Anchor: 669 · U4 · L11 · 03:14–06:44

I think I’ll keep this one from the glass photos. Perhaps one in black and white too. There are many photos that are practically the same, and that’s where it’s hardest for me to choose, because the differences are really small. Here, for example, Andrea is much more centered and her position is more relaxed through her shoulders and head. In the earlier photo she is a little farther off-center, with her head more tucked toward her shoulders, as if she were lower down. This one tells me more because I see her as more decisive. But in the end it’s a matter of intuition and seeing which one we connect with more. Of these first ones, I might keep this one; the gesture seems sweeter and more sincere to me.

Now we’re looking at the reflected-light photos. I love this one and the way she looks in it; I like that the orange light is quite visible and draws attention. It often helps to look all the way through the photos and then go back to see which ones you left out, because you can’t really compare until you’ve seen them all. I might keep this one too.

Next we’ll continue with the light from the CD, which makes these lines. This one works very well for me. Andrea is off to one side, but I really like this position. She looks very honest; her hair makes her look comfortable, and I see less of the camera. In this one I like that she seems to be looking back, as if searching for something.

Now we’ll move to the black-and-white photos. Here we have three images that are practically the same. That’s the selection. We can see which ones we kept; these are all the images we’ve selected to keep. Let’s see how many there are: 63. Perfect. Starting from these 63, I save them separately or put them on Drive so I can look at them on my phone. Then, whenever I want to choose one of Andrea’s balcony photos, I might have five to choose from, and it’s easier to find one I fall in love with.

We gradually reduce the group of 63 to our ten favorites. Still, I don’t think this can be done all at once. We need to digest the photos, forget about them, and let them surprise us. For now, I think this first selection is more than enough. Each time we return to the photos to use them for something, we can see which ones affect us most and which ones we connect with most.

We’ve made the selection and seen which photographs to choose, which ones we separated from the rest because they may not work as well, and what criteria we used. The next lesson will be about what to do with our work and our photographs once we have a personal body of work.

### Key points (derived)

- Make a first selection that keeps variety across spaces, gestures, and viewpoints; let the photos rest before narrowing further.
- Compare similar images together and choose the gestures and details that make a set richer.
- Treat the first pass as a working group: here 216 exported images became 63, which can later narrow toward ten favorites.
