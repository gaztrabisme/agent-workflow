## Choosing a place and arranging the room

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–00:56

669 · U03 · L08 · 00:00–00:56

In this lesson I show how I tried to get the most out of an interior space. After leaving the window area, I started using some of the furniture in the living room. I noticed a blue armchair and kept it in the corner, then moved the other furniture away: another armchair, a table, and other things. I left a small palm tree behind the armchair because its color worked well. Once I had decided on this arrangement, I moved the armchair closer to the window because I liked the patches of light it made.

## Testing hosiery in front of the lens

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L08 · 00:56–02:51

669 · U03 · L08 · 00:56–02:51

With Andrea sitting in the armchair, I wanted to try something. I took a bodysuit made of hosiery, black hosiery, although I prefer a flesh-colored one because it gives a more natural effect. I put the hosiery in front of the lens and got a much softer, slightly blurred photograph. I tried it for a while, but I realized I was not fully convinced by it and that the photograph worked much better without the hosiery. I took it away and spent some time framing Andrea without it, with the lovely armchair in the living room.

Along with the side light the armchair got from being in front of a window, I opened the window behind it because I liked the backlight (*contraluz*) it gave me. Just before taking those photographs, I switched from the Sigma 50 mm f/1.4 I had used for the previous shot to the Sigma 35 mm f/1.4. Because this was a much wider shot, the 35 mm let me include many more things that interested me.

Andrea's dress seemed to me to have an older woman's style, even though it was very beautiful. I suggested she make her gestures more natural and fresher, so that a more organic attitude and air would balance that slightly older aesthetic. In the end I gave the hosiery another chance because I did like its blur, even though I had not yet found a framing that worked for me. I moved a little closer and then found a look I liked much better. I had to switch to manual focus (*enfoque manual*) because the autofocus went a little crazy with the hosiery, but focusing manually worked very well.

## Working with the mustard sofa

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L08 · 02:51–04:01

669 · U03 · L08 · 02:51–04:01

After that shot, I noticed a very attractive mustard-colored sofa in the living room and decided to try including it in the photographs. We changed Andrea's outfit. She put on a slightly old-fashioned shirt in a dark blue-black color, with gold threads, tied just below the chest. With the Sigma 35 mm f/1.4 I took photographs from above, almost overhead shots, and tried to play with the colors. The 35 mm, being a little wider than the 50 mm, let me take those shots without moving too far away.

To light the photographs well, I moved the sofa away from the wall at the back of the living room and as close to the window as I could. I also opened the side window to fill the shadows. Once the space was very well lit, we took the photographs. If we had made them directly against the wall, they would have looked much flatter and less defined.

## Discovering the portrait in the mirrored table

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L08 · 04:01–05:56

669 · U03 · L08 · 04:01–05:56

Finally, I focused on a table with a square mirror on top. It was quite dirty and old, and I was fascinated by it, so I saved it for last. Because it held my curiosity and attention, I removed the other things in that part of the living room: the armchairs, plants, and other tables. We even moved the sofa a little to make a clean, well-lit corner. Once the space was clear, we put the mirrored table there and cleaned the mirror a little. Andrea knelt and leaned against it.

When I had her in place, I noticed that if I took the photograph from a slightly overhead angle, the Andrea I saw reflected in the mirror looked like a normal portrait of Andrea standing up. I spent time exploring that, because I think it is great to see how photographs reveal themselves to us when we play with the elements around us. Curious things happen that we might not have known how to make if we had set out to plan them.

I played with the portrait of Andrea that appeared in the glass tabletop. Then I had her lie down in a gentler way so I could move closer to her face and its reflection. I continued until I felt we had photographed the image from all the angles I had thought of. The light for the photographs at the mirrored table came from the side door, which was already open, the side balcony, and the rear balcony. That backlight made the illumination a little dreamier.

## Following an unexpected rainbow

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L08 · 05:56–07:00

669 · U03 · L08 · 05:56–07:00

While I was taking the living room apart and putting the furniture back, I moved the glass-topped table right beside the balcony. Suddenly I noticed that the sun coming through the balcony was bouncing off the glass table and making a rainbow on the door of the other balcony. It amazed me. Many things appear as a surprise, as I said earlier: you do not know there is a photograph there until there is one. I put Andrea on her knees there too and took photographs with the rainbow that had appeared on her face and in her eye. I played a little with the colors, especially the color of her eye, to see what we would get.

I encourage you to play and investigate with the furniture and spaces in any apartment or house. Even if a place seems incredible, I am sure it has corners, windows, or a piece of furniture that suddenly makes a great photograph. That is the end of photographing in an interior space. In the next lesson I show how to make photographs with several elements.
