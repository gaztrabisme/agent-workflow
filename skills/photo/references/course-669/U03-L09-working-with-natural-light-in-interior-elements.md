## Reflecting sunlight with a golden coaster

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:43

669 · U03 · L09 · 00:00–01:43

In this lesson I show how to use different things we all have at home to approach photographs in a more creative way. The first thing I used was a golden coaster. I was photographing Andrea while I stood on the balcony and she was just inside the house, with the balcony doors open. We got a very clean photograph in which the person looked as flattering as possible. Even so, it bored me because it seemed quite standard, and I wanted to give it a more interesting, artistic feel.

I noticed the golden coasters beside us. With one, I reflected the sun that was reaching me from the balcony onto Andrea's face inside the house. For this to work, I had Andrea sit farther back inside the house, and I closed the other balcony doors in the living room. That made Andrea as dark as possible inside, so the light from the coaster, which was reflecting the sun, showed much more strongly and clearly on her face. Suddenly there was a golden patch of sun on Andrea's face. I found this very interesting, and we spent a while exploring what we could make with it.

## Shaping a reflection with a CD

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L09 · 01:43–03:15

669 · U03 · L09 · 01:43–03:15

Once I had finished with the golden coaster, I decided to try the same setup with a music CD, something everyone has at home. I followed the same process and tried to reflect the sun coming to me onto Andrea's face. The reflection a CD makes on a person is circular, the same shape as the CD, as if that circle of light were reproduced on the face.

We can change what we want the CD to reflect and what we do not. For example, if I cover half of the CD, the reflection takes the shape of a half-moon. A CD lets us make specific shapes on a person's face with whatever we cover it with, using our hands or, if we get creative, putting a piece of paper over the CD. When the light bouncing off the CD is not very direct or pronounced, the result is almost like water on the face. We see lines and several shapes instead of a defined circle. I think the CD reflecting light is a very interesting tool to play and experiment with.

## Making a camera filter from clear plastic

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L09 · 03:15–04:31

669 · U03 · L09 · 03:15–04:31

At another point I felt that the photographs looked too ordinary, too standard, as if I had seen them before. I looked for something else in the house that could make the images more interesting. I took a bag from breakfast croissants. It had a strip of clear plastic, which I cut out. I also cut a hole in the plastic so that when I put it in front of the camera there would be a defined area. I put it in front of the lens (*objetivo*) and tied it on with a hair tie.

Once the plastic was in front of the lens, with the hole centered over the lens, I put Andrea by the window and began taking close-up portraits. The interesting thing was that the sun came from the side and filtered through the plastic, creating very interesting flares (*destellos*) in the image. In some photographs they looked almost like ghosts; in others they looked like grand lights filling nearly the whole frame. It is also an interesting tool when we know clearly what we want to photograph. In this case I wanted only Andrea's face; the rest did not interest me. The plastic did not only blur that area, it also made very interesting shapes of white light around her face.

## Adding color with small dried flowers

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L09 · 04:31–07:05

669 · U03 · L09 · 04:31–07:05

Another thing in the house that I thought was great to work with was these dried flowers. They are lilac-colored. When you put thin, small plants of a particular color right in front of the lens, the dark tones suddenly take on the color of the plant. The objects have to be small enough that they do not cover the image behind them. I took some of the flowers while Andrea was sitting and put them right in front of the lens, not attached to it, but about three fingers away.

To light this photograph, I closed the window right behind Andrea so there would not be backlight (*contraluz*). But the photograph suddenly looked too flat, so I opened the window again; I realized it had much more life and depth that way.

Throughout the session I showed Andrea all the images we were making together. I praised her whenever I liked how she was posing and liked the photographs. We looked at them together and celebrated the small wins. I think photography is always work done by two people. There is nothing better than helping the model feel safe and confident. When we feel safe and confident, we do everything better because we believe in ourselves. That is the feeling I think should come out of everything we do during the session: a space of safety and trust where we can do incredible work as a team.

To finish, I encourage you to investigate and look for things around your house, things you think of or find in the street or in little shops. Anything can work. If we play and investigate, I am sure there are a thousand things no one has found before that can suddenly give your photograph a style of its own and help you build a more defined identity. I also encourage you to look for spaces that motivate you, and to take spaces you already know and turn them around so you can make a photograph you feel proud of, even if at first that place did not seem to offer much. In the next lesson we will work on editing with Lightroom.
