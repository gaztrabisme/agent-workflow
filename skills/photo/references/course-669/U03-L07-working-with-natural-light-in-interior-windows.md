# Lesson 07 · Working with natural light in Interior: windows

## Finding and shaping sunlight through shutters

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–02:45

669 · U03 · L07 · 00:00–02:45

In this lesson I show how I use the light coming through the windows of an apartment to get the most out of it. I took all the photographs with a model named Andrea. At the beginning of the session, I saw direct sunlight coming through one of the living-room windows onto the balcony. The windows had adjustable wooden shutters, so we decided to play with the sun coming through their slats and draw lines of light across Andrea's skin.

I first had Andrea crouch and face the sun. The stripes from the shutters then fell across her face. The difficulty was that she was in profile to me: the stripes appeared, but she could not look at the camera. When I tried to have her look at me, a shadow fell across her face. That can still be interesting if you can make it work, but I chose to keep Andrea more in profile and ask her to open her eyes in a gap between the stripes. The light reached her eyes and brought out their pale blue color. I realized this made the photograph much more beautiful, so I concentrated on placing her eyes in the gaps of light.

Once we had that, I had Andrea stand up to see how the light fell on her body. I was not convinced by the framing, so I worked more on her performance. Instead of making the usual expression of someone being photographed, I asked her to focus on the sun on her skin, being on a small balcony, being in a square, and the calm feeling of that moment, rather than on posing for a photograph. That was my intention, but I still was not fully convinced by the image, so we went back down to the floor.

I then thought it might be interesting for Andrea to light a cigarette while the sunlight came through the shutters. The sun was very dense, but there was too much wind on the balcony. The smoke moved away quickly instead of staying and forming a cloud, so I gave up on that idea quite early.

## Placing something between the camera and the subject

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L07 · 02:45–03:34

669 · U03 · L07 · 02:45–03:34

There is another thing you will notice in some of the final photographs: I often put a finger a little in front of the lens. I have always done this. Apart from blurring one corner slightly, it sometimes gives me the feeling that there is something between me and the model. It makes the moment feel more real to me, as if I were a voyeur seeing something that is actually happening. At first it was an occasional thing I did almost as a joke, but now I notice I do it more and more, nearly without realizing. I think it is interesting to play with whatever may be between you and the person you are photographing; it can turn the image around completely.

## Photographing Andrea through the glass

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L07 · 03:34–05:05

669 · U03 · L07 · 03:34–05:05

Next I moved Andrea inside the house and stayed outside on the balcony, behind the glass. As in the earlier photographs, I was using the Sigma 35 mm f/1.4. I was not using my usual 50 mm because this was such a small space and I needed a slightly wider-angle lens (*objetivo angular*) to fit more into the frame.

The first problem when I put Andrea behind the glass was that I appeared in it. I could not close the balcony doors; I had to leave them open and adjust one door's angle with an arm. I looked for a position where I disappeared from the glass but the park and the trees still appeared. I kept adjusting the door and guiding Andrea. I also needed manual focus (*enfoque manual*) because my camera's autofocus was not very capable. There were several possible planes to focus on: the whole image reflected in the glass, everything happening inside the house, Andrea just behind the glass, or the balcony around the glass. When Andrea seemed overwhelmed by this, I switched straight to manual and set the focus where I wanted it.

## Using flattering frontal light and choosing balcony light

Anchor: 669 · U3 · L07 · 05:05–07:45

669 · U03 · L07 · 05:05–07:45

After finishing with the glass, I moved Andrea inside and stayed outside on the balcony, leaving the doors open. I think one of the most flattering photographs is made with frontal light (*luz frontal*) coming from slightly above the model's eye line. The shadows then fall where our idea of beauty places them: on the cheekbones and under the chin, while the under-eye circles look very flat. It is a very practical photograph to make during a session because you know the person will look good.

Finally, because there were several balconies next to each other, I thought I might photograph Andrea (captions: *Andrés*) as she came out onto one balcony while I stood on another. I wanted the image to feel like a quiet, intimate moment of someone looking out at the street without thinking about anything in particular. Andrea stood at the railing, watched people pass, and took in some sun.

I do not think this kind of photograph has much of a secret, but I do think two lighting situations work well. One is a time when the sun is low enough to make a backlit photograph (*contraluz*) on a balcony; I think backlight works very well there. The other is when the sun hits the model directly from the front. A middle ground, with patches of sun and shade (captions: *pegajos de sol, pegajos de sombra*), looks so contrasty and forced that it is harder for me to connect with the photograph. In balconies and through windows I therefore look for direct light or backlight, never the middle ground.

When I had to choose between the right balcony, where the sun was direct, and the left one, where it was behind Andrea, I would normally have chosen the backlight because I think it flatters balcony photographs. But it was noon and the sun was too high, so the backlight looked too forced. I decided it was better to put Andrea facing the sun. That is how I work with window light; in the next lesson I look at how to use an interior space.
