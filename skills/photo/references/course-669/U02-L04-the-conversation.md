# Lesson 04 · The conversation

Anchor: 669 · U2 · L04 · 8:07 · https://www.domestika.org/en/courses/669-intimate-photography-portraits/units/3862-get-ready#course_lesson_12194

## Build a safe and trusting space

Anchor: 669 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:36

Before each photo session, I try to have a conversation with the model to build a bond of trust and security. In this lesson, I explain what that preliminary conversation involves. The conversation itself is not the goal; I am trying to create a safe space where the model can develop throughout the session, move and pose in their own way, without feeling judged. I want complete safety and trust.

By talking and showing interest in the other person, I try to become an accompanying hand. I am not trying to become their friend. I listen, respect their space and try to understand them as well as I can. I want them to see me as a companion who will accept what they do. Once that trust is there, the photographs can become more truthful.

I meet the model wherever we agreed. If it is a public place, we might go to a bar; otherwise, we might go for a meal or they might come to my home. The point is to sit and talk as casually as possible, about everyday things: where the person is from, what they study, what they like. It is an ordinary conversation, like one you might have with a friend in a bar.

## Listen and notice what makes the person distinct

Anchor: 669 · U2 · L04 · 01:36–03:24

The main principle is to listen and ask questions genuinely. In an ordinary conversation, I would expect both people to contribute about equally, but here I am trying to understand the other person. I notice how they move, what they are passionate about, the poses they take and how they move their hair. I look for the things they do that I find beautiful.

In a calm, everyday conversation, people show parts of themselves that they may not show during the photo session. In front of a camera, people are generally more tense and move away from what makes them different. I try to remember particular gestures and ways of looking or sitting, so I can encourage those qualities later and help the person return to themselves.

People are not mannequins. They have gestures, looks and attitudes that are often hidden but very interesting. It matters to find what distinguishes this person from everyone else and keep it present in the photographs, so I can portray the most genuine version of them that I am able to capture.

## Let the model choose what to share

Anchor: 669 · U2 · L04 · 03:24–06:21

After I start with ordinary, sometimes banal subjects, there is often a point when I give the model space. They can decide how far they want to explain or open up. In my experience, people have a lot to say, especially to someone they do not know, because that person is outside their usual circle. It can feel freeing to talk about what troubles or moves them.

When you ask and show real interest, you can notice small things people offer and give them room to explain what hurts or what they would not tell everyone. For example, someone might say, “I moved to a new apartment because my partner and I broke up recently.” You can ask how long they were together. As you ask small questions, the person may explain more about their emotional state. Then you are photographing someone vulnerable, not someone presenting only their best self: someone who has bad days and difficult situations. Showing people as human and vulnerable can make us feel much safer, because there is nothing to prove.

People sometimes cry while telling you about something they have kept inside or talking about their insecurities. Women in particular face so much pressure around their appearance that being photographed can put them in an especially vulnerable position, with all the attention directed at them. I think the process can be very therapeutic and interesting.

After talking for a while, wherever the conversation takes us, I ask what music the model likes or wants to hear. We put on music and start taking photographs, without making too much of it. I think the best things emerge when you are playing. Do not feel defeated if the conversation stays superficial. You might take the photos laughing, exchange two kisses and say goodbye; that is fine. Each person decides how much they want to open up and what they want to share. One session is not worse than another because it stayed light rather than becoming emotional. What matters is giving each model the space they need to feel comfortable during the photographs.

## Make the approach your own

Anchor: 669 · U2 · L04 · 06:21–08:07

This approach works for me because of my personality. I have gradually tried different ways of relating to models; some did not work for me, some embarrassed me and others went very well. Explaining how I work and try to connect with people does not mean this is the only way, or the best way. No one knows you better than you know yourself.

If you are more introverted, you might go through the process in a less invasive way, leaving more silences and calm spaces. It is important to examine what feels comfortable to you and what you want to do, then try it and see whether it works. I might discover that taking the photographs straight away, instead of talking first, makes them more intimate. Nothing is right or wrong in itself. Everyone has their own way of reaching the goal I described at the beginning: creating trust, care and calm. I encourage you to question your own approach, think about how you can get there and experiment; that can be fun too.
