## Build a base edit with light and color

Anchor: 669 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–03:49

In this lesson I’ll show you how to edit the photos we just made with Lightroom. We’ll choose a few so you can get an idea of how I would handle the light and color. Let’s begin.

I already have Lightroom open. I imported all the photos we made with Andrea. On the left I generally have the filters I make for myself. We’ll see how to make one from scratch and how to work with the colors and everything else. I start by working on the light. The background here is a little blown out, so I’ll begin by lowering the highlights (*brillos*). From there I’ll work on the blacks (*negros*) a little too.

With the tone curve (*curva de tonos*), I always work with three points. I try to veil the dark tones a little. Sometimes I crush them quite a lot, but then I raise the blacks so a little more shows through. That gives me the texture I’m looking for. I lower the whites (*blancos*) a little too. This way we can see more information in the areas that were very blown out before.

In general, I desaturate the yellows because I don’t really like that color. There’s almost none here, as you can see, but I still prefer to get rid of some of it. If possible, so the color doesn’t look yellow-green, I shift it a little toward magenta. Basically, I try something and see what happens, then decide what to do. I also like to darken the oranges because I think that gives skin a little more texture. Sometimes I desaturate them a little if they look too intense or too red, and keep adjusting from there.

After working on those colors, if one color stood out—for example, if the dress were blue—I’d work on the blue too. I don’t think blue has much presence in this photo. There is some, but changing it doesn’t change the overall look much.

Then I work directly on the highlights and shadows. Here I’d like the highlights to be a little yellow. I’m not sure whether to leave the shadows like this. Now I think I’ll move them a little toward yellow too. From there I keep working on the color until I find something I especially like. I go back to the reds down here; this lower section works well for me when I’m making the final color adjustments and deciding which direction they should take.

This is all very intuitive. I’m showing you that you learn by playing and adjusting a bit of everything. That’s how you find the colors you identify with. This is starting to look more like something I like. Maybe I’ll lower the blacks a little more [captions: *dolaria*]. More like that.

At this point, I think we’d have this photo finished. Before moving to the next photos, I can see there’s quite a lot of green here and down below too. I’m not a big fan of green, so I’ll take some of it out. This is one hundred percent intuitive. You can also see which colors work for you and which do not; there’s no way around that. I’m doing it this way today, but another day I might do it differently.

What matters to me is that, with the first photo in the first space, I define the colors that will run through that session. Those will be the base colors. That doesn’t mean I won’t change them later, but in the other spaces we’ll start from them so the photos have a little more coherence.

## Carry the look through a scene

Anchor: 669 · U4 · L10 · 03:49–05:57

I always like adding a little grain (*grano*). This much would be far too much, but a little works well for adding texture. We’ll start from this version: copy it with Command-C, then paste the edit onto the other photos from the same space and adapt it a little to each one. That saves time; editing each one from scratch would be a lot of work.

At this stage I think it’s important to edit every photo that works for you, even a little. Later, when you select, you can decide which ones really have potential and which do not. Photos change so much when they’re edited that I think it’s worth doing.

I like making photos both very close and very far away within the same scene, to explore all the possibilities it gives me. That way the photos tell me more about that scene, and they’re more interesting to look at when there are several perspectives of the same moment. I’m especially drawn to photos where light comes in. I feel as if it paints the image. When you work on the colors afterward, it gives the picture a more dreamlike, almost fantastical feel. Even though it’s a very real photo, it has something more artistic and special about it.

It’s good to experiment with all the options a moment we like gives us: make all kinds of shots, and try having the model do several things, because you never know what will work or how the light will respond to each pose. I’m raising the light in these photos because the blacks looked very dull. I like that the light falls not just on her face but on her arms too.

## Keep lines straight and retain detail

Anchor: 669 · U4 · L10 · 05:57–08:53

Even when we edit a lot of photos, I think it’s important to keep them as straight as possible. A crooked photo gives me a bit of a jolt, though some people work very well with more tilted photos and give them an artistic touch. Whenever there are lines, I want them as vertical as possible, and horizons as centered as possible; otherwise the whole photo feels as if it’s falling.

We should also watch that, if we lower the whites too much, we create patches on the skin. This area was already a little blown out, so there’s no information underneath. It’s important to find the middle ground between lowering the whites and letting them blow out. That would be around here.

I thought it would be interesting for Andrea to smoke—not because of smoking itself, but to see how the smoke behaved in this space, with the light coming through the blinds. We could have used a smoke machine, a match going out, or anything else; that was simply what we had closest to hand.

You can see that I edit a lot of photos, but the effort of editing one more or less isn’t very great. As I said, I’d rather make more material and choose later than leave a good photo behind because I can’t quite see it yet. I also often edit photos that are practically the same, with only small differences. I think it’s interesting to see three almost identical photos and watch how the gesture develops. It’s a little like telling a story.

Smoke is curious because it’s so volatile. When you take the photos, you can’t control it; you can only decide to have it there. Then, when you edit the photo, there’s something really nice about discovering the photo itself. When I look at a photo, I don’t want to see the camera in it or feel that the model is doing something because there’s a camera in front of her. I want to feel that she’s living a piece of reality and that I’m simply watching it. I love how the smoke and light enter her eye. It works so well.

I really like photos taken through glass because the lines of the person behind it get confused with what’s outside. Anything that adds information to the photograph is very interesting to me. As before, the first photo always takes much longer to edit. But from here we already have the look for the whole series in that space, so it’s much easier.

## Balance focus and color in the chair portraits

Anchor: 669 · U4 · L10 · 12:22–13:56

I can’t even edit many photos because they aren’t in focus; I can see that from a distance. That always makes everything a little more complicated. Sometimes it’s easier to take the photo normally and then use these tools while editing to manage the sharpness (*enfoque*) and softness (*suavidad*), instead of putting a stocking (*media*) over the lens and then not knowing how to repair the photo.

I’ll take this one. I really like how the turquoise of her dress works with the chair’s turquoise. At first they were different blues. By changing them, they’ve come to a similar point. If we look closely, they aren’t the same tone at all.

After that I took off the stocking and started taking the photos normally, because I realized the result could get too uncontrolled. I’ll reduce the sharpness a little more now that we don’t have the stocking and see if we can get the same effect. I like it a lot, although I’m a little worried the blue and orange might be too strong. For now, from this screen, they look fine.

## Work with dominant color and unexpected light

Anchor: 669 · U4 · L10 · 15:06–17:56

Now we’ll look at the penultimate set: Andrea on the sofa in the living room. The sofa has a very, very dominant color, so let’s see how we can manage it. As you can see, just by changing a color a little, the whole image suddenly changes. For me, that’s the most interesting thing about color in general: it ends up deciding what the whole photo will be.

Finally, we’ll start editing the last photos we took, almost by chance. Just before we took everything apart and put the living room back as it was, I noticed a small rainbow that the window was casting. We decided to try to make use of it, because it isn’t easy to create this kind of light. I think an important part of indoor photography is knowing how to look and make use of the elements an interior gives you at the particular moment you’re there.

We’ll try to make the light as visible as possible. That isn’t easy, because when we raise the light, we wash out the colors. I think I’ll choose this and see how we can change those colors.

That’s the editing of the photos we took earlier. Now we export them and decide which ones we keep, which work best, and which are repeated without adding anything. You’ve seen how I edit photos with Lightroom. Although it may seem complicated at first, it’s much simpler and a lot of fun: you play with light and colors and gradually build a language of your own.

### Key points (derived)

- Build a color and light starting point from the first photo in a space, then adapt it to the other images.
- Make enough photographs to compare gestures, distances, and light, and select later.
- Look for unexpected light and color in an interior; small adjustments can change the whole image.
