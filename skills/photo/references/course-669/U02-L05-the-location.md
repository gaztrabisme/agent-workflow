## Make a small space work

Anchor: 669 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:39

In this lesson I show several location examples and how to make the most of the resources available. The first is a space that might not seem to offer much at first glance. People often think that if a space is not remarkable, the photograph cannot turn out well. In fact, sometimes all we need is a neutral background and a source of light.

In my living room, I put the camera on a tripod and placed a small piece of plastic with a hole in the middle in front of it. That made the area around the centre a little out of focus while the centre stayed sharp. I sat on the floor, covered myself with paint I had at home and took some self-portraits. You can see the result here. These photographs do exactly what I wanted them to do. Even in the smallest apartment, in the most inhospitable corner, we can make the photograph we imagined if we use the right tools.

## Find and frame the space you want

Anchor: 669 · U2 · L05 · 01:39–03:59

The second example is for when we want a beautiful space that will play a major part in a much wider shot. Often we do not know where to start or do not know a place like that. I decided I wanted photographs in a house with an aristocratic feel, from the beginning of the twentieth century, so I requested a journalist’s permit for La Pedrera in Barcelona (captions: *la Padrera*). It is a very beautiful monument, with an exhibition house that tourists can visit.

I was able to take my model there. Although tourists were everywhere, we found small areas where they did not appear, by managing the framing (*encuadre*). The result looks as though she is in a nobleman’s house, around mid-century (captions: *medianos de siglo*). This shows that even without a spectacular apartment or house, we can create the space we want with some audacity, imagination and thought. We can ask ourselves how to reach the location we dream of; there is usually a way.

Another useful option is to look at the homes of friends and acquaintances, or at the home of a friend’s partner. Find a space you like and do not be afraid to ask if you can come and take photographs there; you could bring croissants in return. This can give us access to spaces that improve our photos without spending a fortune renting studios.

Sometimes the whole space will not work for us, so we can play with angles and the framing. That La Pedrera room was full of tourists. By framing carefully, they disappear and the place looks empty, as if it were there just for us. If there are people or elements that do not work, frame them out and the space can become what you want it to be.

## Choose the time and light

Anchor: 669 · U2 · L05 · 03:59–05:58

Now let us look at why light matters in outdoor photographs. The location is not the only thing that matters; the time of day matters too. The first image shows a woman in a park, photographed at about three in the afternoon in summer. The sun is very strong then. You may find only small areas of shade, and the photos can become too contrasty, with a big difference between light and shadow. You can make that work, but the result changes a lot if, for example, you go to the beach at eight in the evening in August. The sun is low and setting, giving you light that comes completely from the side (captions: *completamente de helado*), orange, almost magenta. The mood is completely different. Depending on the time you visit a place, the light changes greatly, and the place changes with it.

In one photograph, a woman is in a hotel bathroom with no natural light, lit directly by fluorescent lights. If you know that, you can make a very particular kind of photograph with that atmosphere. Another photograph is also indoors, in a house full of windows, at a time when strong sunlight enters from the side. The two photos have completely different identities and convey very different things. In each case, the photographer has been aware of how the light affects the space.

## Arrange an interior to suit the light

Anchor: 669 · U2 · L05 · 05:58–07:18

Outdoor photographs are shaped by the light and the time, but with interiors, the space itself matters as well as the moment and the kind of windows it has. We often do not control where the light is. The windows may be in an inconvenient position compared with the furniture, for example. Remember that we can move tables and chairs and make a space look different within an interior. What we are not given is not immovable (captions: *donado*); we can arrange things however suits us.

If we need to move a table somewhere it would not normally be in the house, we can do that as long as it looks natural in the photograph. A natural-looking frame matters. We can move things so that the light, furniture and whole space work together. From these examples, we can see that we do not need large spaces or a lot of equipment; we need to make the most of each thing and each situation.
