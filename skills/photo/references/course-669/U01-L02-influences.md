## Poetry and Mario Benedetti

Anchor: 669 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–02:10

In this lesson I want to explain some of my influences, my great teachers, and how they led me to do what I do and to do it the way I do. I think poetry has an almost leading role. Poetic language gave me the sensitivity to connect with many other kinds of art that I might not have connected with before.

I began with poetic language through Mario Benedetti’s poetry anthology, when I was fifteen. My first boyfriend had just left me—a typically very painful teenage breakup—and I bought this book. I feel enormous affection for it because I, someone who barely read and had never been interested in poetry, suddenly realized that a person who had lived across the ocean and had nothing to do with my reality could have felt something like I did. Above all, that person could put words to an emotion I had but could not explain.

Having someone say what you feel before you are even aware you feel it is such a beautiful experience. That happened to me with Benedetti. I fell in love with poetry, so it is hard for me to be objective about him or his work: I connect with the teenage Marta. I have had this anthology all my life, and it is covered in marks. I love writing in books; I love leaving the trace of the person who has read them. I take something from a book, and I want the book to have something of me too. My favorite poem in it is *Ausencia de Dios* [Absence of God], which is why I opened the book to that page. Benedetti was my first poetic reference, so he had to be the first one I showed. I am not saying he is the best; for me, he was an awakening, like coming out of Plato’s cave.

## Pedro Salinas and abstraction

Anchor: 669 · U1 · L02 · 02:10–04:02

The next poet, who came just after Benedetti, was Pedro Salinas with *La voz a ti debida* [The voice owed to you] (the captions transcribe the title as *La voz a ti de vida* and later *La voz de arte de vida*). For me, Salinas was a step forward in poetic complexity. His metaphors (*metáforas*) seemed more carefully worked, as did his way of writing and explaining what he wanted to explain, but he connected with me in a way very similar to Mario Benedetti. I felt that he was saying something I could be saying from somewhere inside me, while also feeling that a friend, or someone who knew me, was explaining it to me.

I have only felt this kind of closeness to a writer with these two, Benedetti and Salinas—as if they somehow knew me. Feeling that with any work of art is wonderful. *La voz a ti debida* was my second step into poetry. From then on, I never stopped exploring it, loving it, and devouring poetry books, but they were the first.

Unlike Benedetti, Salinas gives me a much more abstract world. Benedetti has always been a poet of the everyday for me. Even though he speaks about emotions and very profound things, underneath it all he feels much more grounded. With Salinas I discovered a way of speaking about love, pain, and the fears that accompany us from birth in a far more abstract way. He does not specify gender, where something is happening, what it relates to, or the context. He describes an emotion in so many ways that it is easy to connect with and apply it to any reality. In this sense Salinas moved me forward; he lifted my feet a little off the ground. After those two came Chantal Maillard.

## Chantal Maillard and poetry’s connection to photography

Anchor: 669 · U1 · L02 · 04:02–05:34

Chantal Maillard taught me a new way to express how we feel. She is a very sad woman, or at least she seems to be, and her poetry is very sad too. In painful moments when I did not need someone to explain why I was sad or talk to me about sadness, I needed to survive somehow. Chantal taught me that you can write exactly as thoughts come into your head, and you can write from pain. Sometimes you do not even need to look for coherence, because pain is often incoherent. Her impulsiveness, that incoherent pain and that wound reached deep inside me. She is an immense influence for me.

Once again, I return to anthologies. This was a very interesting poetry anthology with a bit of everything she has done, as well as some really good texts. I recommend it because it lets you see her imagination from a distance, and then you can choose where you want to enter. Those are my poetic references.

Poetry and photography are completely linked for me. The sensitivity I bring to taking photographs is the same sensitivity with which I read and write poetry. I think poetic language goes far beyond letters and lines of verse. I think exposure (*exposición*) has given me a lot of emotional intelligence. Thanks to it, I have connected with many ways of seeing and with many photographs, and I have handled things in my life much better than I would have without it.

## Peter Lindbergh and intimate portraiture

Anchor: 669 · U1 · L02 · 05:34–07:25

As well as poetry, many painters and illustrators, and other people in the arts, stimulate me. But now I am going to focus on two photographers with whom I have connected in a special way, just as I have with poetry, in a way that is incomparable with the rest.

The first is Peter Lindbergh, who died very recently. He fascinates me. He managed to photograph some of the most famous people in each decade: top actors and major public figures. Even so, he did it with intimacy, closeness, and a sense of truth. You do not just see the actress who is currently making waves as a great diva; you see her as a human being, with shortcomings, vulnerable and fragile. I think public figures are among the most solid and impenetrable figures in our society, so being able to show them as fragile and vulnerable, with signs of age, here and now, in front of you, and in a horizontal frame is incredible. Peter Lindbergh is an amazing influence for me.

It is fascinating to watch how he works and how close, truly close, and gentle he is with the people he photographs. He hardly speaks during sessions. All of this moved me. I was moved watching Peter Lindbergh take photographs because I felt he took them with his heart. That is exceptional. It seems impossible to look at one of his photographs without connecting with all your senses and feeling something vibrate in your chest.

## Silvia Graf and emotion in photographs

Anchor: 669 · U1 · L02 · 07:25–08:19

Another photographer I love, and connected with from the first moment I saw her work, is Silvia Graf (the captions later spell her surname *Graff* and *Grapp*). I like her photographs because they remind me a lot of painting. A photograph often reproduces reality too exactly, and I think a work of art should tell me more about its maker than about what appears in it.

I think painting has often allowed people to paint how they feel, or to put an emotion on top of the image being depicted. I get that same feeling from Silvia Graf’s photographs. I see emotions right on the surface; in the end, the image becomes a symbol of the emotion that covers it completely. Silvia Graf makes me vibrate inside. Her work moves and touches me. I could cry at her photographs.

## Alessio Albi, color, and light

Anchor: 669 · U1 · L02 · 08:19–09:23

The third photographer I want to talk about is the well-known Alessio Albi (the captions also give his name as *Aleixio Albi* and *Alicia o Albi*). I want to talk about his treatment of color and light. Aesthetically, I think he is my greatest inspiration. Peter Lindbergh and Silvia Graf inspire me much more emotionally; Alessio Albi creates scenes and environments that are very connected with his emotions, yet beautiful and not overly prepared.

I find it difficult to connect with photography that feels too staged, because I do not believe it. With Albi, I see real situations, but with impeccable colors and light. For me, that is a fantastic combination.

These are some of my favorite poets and photographers, or at least those who have marked me most in certain periods of my life. Of course, I have many other influences in other areas, and I will leave them in the additional resources. In the next lesson, we will work on what we are going to do in this course.
