## Why manual HDR can look more natural

Anchor: 1343 · U33 · L51 · 00:00–02:18

High dynamic range means combining images to preserve more detail, especially in the highlights and shadows. Bridge provides an automated route through Tools, Photoshop, and Merge to HDR Pro. The teacher finds that this can look hyper-realistic or slightly fake, so he prefers to show a manual approach.

For a house interior he photographed, he opens two exposures in the raw converter. In the first, the kitchen is correctly exposed, but the living room and outside are very bright. In the next, the kitchen is underexposed while the living room is better exposed; the outside is still bright. He prefers this balance. If the kitchen, living room, and outside were all perfectly exposed and then joined perfectly, the result could look fake. His manual method looks more natural and is quick and easy.

## Prepare both raw exposures

Anchor: 1343 · U33 · L51 · 02:18–04:56

He treats the two images individually in the raw converter and makes any changes he wants, but keeps distortion corrections consistent. The images do not need every setting synchronized, but chromatic aberration and distortion should be removed equally. He briefly turns Profile Corrections off to show the effect, notes that both exposures need the same treatment, then turns it back on and returns to the Basic panel.

![1343 · U33 · L51 · 02:57.30](frames/U33L51-F0257.30.jpg)

Usually, he would use the same white balance for images being merged. Here there are two light sources: tungsten light (captions: *tungsten lye*) in the kitchen and daylight in the living room, where the lights are off. The kitchen is warmer. He raises the temperature slightly to compensate, without pushing the exposures too far apart; he can make further adjustments in Photoshop. The blacks look washed out, so he darkens them. While adjusting this, he says he is ignoring what happens in another part of the image.

Before opening the pair, he holds Shift to open them as objects, so he can return to the raw converter for further changes. Workflow Options also lets him make “Open in Photoshop as Smart Objects” the default.

## Create a black mask and set up the brush

Anchor: 1343 · U33 · L51 · 06:17–08:34

He adds a black layer mask to the Highlights layer. A black mask hides the layer’s contents, so the highlight exposure starts out invisible. He shows the Layer > Layer Mask menu, but prefers the mask symbol at the bottom of the Layers panel. Clicking it normally adds a white mask; holding Alt or Option while clicking creates a black one. If the mask is white by mistake, he can choose Invert in Properties or press Command-I on Mac or Control-I on Windows.

He selects the Brush tool with B and chooses a smaller brush. He starts with a soft brush at 0% hardness and may change the setting as he works. A larger brush has a softer edge; with a very large brush, he may raise the hardness slightly.

## Set opacity and flow, and understand airbrush

Anchor: 1343 · U33 · L51 · 08:34–10:35

Opacity and flow take practice to set well, and even experienced users sometimes get them wrong. Because he is painting on a layer mask, the work is non-destructive and easy to correct. He wants 100% opacity so he can paint up to the full effect, and he usually uses keyboard shortcuts rather than the Options bar. Pressing 0 sets opacity to 100%; 1 sets it to 10%, 2 to 20%, 3 to 30%, and so on. Typing digits quickly combines them, so 2 then 3 gives 23%; to set 5%, type 05. Zero is a useful shortcut for full opacity.

Flow works the same way with Shift held: Shift-1 sets 10%, Shift-2 sets 20%, and Shift-0-5 sets 5%. He says 5% flow will work well here. When Airbrush is enabled, holding the mouse button or tablet pen down keeps laying down paint. He often switches Airbrush off so the brush stops painting when he stops moving, but enables it to demonstrate the behavior. With Airbrush enabled, the normal number shortcut controls flow rather than opacity.
