## Where dodging and burning come from

Anchor: 1343 · U39 · L60 · 00:00–01:57

Usually the eyes are the most important part of a portrait. In this lesson I’ll show a simple, quick, natural way to brighten them. Photoshop has several ways to brighten or darken areas of an image; this is often called dodging and burning. Photoshop has Dodge and Burn tools, but their symbols may not make sense if you have never worked in a darkroom. The lollipop or tennis-racket symbol represents a small stick with something on its end. Under an enlarger, you would move it over the light to stop light reaching the paper, so that area would come out lighter in the print. The fist represents a shape made with your hand to let more light reach part of the paper. That is burning, and the area comes out darker in the print.

That is where the terms come from. Some people still say “dodging and burning”; others call it “light and dark.” They mean the same thing. The Photoshop tools are probably the easiest to understand quickly, but they are destructive and do not give as good a result as the techniques I’ll show you.

## Why a loose selection can look more natural

Anchor: 1343 · U39 · L60 · 01:57–03:17

I’ll show a very quick and very effective way to brighten the eyes. One common approach is to make a very careful selection around the eye and brighten it. That can make it look as if a light bulb has been switched on inside the person’s head and the eyes are glowing. It does not look natural or appealing, and making accurate selections can take time.

Sometimes a loose selection works well. Think back to the darkroom: whether you use your hand to let extra light into an area or a stick to keep light away, the masking is never very precise. It is a loose way to control the area, yet it can produce a good result. We can bring some of those darkroom ideas into Photoshop.

## Build a gentle curve and brush it into both eyes

Anchor: 1343 · U39 · L60 · 03:17–07:05

I’ll add a Curves adjustment layer and call it “Eye Brighten.” I lift the curve a little more than I want the final result to be. I also pull up the very darkest black point slightly. This keeps the blacks in the eyelashes and the eye dark while the rest of the tonal range becomes brighter.

Next, I invert the layer mask. Select the mask and click Invert in the Properties panel, or press Command-I on a Mac or Control-I on Windows. The mask turns black and hides the brightening effect. Do not confuse black with darkening or white with brightening: here, black hides the adjustment and white reveals it.

I select the regular Brush tool with B, choose a very large brush, set opacity low at 10% and leave flow at 100%. A brush around 300 pixels is much larger than the eye. Think of a reflector used while taking the photograph: it would not put a pinpoint of light into the eye; light would spill onto the face too. I make a quick stroke. If I can see the stroke, someone else probably will too, so I undo it with Command-Z or Control-Z and lower opacity further, perhaps to 5%.

Use white as the foreground color. Press D to reset the colors to their defaults, and X to swap foreground and background colors. Make one stroke over each eye, reduce the brush with the left square bracket key, then make another stroke over each eye. Keep reducing the brush and gradually focus on the eye. Work quickly without thinking too much about each stroke. Compare before and after: the result should look fairly natural. If it is too strong, lower the layer opacity. If the color shifts, try the Luminosity blend mode instead of Normal. I can compare the two in the History panel as well, which may make the difference easier to see. It makes little difference in this image, but it can make a big difference in other images, so it is worth trying.

## Lighten darker parts of the eye subtly

Anchor: 1343 · U39 · L60 · 07:05–09:07

In the next example I’ll work on a darker part of the eye and a couple of red areas. I add another Curves adjustment layer called “Eye brighten” and lift the curve to brighten a slightly dark line. I invert the mask again with Command-I or Control-I.

This time I use a low-opacity brush and lower the flow to about 15%. Turn off smoothing so Photoshop does not try to steady your hand by changing the stroke. Smoothing has nothing to do with brush softness. With a small brush, paint inside the darker areas. They should gradually get lighter, almost as if nothing is changing. If the changes are easy to see, the final result is likely to be poor. Resist the temptation to raise your opacity too high. Compare before and after: keep it subtle and natural. This portrait is meant to look natural; a fashion image would usually call for a somewhat different approach. I could take the adjustment slightly further, but that is the idea.

## Clone visible vessels while preserving texture

Anchor: 1343 · U39 · L60 · 09:07–10:42

For the little bloodshot vessels inside the eye, cloning is the best approach. I add a blank layer above the background and call it “Eye,” then select the Clone Stamp tool with S and zoom in. Clone Stamp opacity is generally kept quite high so you do not lose texture. Here I only want to hide the vessels, which are natural and should not disappear completely, so 30% is a good place to start. Press 3 to set that opacity. Use a soft brush by lowering hardness all the way, and make it quite small.

Sample nearby and paint over a vessel. Keep sampling from slightly different places so the same pattern is not repeated; I keep coming back to sample again. That is generally the best approach for this kind of work.
