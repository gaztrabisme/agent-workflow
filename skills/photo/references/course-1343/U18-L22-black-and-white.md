## Why convert from color

Anchor: 1343 · U18 · L22 · 00:00–01:08

In this lesson I’ll show you how to make proper black and white images. We often see gray images rather than true black and white, so I’ll show you how to create real blacks and true contrast. For the best results, convert from color instead of simply desaturating. There are several ways to work in black and white; I’ll walk you through the methods I prefer. First, it helps to understand how colors are converted. One straightforward way to make an image grayscale is to add a Hue/Saturation adjustment layer.

## What desaturation does

Anchor: 1343 · U18 · L22 · 01:08–03:11

When I remove the saturation, the colored blocks become almost identical and their text disappears. To see why, I turn the adjustment off and inspect the color values in the blocks. The brightness value for red is 100%; green, blue, cyan, magenta and yellow are also each 100%. Desaturating an image removes its color information, but the brightness value from each original color remains, and that brightness determines the resulting gray. I call this gray rather than black and white, because these colors have not produced real black or white; they have simply turned gray.

For many years, older versions of Photoshop required fairly complex color conversions to produce good black and white. My preferred older method used two Hue/Saturation adjustment layers. One changed the hues and saturation of the existing colors, and the next removed the colors. That process gave a good black and white conversion.

## Map colors to separate tones

Anchor: 1343 · U18 · L22 · 03:11–05:40

Instead of Hue/Saturation, we now have a better tool called Black & White. I close the Colors group so the new adjustment layer sits above the group rather than inside it. The result is immediately different: the colors separate from one another. The Black & White tool maps each color to a tone. Its sliders run from black at the left to white at the right. The first square is red; moving the red slider down makes that red darker. Cyan (captions: *science*) is the complementary color to red, so brightening cyan makes the red text stand out by separating the two tones.

This is how I start a black and white conversion: I look at the existing colors and separate their tones as much as possible. The Black & White adjustment is not where I’m trying to create overall contrast. I could pull red far down and cyan far up to make a very high contrast, but I’m better off using other adjustments with it, such as a Curves adjustment. The main purpose of the Black & White layer is to separate the tones from each other.

## Compare conversion methods on a photograph

Anchor: 1343 · U18 · L22 · 05:40–08:27

Now let’s look at a photograph with clearly different colors. Once it is converted to black and white—or gray, as I call it—the colors become almost identical, with very little difference between them. I switch the layer off, then add a Black & White adjustment. At first, the tones still do not differ very much.

I can turn the adjustment off to identify the colors, then turn it back on and adjust their sliders: the first area is green, and another is red. The tool also has a targeted adjustment, similar to the one in Curves. After I select it, I can click and drag on the image. The Properties panel highlights the color being adjusted; arrows show that dragging left moves its slider left and makes it darker, while dragging right makes it brighter. Moving over the next roller selects yellow, and the next selects cyan. Sometimes a point contains a combination of colors.

Keep in mind that what we perceive as green is often treated as yellow by Photoshop. Foliage in nature is often more yellow than green, so something that looks green to us may be picked up as yellow when we convert it. This is useful to remember when adjusting a black and white image.

## Simplify skin tones and build contrast

Anchor: 1343 · U18 · L22 · 09:16–11:09

Black and white is often useful for simplifying an image. This photograph is of my daughter when she was one day old. Parents tend to look at pictures of their own babies and think they are beautiful, while other people may first notice blotchy colors. Black and white can simplify a picture like this. Adding a Black & White adjustment immediately reduces some of the blotchiness, though it is still visible. A simple change is to brighten the reds; the blotchiness then starts to disappear. I can continue to work with the other sliders as well.

The first result uses the default settings. These are my adjusted settings, and I would keep working on them. For example, I can add a Curves adjustment and darken the image slightly, then add another curve. Black and white works very well with strong contrast, whereas strong contrast is not always the best choice in color.

## Blend black and white into a color look

Anchor: 1343 · U18 · L22 · 11:09–13:17

The last example shows how black and white can create a different effect. Commercial photographs often use a slightly different color palette from what comes straight out of the camera. One way to achieve a different palette is to work with a Black & White adjustment, but keep some color by reducing the layer’s opacity or changing how it interacts with the image.

First, I add a Black & White adjustment layer and reduce its opacity to 35%. The image still has color, and I can adjust the reds, the blues, and the cyans—for example, the sky and water. Here is the original, and here it is with the adjustment. I can control the effect further with opacity, and combine it with a Curves adjustment to increase contrast. The before and after show how this can give the image a different look. Photographers often try to make their images stand out; giving an image a look that is a little different can help it stand out.

## Change the layer blend mode

Anchor: 1343 · U18 · L22 · 13:17–14:57

There is another similar but slightly different way to blend black and white with color. I remove the Curves layer, reset the Black & White adjustment, and return its opacity to 100%. Then I change the blend mode. The default blend mode for any layer is Normal, which makes the tool behave as designed: a Black & White adjustment layer turns the image black and white. Changing the blend mode changes how that layer blends with the layer underneath. The menu has a number of options, and each gives a slightly different effect.

The one I think works well is Soft Light. It adds some contrast and makes the colors slightly more saturated. Here is the before and after. With just a few clicks, you can get a fairly strong look in the image. If it is too strong for your taste, simply reduce the opacity.
