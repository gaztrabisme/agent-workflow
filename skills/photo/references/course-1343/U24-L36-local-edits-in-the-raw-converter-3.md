## Use graduated and radial filters to shape local light

I reset the settings because I want to darken this part of the sand slightly (captions: *sound*). I use negative Exposure and negative Highlights, then place the adjustment there. Since that darkens some of the shadows more than I want, I compensate by raising Shadows.

The sign is a vital part of this image, so I highlight it a little with a radial filter.

![1343 · U24 · L36 · 01:56](frames/U24L36-F0156.50.jpg)

On screen: "Radial Filter" [rest omitted]  
Shows: The vineyard photograph is open with the Radial Filter selected and the sign near the center.

You do not need perfect brushwork for something like this. If you used a brush, you would have to be very careful: the slightest stroke over the edge would leave a mark. When brushwork is too perfect, the subject can look cut out, and we want to avoid that.

I will use a fairly strong setting to show the effect, although these adjustments are generally quite subtle. If you know photography, one stop means twice as bright, so +0.5 is a strong increment. I combine that with some Shadows and place it over the sign. It is much stronger than I want, so I pull it down. When we brighten something, we often lose contrast. Clarity is a good way to compensate, and I also darken the Blacks because I do not want to lose them, especially when I have brightened the shadows.

They are never a perfect shape of the subject; they are a generic way to brighten or darken something in a picture. That experience also helped my understanding of retouching in Photoshop. ## Even out the image with opposing gradients

Anchor: 1343 · U24 · L36 · 05:48–08:47

Another thing I always look at is the image's sections. Here there is the sky, this area, and the little road, but I see another division too: left and right. The right side is much darker than the left. As I said at the beginning, one thing I very often do in my retouching is even things out, so I may use a gradient across the image.

![1343 · U24 · L36 · 06:39](frames/U24L36-F0639.20.jpg)

I raise the Exposure, but not much. I still want the right-hand side darker than the left; I am just bringing the two sides closer together. I raise Exposure and Shadows, and use a negative value so the Highlights do not brighten too much. I place the gradient and hold Shift to keep it straight, then press Enter to apply it.

Next I make another gradient from the other side of the image. I reset the settings, then darken Exposure a little and darken the Highlights. I do not necessarily want to darken the Shadows, so I compensate with a slightly positive value (captions: *Vale*).

Generally speaking, a more even distribution of tones is easier to look at and is often considered a better image. ### Key points (derived)

- Graduated and radial filters can adjust broad areas without requiring precise brush edges.
- When local brightening reduces contrast, use Clarity and darker Blacks to retain definition.
- Compare image sections and use restrained, opposing gradients to even out tonal differences before continuing in Photoshop.
