## Place and control a graduated filter

Anchor: 1343 · U24 · L35 · 00:11–02:35

The graduated filter uses the same settings as the other local adjustment tools. I’ll pull one down to darken the sky, using negative Exposure and negative Highlights. I position the cursor in the middle of the sky and click and drag down.

The first time you do this, it can feel a little like a flight simulator because everything moves back and forth very quickly. The longer you drag, the more controlled your movement becomes. A very short drag is difficult to control, and a longer drag also gives you a smoother transition. You do not need to drag as far as possible, but a longer span blends better. Hold Shift to keep the gradient straight. After applying it, you can make further adjustments, such as adding saturation to the sky.

There are two lines: green and red. The area before the green line receives 100 percent of the settings. You can also start at the bottom of the image and drag up, so the affected area is not always above the line. The area beyond the red line has no adjustment, and the transition is between the two lines.

The preview is another part that bothers me. When you preview, the gradient becomes deselected: its nodes turn white. If you decide it needs to be darker, changing the settings does nothing until you select it again.

## Add another gradient and refine its mask

Anchor: 1343 · U24 · L35 · 02:35–05:45

Suppose I want another adjustment here. In the previous Camera Raw, when I showed the Adjustment Brush, I pointed out the plus icon for creating a new adjustment. Unfortunately, Adobe removed it from the graduated filter, and I do not know why.

To create another adjustment, I can either drag out another gradient with the same settings or press Enter or Return to deselect the current one. Then I can reset the settings and create a new adjustment. For example, I can darken the foreground highlights: set the values, drag upward, and hold Shift to keep it straight. I can compare before and after. The difference is subtle, so I can strengthen it with a little more negative Exposure and darker Blacks. Now there is one gradient on the sky and another on the foreground.

I can also drag a gradient from the side or on a diagonal. The graduated filter has a brush attached to it, so I can continue adding the effect where I want it, or use the Eraser to remove part of the adjustment. The red overlay shows where the filter is applied, and the brush lets me extend or erase that area. This mask can be useful.

## Shape a radial filter and shift its colors

Anchor: 1343 · U24 · L35 · 05:45–09:05

Next is the radial filter (captions: radio filter), beneath the graduated filter. It works much like the other two tools and uses the same kinds of options, but the adjustment is circular or oval. It works well for darkening a small bright spot. I’ll use negative Exposure and darken Highlights. I prefer to decide what I want the filter to do before applying it, though I could draw it first and then adjust it.

I draw the circle and position it over the bright area. To rotate or angle it, move just outside the circle until the corner arrows appear. With the mask on, the main part of the filter is inside, like a soft brush. Lowering Feather takes the effect to the edge; increasing Feather moves the transition gradually toward the center. Keep in mind that the line indicates the main area being edited. As with the other filters, I can continue adjusting it after placing it, including changing Exposure or the color temperature.

Camera Raw 12.3 also introduced a Hue adjustment. It does not let us choose one color to shift; it shifts all the colors at once. In this example, changing Hue affects the sand, but it also changes the person’s jacket. You need to be selective when using it. I welcome the addition, although I think there is room to improve the tool.

As with the gradient, there is no plus icon for a new radial filter. The workaround is to press Enter to deselect the current one. Then I can add another and change or reset its settings without affecting the first.

## Use zoom to fit the next oval, then correct the selection

Anchor: 1343 · U24 · L35 · 09:05–12:08

I’ll make another radial adjustment where I wanted to darken a different part of the image. First I zoom out. There are zoom ratios for 100 percent and Fit in View. Command or Control plus 0 still fits the image to the screen; Command or Control plus 1 is a different shortcut. For 100 percent, use Alt-Control-0 on Windows or Option-Command-0 on Mac. Command or Control plus and minus also zoom in and out.

I am zooming out because this area feels like an oval. That lets me start the shape outside the image instead of trying to fit it inside. The outline is not necessarily the main edited area because of the feathering, so I pull it slightly farther out. If Feather is high, the edge is not very defined; I reduce it to make the edge slightly sharper. Then I turn off the mask and preview before and after.

The sand is turning gray as I darken it, so I’ll add a tiny bit of yellow and a hint of magenta (captions: my gents), and increase saturation. I made a common mistake: the node was not selected, so those color changes were not added to this adjustment. I select the node, add a little yellow and magenta, raise saturation, and the sand color looks better. I reduce Highlights a little further and add a little Clarity for texture. I press Enter to apply that adjustment.

Now I’ll make another radial adjustment over here. I reset the settings because I want a different effect. This time I want to increase Exposure and brighten the shadows. The area is oval-shaped, so I start outside it again and make the filter a little softer.

## Even the beach and waves with several local adjustments

Anchor: 1343 · U24 · L35 · 12:08–17:54

I press Enter, reset the settings, and add another adjustment. This time I want to darken Exposure, mostly in the Highlights, to even out that area. I reset again and make a further filter to brighten the center, raising the overall Exposure and especially the Shadows. Most of the values I use are very small and subtle, but together they make a big difference. Before and after, the tones are much more even.

Instead of brushing the boats one by one, I can often make one global adjustment. I reset the filter and try Clarity. For the preview, Adobe should make this easier: I should not have to invent workarounds for something fundamental to editing. My workaround is to select and delete the adjustment, then press Command or Control plus Z to undo and see the before and after. The change is subtle, as I want it to be. I raise Clarity a little, darken Blacks slightly, and add a hint of Dehaze.

I think the waves in the foreground could benefit from the same adjustment. I press Enter and use the same settings, adding some brightening in the Shadows and a little Exposure. This gives that part of the image more life.

I return to the graduated filter to darken the sky, adding some Clarity and probably Dehaze. I drag down, holding Shift to keep it straight. The change is subtle, as I prefer it. I press Enter to apply it and reset the settings for a new adjustment. I want to brighten this section a little to draw attention to it. Looking again, I decide to darken it slightly more and reduce the Highlights. Moving a slider back and forth often helps me decide where to leave it. I add a hint of blue and some saturation. The color becomes a little strong at the side, but I could correct that easily when working further in Photoshop.

I press Enter and create a new filter. Here I want to raise Exposure a little and add Clarity to define this area. I hold Shift to keep the gradient straight. The before-and-after view includes all the adjustments, both the graduated and radial filters.
