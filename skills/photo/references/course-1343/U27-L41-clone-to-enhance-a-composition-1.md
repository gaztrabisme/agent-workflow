## Prepare and compare the two photographs

Anchor: 1343 · U27 · L41 · 00:11–07:12

*Adobe Photoshop: Beginner's Guide for Photographers · U27 · L41 · 00:11–07:12*

In this lesson, I’ll show how to add or remove an object to improve a composition, using the different cloning tools. Before opening the photographs in Photoshop, I first convert the raw files. As I work, I look for lens flaws. The first photograph has a blue outline, which is chromatic aberration, and its edges are darker than the center, which is a vignette. In Lens Corrections, I select Remove Chromatic Aberration and enable the profile corrections. This removes the colour fringe and corrects some distortion and vignetting.

Back in Basic, the overall exposure is good, but the highlights are bright, so I lower the exposure a little and darken the highlights. I can already see a hint of blue sky. I brighten the shadows, darken the blacks slightly, add clarity for mid-tone contrast, and add a little dehaze. I then darken the sky further with a graduated filter, holding Shift to keep the adjustment straight. I add a little dehaze and clarity, and darken the blacks a little more to keep the result realistic. The background still needs to look slightly faded, so I push the adjustment too far on purpose and compare the before and after. To leave the graduated filter and return to Basic, I click the zoom or hand tool.

The second photograph is overexposed. I exaggerate the adjustment so I can see how much detail is recoverable. The highlights hold up well, but there is little detail to retrieve from the sky; I keep that in mind for later selective adjustments. I darken the highlights, brighten the shadows, leave the blacks as they are, and add clarity, mid-tone contrast, and a little dehaze. The colours are too saturated, so I reduce them slightly. I’ll fine-tune the colour later in Photoshop, where I can work with a mask. To bring this photograph closer to the first one, I reuse the graduated-filter settings rather than resetting them with the minus button. I drag the filter down, then pull it back where the transition looks too sharp. I press H for the hand tool, remove chromatic aberration, and enable profile corrections.

Before opening the files, I make one final adjustment in HSL. I exaggerate the green-saturation adjustment first to see which colours it affects. Greenery often contains more yellow than green, so this check helps me confirm that I’m changing the intended colours. I then reduce the greens’ saturation. I may do more in Photoshop, where I can be more selective. Finally, I compare the two photographs so the same mountain in the background matches well enough for the images to work together. I can choose Select All from the small menu, or, with only two images, hold Command or Control and click each one. Then I open both in Photoshop.

## Plan the edit and set up the Clone Stamp

Anchor: 1343 · U27 · L41 · 07:12–10:02

*Adobe Photoshop: Beginner's Guide for Photographers · U27 · L41 · 07:12–10:02*

I’ll also show a few new features for customizing and fine-tuning the clone tools in Photoshop.

I like to begin an edit by making a mental roadmap. In this photograph, the large puddle and mud in the foreground distract me. The bright rock also attracts attention, as do the small plants. I want the viewer’s eye to move toward the bridge and into the landscape. There is also a car in the background that I would prefer to remove.

I duplicate the background with Command or Control+J and rename the new layer `Clone`. I select the Clone Stamp with S and make sure its blend mode is Normal. I begin with full opacity and full flow. A soft brush will probably work best, although it can reduce some image texture, so I watch the edges. For cloning, I zoom to at least 100%. At 100%, or actual pixels, one screen pixel represents one image pixel. This matters because details that look slightly misaligned at another zoom can lead me to do more cloning than needed. If it looks right at 100%, it will also look right when printed or reduced for a screen. With a very high-resolution image or a close-up, 100% can be uncomfortably close, so I would work at 50% instead.

## Remove the car and begin rebuilding the path

Anchor: 1343 · U27 · L41 · 10:02–13:33

*Adobe Photoshop: Beginner's Guide for Photographers · U27 · L41 · 10:02–13:33*

I zoom to 100% with Command or Control+1 and hold the spacebar to use the Hand tool temporarily. To reduce the brush, I use the square-bracket keys beside P. I hold Alt or Option to sample. Before cloning over the car, I consider the nearby bush, the shadows, and the line of the road. I begin by copying part of the bush. That works fairly well, but a trace of the car remains, so I use a smaller brush and zoom in farther with Command or Control+plus. I watch the preview and move the sample up or down until the road line lines up, then click and drag. I finish by copying a little more of the shading. I return to Fit on Screen with Command or Control+0.

Next, I work on the foreground puddle and mud, which I find unattractive, and continue the path upward. I choose a zoom that lets me see the full path. If 100% is too close, I would use 50% to get a useful preview. Although there is a straight area I could copy, the rest of the road edge is wobbly, so I sample from a more suitable area. I hold Alt or Option and place the sample. The angle does not quite match, so I undo and move the sample farther out. If the path’s angle still does not match, I can change the clone source.

## Rotate the source and manage sampling

Anchor: 1343 · U27 · L41 · 13:33–17:18

*Adobe Photoshop: Beginner's Guide for Photographers · U27 · L41 · 13:33–17:18*

I open Window > Clone Source. The angle control can seem unusual at first. When I click and drag its small rotation symbol, the image preview rotates; that preview shows how the source is being rotated. Releasing the mouse returns the preview. I move the angle to around 30 degrees to demonstrate, then bring it back. About 4 degrees looks useful for changing the sample angle slightly. The brush preview also shows the original sample point.

I generally keep Aligned checked. With Aligned on, the source point follows the brush in relation to its movement instead of returning to the same point each time. I recommend leaving it checked. The main reason to turn it off is when there is only a very small area available to clone from.

I sample again and continue along the path. At first I’m not worried about repeated patterns; I quickly copy over the areas I want to remove, then inspect the result and fix anything that looks copied or edited. I can already see a repeated pattern, which I’ll address afterward. To show why source rotation helps, I draw a line on a new layer. The original road edge and the area I’m filling have different angles, so cloning without rotation would continue the source’s original angle. I hide that layer and return to `Clone`.

## Match the path angle and inspect the result

Anchor: 1343 · U27 · L41 · 17:18–20:46

*Adobe Photoshop: Beginner's Guide for Photographers · U27 · L41 · 17:18–20:46*

I reopen Clone Source, but the preview is missing because the Brush is selected. I need to select the Clone Stamp to see it. I set the source angle to match the path and sample. The first attempt is not quite right, so I undo and rotate the source farther; the second attempt works much better. It looks over-rotated, so I undo and use History to return to an intermediate setting, around 10 degrees. The earlier appearance was also affected by the lighting. I then clone along the road edge, bringing it down properly.

The work is not perfect; the surface still needs attention, but this is enough to demonstrate the technique and provide a starting point.
