## Color casts and tonal range

Anchor: 1343 · U14 · L17 · 00:00–01:05

In this lesson, I’ll show how to balance the colors in an image, remove an unwanted color cast, and creatively color an image to give it a look of your own.

Now that we have improved the tonal range, the colors are more visible. The colors were not completely correct before the earlier adjustments, but the green cast is now much easier to see. An unwanted color in an image is what we generally call a color cast. This is why you should always correct the tonal range before doing anything else: once the tonal range is correct (captions: “tunnel range”), it is easier to see the colors in the image.

## Complementary colors and a notes layer

Anchor: 1343 · U14 · L17 · 01:05–02:33

The primary colors have opposites, or complementary colors, in the spectrum. To mark up the image without losing its original details, I add a layer by going to the Layers panel and clicking the plus icon. I rename the layer “notes,” select the Brush tool by pressing B on the keyboard, and make the brush a little harder and smaller.

If an image has a yellow cast, adding an equal amount of blue will make the colors neutralize one another. The yellow is removed, and the result goes toward white or gray, depending on the tonality. The same principle applies to the other pairs: red neutralizes cyan, and green neutralizes magenta.

The image we are working on has a green cast. That does not mean we need to add magenta. We need to remove green from the image, even though a simple tool in Photoshop may make the adjustment look as if we are adding magenta.

## Remove the green cast with Color Balance

Anchor: 1343 · U14 · L17 · 02:33–03:55

Back on the image, I use a simple method to remove a color cast: choose Image, then Adjustments, then Color Balance. The tool spells out the complementary pairs: red and cyan, green and magenta, and blue and yellow.

Here, I reduce the amount of green (captions: “grease”). This can look as if we are adding magenta, but notice that the value is negative: it is a negative value for green.

In more complex Photoshop tools, that negative value is the only thing you will see: it means a color is being removed, rather than a complementary color being added.

I adjust the image until the green disappears and the neutral colors look neutral. I can also check the white; here it looks good, so I click OK.

## Separate correction from creative color

Anchor: 1343 · U14 · L17 · 03:55–04:57

From here, we can color the image further. I put image color editing into two categories: color correction and creative coloring. Removing the green cast was a color correction; now I’m going to color the image creatively.

I reopen Color Balance by choosing Image, then Adjustments, then Color Balance.

Before making these changes, it is useful to separate the two steps. When comparing before and after, you want to compare the image with its color cast neutralized, rather than with the original cast still in it.

## A recipe for a warm tone

Anchor: 1343 · U14 · L17 · 04:57–06:35

There are no real rules for creative coloring. What you like may differ from what someone else likes. A popular treatment is to warm the colors in an image. It can be difficult to warm an image if you do not first understand the colors. If you consider only red, green, and blue values, you might reach for red, but red is a harsh color. Instead, look at magenta and yellow—in other words, remove green and blue.

I think of a creative adjustment like reading a chef’s recipe. This is my recipe for a warm tone, not a rule you have to follow: remove twice as much blue as green. In other words, combine yellow and magenta. I start with blue because that is where I’m making the biggest change, and reduce it by 20 units. Following the recipe, I set green to −10.

## Judge the image, then confirm the adjustment

Anchor: 1343 · U14 · L17 · 06:35–07:45

A common editing problem is overdoing an adjustment. This is especially likely when you are new to editing and pay more attention to the sliders than to what is happening in the image. Looking at the before and after is essential for judging how much to apply.

The Preview checkbox lets us see the before and after. Here, the image has an appealing warm tone. To push the effect further, I can add more to the adjustment; for a slightly weaker effect, I can reduce the values. When you are happy with the result, click OK.

So far, we have covered the basics of adjustments. As you progress through the course, you will learn more advanced tools. I hope you now have a better understanding of how to balance color and color images creatively. In the next lesson, we will learn how to crop and straighten images.

### Key points (derived)

- Correct tonal range first so color casts are easier to identify.
- Use complementary colors to neutralize a cast; for this portrait, reduce green to correct the cast.
- Keep neutral color correction separate from creative color grading.
- For this warm-tone example, start by reducing blue by 20 and green by 10, then judge the result by comparing the image before and after.
