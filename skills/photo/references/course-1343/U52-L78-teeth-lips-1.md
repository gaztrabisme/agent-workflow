## Replacing a pointed tooth

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L78 · 00:10–02:16

This lesson shows how to work with lips and teeth: perfect the shape of the lips, work with their color, and even simulate lipstick.

Liquify can be a quick, easy way to fix some teeth, but it is not always enough, and clone tools can be difficult to use. For these sharp, pointed teeth, I use a copied tooth to build the shape I want.

I zoom in, choose the Lasso tool, and set Feather to 5 pixels. That softens the selection edge without blurring it. I select a little more than I need; the selection does not have to be precise. I press Command or Control-J to copy it to a separate layer and rename that layer “tooth.”

With Free Transform (Command or Control-T), I move the copy over the tooth I want to correct and place it slightly above so I can compare the size. I angle it to fit and make it a little bigger if necessary. I use the arrow keys to nudge it, press V for the Move tool, nudge it down, and transform it again to rotate it slightly. The placement looks workable, although the top edge is still odd.

## Revealing the new tooth and removing the old edge

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L78 · 02:16–04:50

I hold Alt or Option while adding a layer mask, which makes a black mask, then choose the Brush tool with B. I keep brush opacity at 100% but lower the flow. Painting white reveals the copied tooth's edge. Its color and tone do not match yet; I will fix those later. I nudge the layer with V and the arrow keys until it fits, then rotate it a little more if needed.

I switch to black with X and paint on the mask to hide the strange edge. Some of what appears to be an edge is actually part of the previous tooth, so masking it away cleans the join. The mask can also reshape the tooth: painting across it visibly changes its shape, but I undo that because I do not want to reshape it completely. Teeth often shift in color toward their edges.

To remove the remaining little edge, I hide the copied tooth briefly, add a layer underneath, and call it “tooth edge remove.” I choose the Clone Stamp, make the brush smaller, and clean the small edge on that layer. I do not need to be especially precise because I am fixing only that little area.

## Blending the replacement and adding shadow

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L78 · 04:50–06:59

I switch the copied-tooth layer on and off so I can see where the old tooth ends and the new one begins. I lower the soft brush opacity to around 20% to blend some of the new tooth into the old one. I then mean to enter 2%, but I type too quickly and get 32% instead; I correct it. I paint black on the mask to hide the edge gradually, then switch to white with X and paint upward from the bottom to bring the new tooth over the old one. The before-and-after looks better, though the replacement needs darker shading on one side.

To show how efficient it is to have the action here, for a bit of Dodge and Burn I only need to come to the action and press Play on Dodge and Burn. I switch to black and set the brush opacity to 5%. I clarify the keyboard entry: pressing 5 gives 50%, while entering 0 then 5 gives 5%. I darken the side and enlarge the brush. Comparing before and after, that small shadow makes the copied tooth fit better.

## Reducing yellow without flattening the teeth

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L78 · 06:59–08:03

Next I add Hue/Saturation and choose Yellows. I sample the yellow on the tooth so Photoshop can select the color range I want to adjust (captions: “to Photoshop the right type of yellow”). I use Subtract to remove the fleshy areas from the selection. I take away some yellow, but not all of it; fully desaturating the teeth would look unnatural. I invert the mask and paint where the teeth are more yellow. The brush opacity is 100%, while the flow stays low so I can build the adjustment gradually.

## Brightening the teeth naturally

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L78 · 08:03–09:38

I can finish with a Curves adjustment to brighten the teeth slightly. While using my tablet pen, I sometimes double-click by accident. Photoshop asks whether I want to view the layer-mask properties or enter Select and Mask; for this work I would choose Select and Mask, although I do not want to open it now. I keep that choice in mind.

I name one layer “teeth brighten” and the next “teeth whitening,” then invert the whitening mask and start painting over the teeth. I set the brush opacity to 100%, then lower it to 50%, and use the reduced flow to build the effect gradually. That lets me brighten some areas more than others. The teeth farther out are already bright, so I leave them alone. I compare the original and edited versions by holding Alt or Option and clicking the background layer's eye. Zoomed out, the result looks natural.

## Avoiding repeated shapes

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L78 · 09:38–10:27

A copied tooth can look duplicated, which gives away the retouch. Liquify can help, or I can use Warp (captions: “a wolf”). I choose Free Transform, right-click inside it, and choose Warp. I zoom in and push the copied tooth to alter its shape. I would then refine the mask after warping. The before-and-after shows how a small shape change avoids an obvious repetition.
