## Choosing a global correction and combining Curves with Hue/Saturation

Anchor: 1343 · U37 · L58 · 00:10–03:54

Skin tone is one of the most important things to get right when editing a portrait. In this lesson I use Curves and Hue/Saturation to correct skin tones and enhance them. I downloaded several stock images to work on. There is no single prescribed skin tone: generally, we have to decide what feels right. I am not saying the examples are incorrectly edited; I am showing a different approach to coloring them.

In the first image, the skin feels a little too yellow, so I think an adjustment to the whole image will work. I prefer a Curves adjustment layer. In the Layers panel, I add one and call it "Skin tone." To remove yellow, I add blue: in the Blue channel I put a point in the middle because I am adjusting the image's overall tonality rather than one specific tone. I pull the point up until the result looks a little better.

Adding a color also adds light, so this has made the image lighter. I go to the RGB channel and pull it down to compensate. The image now feels a little different and pink, so I remove a tiny bit of red and add a little green. In fact, taking away some green probably helps. You can also use the keyboard arrows to nudge the points for finer control (captions: "very find you"). I compare the original with this first step in the right direction.

The reds in the skin now feel a little oversaturated, so I combine the curve with a Hue/Saturation adjustment and rename that layer "Reds." The target adjustment tool can sample a color from the image. I rarely click and drag with it, but I often click once in the image so Photoshop samples the color from that spot (captions: "form"). I also use the eyedropper to specify the particular shade of red. In the sliders at the bottom, I move Hue a little to the right to remove some red and desaturate it slightly.

I compare before and after. Option-clicking (Alt-clicking on Windows) the background layer's eye lets me see the untouched original against both adjustments. I think this is a good improvement and a good starting point, although I would still work on brightness and contrast in this picture.

## Preserving a blue setting while changing skin tone

Anchor: 1343 · U37 · L58 · 03:54–05:35

The next image has strong blues that work well, but the skin tone feels a little off. A Curves adjustment would add or remove color across the whole image, and I do not want to lose those prominent blues. Hue/Saturation is a better fit. If I wanted to use Curves instead, I would first make a selection or add a layer mask (captions: "I had to lay a mask off").

I add a Hue/Saturation adjustment layer and click the skin with the target adjustment tool. Photoshop selects Reds, as I expect, but the colors Photoshop detects do not always match how we perceive them. I use the eyedropper to select the more specific color, then shift it slightly away from red toward orange by moving the Hue slider a little to the right. The change is subtle. Comparing before and after, the skin loses some of its redness and looks slightly more appealing and natural. When I zoom in, the difference is easier to see.

## Using Curves when the skin has little color

Anchor: 1343 · U37 · L58 · 05:35–09:23

The third image has very little color in the skin. This is one reason it is useful to learn more than one Photoshop technique. Hue/Saturation may be a favorite method, but it will not always work, especially when the skin has little color to work with.

I add a Hue/Saturation adjustment and try shifting Reds. Not much happens. The hair shifts as well, and when I switch to Yellows, the petals change but the skin still does not change much. When I sample a color from the skin, Photoshop selects Cyans (captions: "sampler color here"), which does not make much sense for skin tones; again, very little changes. Hue/Saturation is useful, but it does not solve every case.

I delete that adjustment and add a Curves layer called "Skin tone," which makes it easy to find again when I open the image. The skin is very light, so I can focus the edit around it. I put the target adjustment cursor on the skin and click. Photoshop places a point on the curve, and I drag it to add red. In the Green channel I take away a little green to make the color more pinkish-magenta, without going too far. Then I go to Blue and take away some blue to add a little yellow. I compare the before and after; the result looks good.

Here is another tip: if you like an adjustment but want to strengthen it, you can duplicate the adjustment layer. We often lower a layer's opacity to reduce an effect, but opacity cannot go above 100 percent. Duplicating with Command-J or Control-J makes the effect stronger. You could instead move the curve points again, but a duplicate is another way to take the adjustment a little further.

## Sampling a skin color and judging RGB changes visually

Anchor: 1343 · U37 · L58 · 09:23–12:05

For the final example, I want to show a technique some of you might like. It is something I am working on, and it gives a visual way to look at colors. I use the Eyedropper to sample the skin on the man's forehead, then open the foreground color picker. This shows the selected color. When working with Hue/Saturation or Curves, it can feel abstract to judge how the color is changing, so this technique makes the changes more visual. You still need to use other adjustments after this step.

In the color picker I look at the RGB values. I click R for red and ignore the color spectrum, focusing on the bar. The current value is 225 units of red. Adding red moves the color toward one end of the bar; reducing red moves it toward the cyan-blue end. I raise it slightly to add a little red.

Then I go to Green. Raising it adds green; lowering it subtracts green, or adds magenta. I make a small shift down. The picker shows the current sampled color and the color I have moved to. This now looks a little too pink, so I pull the green value back up slightly. In Blue, I think the color looks better a little further down, so I lower that value too. Compared with the current color, I find this result appealing.

## Applying the sampled values with a Curves adjustment

Anchor: 1343 · U37 · L58 · 12:05–15:27

To apply the color to the image, I use the values. I note 234 for red, 205 for green and 181 for blue. Your numbers will probably differ if you clicked a slightly different place in the picture, so follow along with your own values or use mine. I click OK, add a Curves layer and call it "Skin tone." The target adjustment tool can put a point on the curve for a tone in the image.

There is a shortcut: on a Mac, hold Shift-Command; on Windows, hold Shift-Control while clicking the same area. The curve seems to move up and down in the bright area, but at first it looks as though no points have been added. The points are there in the Red, Green and Blue channels. This saves clicking three times.

In Blue, my target value is 181 and the current value is 188, so I lower it to 181 with the keyboard arrow. In Green, my target is 205 and the current value is 207, so I lower that slightly. In Red, my target is 234. Holding Shift while pressing the arrow moves the value in steps of 10, so I bring it to 234. Comparing before and after, this now matches the color we chose earlier (captions: "this year"). If I click roughly the same spot again and open the color picker, it should show 234, 205 and 181.
