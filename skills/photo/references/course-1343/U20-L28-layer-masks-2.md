## Brighten the darker part of the photograph

Anchor: 1343 · U20 · L28 · 00:00–01:40

The top of this photograph of strawberries is considerably brighter than the lower part. A gradient mask can even out the brightness. I choose Curves to brighten it. Brightness/Contrast could give almost the same result, but Curves lets me make a more sophisticated adjustment later. For example, I can brighten the image while protecting its very darkest tones, which is difficult or sometimes impossible with other tools.

I add a Curves adjustment layer, rename it Brighten lower part, and raise a point in the middle of the curve. I judge the change by looking at the lower portion of the image. I can always change it later.

## Choose a gradient for the mask

Anchor: 1343 · U20 · L28 · 01:40–04:28

Before working on a mask, get into the habit of single-clicking its thumbnail. I pick up the Gradient tool. Its location can differ between Photoshop versions; it used to be grouped with the Paint Bucket, so check there if the tool is not where you expect.

The options bar previews the gradient colors. Clicking the preview opens presets. The interface has changed: I preferred the older display, which showed a grid of gradients, while the newer version organizes them in folders and takes longer to browse. One useful feature is that I can drag a favorite to the top. It then appears with a blue line rather than a square around the Basics folder, and stays visible when I collapse that folder.

The preset that looks white-to-black is actually Foreground to Background, so it changes with the colors set in the toolbar. If I had selected red and yellow before clicking the mask, those colors would be replaced by white and black, because masks use white, black, and gray. Another useful preset is Black and White; I also pull out Foreground to Transparent. I recommend keeping Foreground to Background as the default because it is easy to alter its grade. For now, I choose it and click OK.

## How the gradient line sets the transition

Anchor: 1343 · U20 · L28 · 04:28–06:18

Alt- or Option-click the mask thumbnail to look at the mask. At this point it is all white. With a white-to-black gradient, the area above the starting point becomes white, the area below where I release the mouse becomes black, and the space between forms the transition. A short line makes a sharp transition; a longer line makes a smoother one.

I can draw diagonally, and reversing the direction swaps which side is white. Drawing from the bottom up puts white at the bottom and black at the top; drawing from the top down puts white at the top and black at the bottom. I can also draw at an angle. Holding Shift constrains the line to be straight, including at 45 or 90 degrees.

## Apply the brightening and correct its color shift

Anchor: 1343 · U20 · L28 · 06:18–08:22

The curve brightens the lower part, so the mask needs white at the bottom, where the adjustment is revealed, and black at the top, where it is hidden. If the no-entry symbol appears, the image thumbnail is selected instead of the mask. Single-click the mask before using the tool.

I want the white area down at the bottom and a gradual fade up to where the image is already bright. I press G for the Gradient tool, drag a straight line while holding Shift, and release where I want the effect to stop. Toggling the layer shows that the lower part is brighter while the top remains unedited.

Brightening can also shift color. The lower strawberries now look a little pinker than those at the top. I return to the curve and add a little yellow; removing blue gives yellow.

## Combine a gradient with brushwork

Anchor: 1343 · U20 · L28 · 08:22–10:37

A gradient can be combined with manual brushing. This strawberry at the bottom is now a little too bright. To bring it back to how it looked before the adjustment, I paint black over it with the normal Brush tool. I single-click the mask and press B. Make sure black is the foreground color. Press X to swap foreground and background. You will use this shortcut over and over, so make a note of it. I remember X as “exchange,” though I do not know whether that was Adobe’s logic. The square-bracket keys next to P make the brush smaller.

## Darken distracting areas in another photograph

Anchor: 1343 · U20 · L28 · 10:37–13:21

Here is another stock photograph, a market display of carrots, leeks, and other vegetables. It could be used as it is, but editing can also recompose an image. The bright strip at the right and the bright box draw attention away from the carrots and leeks. The cloth on the table and those signs could use some darkening too.

I add a Curves adjustment, name it Darken edges, and pull the curve down. Then I select the mask thumbnail and the Gradient tool. The foreground is black, so the gradient runs from black to white. A common misunderstanding is to think black darkens and white brightens. The mask controls whether the adjustment is hidden or revealed: here the curve darkens the image, so white in its mask darkens the photograph.

There is a Reverse option in the gradient controls. Reverse is useful for certain things, but here the easiest approach is to press X to swap the foreground and background colors, making this a white-to-black gradient. I position the cursor around the middle of the cloth, perhaps halfway or two-thirds up, and drag. A very short gradient would leave a sharp edge or visible trace, so I hold Shift to keep the line straight. The lower part becomes darker. It can seem as if the rest of the image has become brighter, but the actual change is that the lower part has darkened. Toggle the layer to compare; the change should look believable rather than obviously brightened or darkened.

## Add angled gradients without losing the first one

Anchor: 1343 · U20 · L28 · 13:21–14:28

To darken another side of the image at an angle, I drag a new line in that direction. The new gradient replaces the earlier one, so the darkening at the bottom disappears. I could add another adjustment layer, but that becomes tedious if I want three or four gradients on the same adjustment. I undo with Command-Z or Control-Z to restore the bottom gradient.

## Build several gradients with transparency

Anchor: 1343 · U20 · L28 · 14:28–16:06

To retain the first gradient, I choose Foreground to Transparent. This gradient has one color that fades to nothing. Painting white to transparent leaves the black already underneath in place and does not replace the white that has already been painted. I draw from white into transparency, and the mask now holds both gradient areas.

The result darkens the left-hand corner as well as the bottom. I can add the same effect on the right. The resulting triangular darkened edges echo the main subject; it is a little like a vignette, but with straighter edges. Compare the before and after.

### Key points (derived)

- A gradient mask can blend an adjustment gradually across an image.
- The mask thumbnail must be selected before drawing or painting on it.
- Combine gradients and brush strokes on one mask to control where an adjustment appears.
- Foreground-to-transparent gradients can add another transition without replacing existing mask content.
