## Keep expression while reducing lines

Anchor: 1343 · U40 · L62 · 00:00–01:26

In this lesson, I’ll show a few techniques for reducing wrinkles. The aim is to reduce them rather than remove them: sometimes they can be softened almost until they disappear, but removing every line from a face can look unrealistic. Before retouching the first portrait, I correct its color because it looks pale and cool. I use Curves, rename the adjustment layer “Colours,” add a little red, and take away some blue.

## Build a low-opacity clone pass

Anchor: 1343 · U40 · L62 · 01:26–04:06

I add a blank layer and call it “Lines” rather than “Wrinkles.” “Lines” is a more polite name, particularly if the person being retouched might see the work. I zoom in and select the Clone Stamp. It is generally best to use the tool at full opacity because lower opacity can lose texture; in this example, though, losing some texture can help. I lower opacity to 30% by pressing 3, and lower flow with Shift+3. When working on a blank layer, I set the sample to Current & Below (or All Layers); otherwise, the Clone Stamp will not work. I hold Alt or Option and click to sample.

![1343 · U40 · L62 · 01:32](frames/U40L62-F0132.00.jpg)

On screen: “Opacity: 100%” / “Flow: 100%” / “Sample: Current & Below” / “Lines” [rest omitted]  
Shows: Close view of the first portrait, with the blank Lines layer selected and the Clone Stamp settings visible.

I start reducing the skin texture with broad, fairly relaxed strokes. I do not need to be very precise, and I am not too concerned if I cross into the eye or over the eyelashes because I will use a mask later. There may not be much nearby skin to sample, depending on the image, so the sampled color can be faint or fail to match. I am not too worried at this point, but the color and texture still need to be reasonably close; they cannot be completely wrong. The first pass looks soft and a little strange. I add a little more to connect the two areas, then compare before and after.

## Color-correct and reveal the clone through a mask

Anchor: 1343 · U40 · L62 · 04:06–07:37

After the clone pass, I check how it fits. The retouched area should be a little darker, and it has become slightly too pink. I add a Curves adjustment called “Skin fix” and clip it to the layer underneath. Clipping makes the curve affect only that layer. The clipped layer name is underlined and has a small arrow beside it; moving the curve up or down also shows that only the cloned layer is changing. I darken the area a little and correct the pink cast. A little color is useful, and I may make it slightly brighter; I can return to the adjustment later.

![1343 · U40 · L62 · 04:28](frames/U40L62-F0428.00.jpg)

On screen: “Skin fix” [rest omitted]  
Shows: The selected Skin fix Curves adjustment above the Lines layer, with the portrait visible.

![1343 · U40 · L62 · 05:22](frames/U40L62-F0522.00.jpg)

On screen: [rest omitted]  
Shows: The adjusted curve and the color-corrected clone area under the eye.

I add a black mask to hide the layer’s contents. I can Alt- or Option-click the layer-mask symbol, use the Invert button, or press Command+I on Mac or Control+I on Windows (the captions read “Command or Control R”). I select the Brush with B, set a low opacity, and press X so white is the foreground color. Then I paint on the mask to bring the cloned area back gradually. It is good to work slowly at low opacity: it can seem as though nothing is happening, which is often a good sign in retouching. I increase the brush opacity to 10% for this demonstration and use a larger brush to cover a broader area.

## Use dodge and burn to preserve skin texture

Anchor: 1343 · U40 · L62 · 07:37–12:44

For another example, I use a portrait of a woman in a striped sweater. This time I’ll brighten and darken the lines with dodge and burn, a common retouching method and one of the best ways to get natural-looking results. There are several ways to do it; here I’ll show a simple one.

![1343 · U40 · L62 · 07:41](frames/U40L62-F0741.00.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: The full portrait of a woman in a striped sweater, open in Photoshop.

I add a blank layer, call it “lines,” and change its blend mode to Overlay. Overlay is a contrast blend mode: values darker than mid-gray darken what is underneath, while values brighter than mid-gray brighten it. I zoom in on the wrinkle. It has a light area, but the dark area is the most obvious part. With this technique, I brighten the dark parts and darken the light parts; leaving the light areas untouched can make the result look strange.

![1343 · U40 · L62 · 09:07](frames/U40L62-F0907.00.jpg)

On screen: “Overlay” / “lines” [rest omitted]  
Shows: Close-up of the second portrait’s under-eye lines, with the Overlay layer selected.

I choose the Brush with B, use a small brush and start at 5% opacity by typing 05. I paint over the darker areas first. If 5% is too low, I can increase it; 10% is quite high here, so I move to 8% by typing 08. The line gradually becomes less visible. The color may shift slightly red, so I may need to correct it with a Hue/Saturation layer afterward. I press X to switch to black and paint over the lighter areas. When I zoom out and compare before and after, the line is almost gone. The advantage is that I have not changed any pixels or cloned anything, so the skin texture matches perfectly. It takes time and can be fiddly, but the result can be very good.

I zoom in again to finish the color correction. I add a Hue/Saturation layer, target Reds and use the eyedropper to sample the line. Because it is so thin, I change the sample size to Point Sample. I click to sample the color, then use the minus eyedropper to subtract colors that I do not want to target. This gives me a focused color selection. I shift it slightly toward orange, reduce the saturation and lighten it.

It is difficult to get the color perfectly right on the first try without a mask, so I apply a mask and can revise it later. I select the mask, invert it, choose the Brush and paint with white to reveal the adjustment. I raise the brush opacity to about 30%. The before-and-after result is not perfect yet, but with more work it can reach very good quality.

## Replace a small area, then restore the eye’s shape

Anchor: 1343 · U40 · L62 · 12:44–15:44

For the last example, I return to cloning, but more aggressively. Instead of blending two areas together, I replace the area. I zoom in with Command+1 on Mac or Control+1 on Windows, add a blank layer and name it “eye fix” (the captions read “I fix”). I select the Clone Stamp, set its opacity to 100% by pressing 0, hold Alt or Option and click to sample, then make the brush smaller.

![1343 · U40 · L62 · 13:00](frames/U40L62-F1300.00.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: The full portrait of a dark-haired woman in a black shirt before the close-up clone replacement.

![1343 · U40 · L62 · 13:29](frames/U40L62-F1329.00.jpg)

On screen: “Opacity: 100%” / “Sample: Current & Below” / “eye fix” [rest omitted]

The before-and-after shows that the line has been reduced, but the eye has lost some of its shape. I have also covered a few small eyelashes. I add a mask, press B for the Brush and X to switch to black, then paint on the mask to bring those lashes back.

![1343 · U40 · L62 · 14:11](frames/U40L62-F1411.00.jpg)

On screen: [rest omitted]  
Shows: Close-up of the eye after the eyelashes have been brought back on the mask.

To restore the shape, I add a Curves adjustment and darken the area. I invert its mask, select the Brush, use low opacity—10% here—and press X so white is the foreground color. I make sure the brush is soft, then work gradually so that individual strokes do not show. I match the shading above the eye. I zoom out and Option-click the background layer’s eye icon to compare the original with the retouched image. I could add a little more shading and use the Healing Brush to tidy the area between the eyes, but that would be another way of reducing lines in an image.
