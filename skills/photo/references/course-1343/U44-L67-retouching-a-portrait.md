## Clean distractions while preserving skin detail

Anchor: 1343 · U44 · L67 · 00:11–03:48

I’m going to retouch a portrait using techniques from the course. I encourage you to use your own portrait, or the same image I’m using if you don’t have one. At the end, upload your image so I can give you feedback on your editing and the techniques you used. Let’s get started.

I create a new layer called Cleanup and remove general distractions at 100% magnification. A mark near the bottom looks suspicious; I think it is a reflector caught in the frame, so I remove it with the Healing Brush. For the small flecks on her jumper, I use the Spot Healing Brush, which works well.

Next I create another layer, Cleanup skin. I use the Healing Brush because the Spot Healing Brush is too basic for this. As I remove small lumps and bumps from her skin, I take care to retain the original texture. I reshape her eyebrows just a little. When I notice smudging, I undo it and switch to the Clone Stamp. That avoids the smudging, but I need to pay more attention to blending the colors and making the texture fit.

She enjoys having hair flowing across her face, but I think some of the little flyaway hairs look better removed. I’m fairly happy with the cleanup and will move on to softening her skin.

## Soften skin and restore texture

Anchor: 1343 · U44 · L67 · 03:48–08:02

I make a composite layer for the skin softening, call it Skin soften, and convert it to a Smart Object so it stays editable. Under Filter > Blur, I choose Surface Blur (the captions render the filter name as “Leur”). The preview looks okay, though the result does not look very good on its own; with experience, you will learn what looks good for skin smoothing.

I click OK, add a black layer mask and pick up the brush at a fairly low opacity. I paint the effect in gradually. It is important to take my time and avoid using too high an opacity. I set the brush to 15% (the captions say “held it 15%”). I also consider which area I’m brushing: if the skin does not need smoothing, I leave it alone and soften only the areas that need it.

The result looks good, but I have lost some of the original texture. I create another composite layer above it, call it Skin texture and convert it to a Smart Object. I apply a High Pass filter. To avoid color artifacts, I desaturate the layer and set it to Overlay. I put both layers in a group called Skin soften. The mask is not doing what I want on the layer itself, so I move the mask onto the group. Now the original skin texture comes back only where I softened the skin. This is not a fashion or beauty portrait, so this amount of softening keeps it fairly natural.

## Brighten the eyes and soften under-eye lines

Anchor: 1343 · U44 · L67 · 08:02–11:51

Next I brighten her eyes with a Curves adjustment called Eye brighten. The colors already look good, so I set the adjustment to Luminosity. I check whether Normal or Luminosity works best by toggling between the two states. It does not seem to make much difference, so I leave it on Luminosity.

I invert the layer mask, pick up the brush and paint the effect in at low opacity to brighten this area. Even though hair covers one eye, I still brighten it a little to match the other eye, then brighten the other eye too. I reduce the small dark spot inside the eye by brightening it (the captions call it a “dog spot”). I check the two blend modes again as I work.

I also want to remove some wrinkles underneath her eyes and brighten them while keeping the result natural. I use another Curves adjustment set to Luminosity (the captions read, “Not a curve. Can use luminosity for that”). I invert its mask, select the brush, zoom in close and raise the brush opacity slightly. I call the layer Eye lines. Another option would be to use a little Clone Stamp work, but I like the natural look of the result.

## Adjust skin color selectively

Anchor: 1343 · U44 · L67 · 11:51–15:22

I want to warm up her skin tones without changing the rest of the image. I add a Hue/Saturation adjustment. Her skin tone feels a little yellow, so I select yellow and push it slightly toward red. I sample the color; what I considered yellow, Photoshop considers red. The thought process is similar when skin has problematic redness: if the skin lacks red, I can use the same technique to add a little more life to the tone. I shift it slightly and keep the change subtle. I remove the adjustment from the lips (the captions read, “I love the lips there”).

I also want to add a little more color, so I use a Curves adjustment and make a selection with Color Range. I sample the skin tones, and Photoshop picks them up automatically. The selection looks good, so I click OK. Photoshop has built a mask for me. I do not want to change the logo, so I paint the adjustment away from it. The ear is already quite warm, so I avoid that too. I keep the amount low and perhaps make it a little brighter (the captions render this as, “Quite a low man. Possibly just a bit brighter”). I change the blend mode to Color so that only the color is affected. Both adjustments work on the skin tones, so I put them in a group. When layers do similar things, it makes sense to group them.

## Sharpen selectively and review the portrait

Anchor: 1343 · U44 · L67 · 15:22–18:47

The last thing I want to do is sharpen the picture. As I've shown you before, when I sharpen portraits, I like to sharpen the image overall but sharpen certain areas more than others. I make a composite layer called Sharpen, convert it to a Smart Object and choose Filter > Other > High Pass (the captions render the name as “high paths”). That looks okay. I also want to bring out the little logo on her top, so I remove the color and change the blend mode to Overlay.

The sharpening has a nice effect on the eyes and lips. The hair is a little too sharp, although the logo looks good. The skin is now very textured. I add a mask and fill it with 50% gray; that gives me 50% of the effect overall. I brush with white where I want to enhance the sharpening and with black where I want to hide it. The hair looks a little too strong, so I reduce the sharpening on the skin, particularly around the nose. Nobody likes lots of texture on their nose.

I toggle the sharpening for a final before-and-after check at 100% magnification (the captions read, “before the sharpening, and often”), then make sure the nose is fully covered by the mask.

Anchor: 1343 · U44 · L67 · 18:47–19:16

I look forward to seeing your results. Upload your images and I will give you valuable feedback. You have reached the end of this course, and I hope you have learned how to retouch your portrait. In the next course, you will learn more about high-end retouching, and we will step things up a level.

### Key points (derived)

- Keep retouching editable with separate layers, Smart Objects and masks.
- Preserve skin texture and apply softening or sharpening only where it helps the portrait.
- Use low-opacity brushes and keep changes subtle for a natural result.
- The unit task is to retouch your own portrait using your own decisions and tools, upload it for teacher feedback.
