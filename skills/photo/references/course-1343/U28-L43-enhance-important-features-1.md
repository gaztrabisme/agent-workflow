## Set the hierarchy and crop

Anchor: 1343 · U28 · L43 · 00:00–02:39

In this lesson, I’ll show how to bring out the most important features of an image by making selections and working with adjustment layers. The photograph is by my student Andy Weatherburn, and I really like this image. At first it may not seem very interesting, partly because we have not brought one specific subject into focus. An edit should make clear what we find important in the photograph and why it was taken. Here the bright, distracting background contributes little, even though it contains information. The picture is really about the man in the newsstand, while the busy surroundings are part of what makes the scene appealing.

I would not normally crop in the raw converter, but if a part of a photograph is not important to the image, cropping here can help the edit. This crop is non-destructive: I can return to the crop tool and recover the original. In Photoshop, by contrast, deleting pixels could make it impossible to go back if I change my mind. I choose the crop tool and a two-thirds ratio, matching the original image, then crop so the newsstand is the focus and press Enter or Return to commit. I can reopen the crop tool later; the original image remains visible behind the crop.

## Balance the global tones

Anchor: 1343 · U28 · L43 · 02:39–04:41

The crop changes the histogram to represent the part of the photograph I consider important. It now looks a little dark, so I raise Exposure slightly. Exposure and Brightness are not the same. In the raw converter, I am trying to bring out as much information as possible, then I can refine the image in Photoshop. For brightening in Photoshop, a curve is a better tool than trying to get the whole effect from Exposure.

Some highlights look too bright, so I darken them and lift the shadows. I am trying to even out the tonal range by bringing the highlights closer to the shadows. This makes the image look a little thin, so I darken the blacks slightly. I avoid pushing Blacks too far in the raw converter. If I want very heavy blacks, I can create them in Photoshop with a layer mask so I can bring detail back in places where it would otherwise be lost. There is plenty of texture, so I raise Texture a little; Clarity adds contrast in the midtones, and a small increase helps here. The photograph has many colors, so some extra Vibrance works well.

## Work with color and white balance

Anchor: 1343 · U28 · L43 · 04:41–06:09

I work with color after the exposure and shadows are right; changing white balance before that is not useful. I can try a preset such as Daylight or Cloudy, or use the white balance tool. In a scene with sunshine, shadows and artificial lights, it can be difficult to know which balance I want until I try it. I click areas that should be neutral and let Camera Raw set the white balance based on what I click (the teacher says “get Photoshop to set the white balance based on what you click on”). I decide that the image works slightly warmer, but not as warm as the current setting, so I bring it back and remove the tint. I can always adjust white balance again later.

## Even the top and bottom with gradients

Anchor: 1343 · U28 · L43 · 06:09–10:26

After the Basic section, I look for ways to even out the image further. The bottom is bright with daylight, while the top is dark. I use the Graduated Filter to lift the top. I first reset the tool by clicking the plus; this clears all its settings. If I then choose another adjustment, such as Shadows, the Exposure value will be reset too.

![1343 · U28 · L43 · 07:00](frames/U28L43-F0700.00.jpg)

I therefore reset everything before every new gradient, then set the sliders manually. I prefer to set the values before drawing, though I could draw a zero-value gradient and add the settings afterward.

I place the cursor near the dark line at the top, click and drag, and hold Shift to keep the gradient straight. I release it where most of the daylight reaches the front of the kiosk (the captions transcribe this as “frontier”), then lower the highlights a little. For a second gradient, I click New. The red and green circles disappear and are replaced by white, showing that the first gradient is inactive. Changes now affect the new gradient only. I do the opposite at the bottom: lower Exposure by somewhat less than half a stop and darken the highlights. I start from experience and adjust after seeing the result; no matter how experienced I am, I cannot know the exact settings in advance.

I drag upward from the bottom while holding Shift. The before-and-after looks better, but the top now feels brighter than the bottom.

## Lift selected areas with radial filters

Anchor: 1343 · U28 · L43 · 10:26–12:45

After this correction, the upper-right part of the image is still dark, making the lighting feel oval. I use a Radial Filter. Resetting the settings is usually good practice, but here the current values are a useful starting point: a little Exposure, slightly lower Highlights in case they become too bright, and lifted Shadows. I zoom out with Command or Control and Minus because the oval will extend just beyond the image. I put the cursor at the center of the area, drag out the oval and check the result. I add a little Clarity.

I click New for another radial adjustment over a second, more circular area. I keep the same settings, place the cursor in the middle of the area I want to affect and drag outward. I review the changes so far with the before-and-after view.

## Rebalance the newspapers and tobacco sign

Anchor: 1343 · U28 · L43 · 12:45–16:08

The newspapers are now drawing too much attention because they are brighter. I like the tobacco sign, but it is also too bright. I first darken that area with another radial adjustment, then switch to the Graduated Filter. I use the same settings, keep the gradient straight with Shift, and make the Exposure and Highlights adjustment stronger. When I lift the Shadows to compensate, I lose some of the effect, so I add another radial adjustment. Editing involves going back and forth; as I move through the image, I notice new things to change. I also darken the blacks in that area and raise Clarity. I review the result with the earlier gradients included.


On review, the top now feels too bright. I reduce its Shadows and Exposure, then darken the Highlights. That makes the area I had adjusted too dark, so I return to the Radial Filter, click New, and add a little Exposure and lift the Shadows there, leaving Highlights at zero for now. The image looks better after these small local corrections.

## Restore contrast behind the glass

Anchor: 1343 · U28 · L43 · 16:08–17:25

Anything behind glass loses some contrast, so I try Dehaze. A value of 25 is much too strong; I try 10 instead. I am not concerned if the radial shape goes beyond the glass because the effect there will be small. I want to do the same thing on the other fridge. Usually I click New before another adjustment, but if I want exactly the same settings, I can draw a new shape as soon as the cursor becomes a crosshair again. The effect could be a little stronger, and some Clarity should help, so I add those to both adjustments.
