## Why lipstick changes the lip contour

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L79 · 00:11–01:06

I often work on lips, particularly when someone is wearing lipstick. In that case, I am not necessarily tracing the lips exactly; I am creating a shape. We are always trying to perfect the shade and shape.

I will use the Pen tool. We looked at it earlier in the course, but it is not an easy tool, and even if you have used it before, you may have forgotten how to use it. It takes practice to get the hang of it, so do not give up if you are struggling.

## Trace the shape with the Pen tool

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L79 · 01:06–03:50

I press P for the Pen tool and click-drag to make the first point, tracing the shape I want the lips to have rather than necessarily following their exact edge. The handles control the curve: pulling them farther or moving them up or down changes its shape. To edit one handle independently, I hold Alt or Option. When the curve changes direction, I adjust the handle on the right; at the next turn, I hold Alt or Option and pull the handle down.

If the lipstick extends too far, I can bring the path inside the lip. I hold Command on Mac or Control on Windows to select a point, then nudge it out slightly with the arrow keys and continue drawing. I use Alt or Option again to adjust the handles and close the path.

The Path Selection tool selects the path; Direct Selection lets me work on individual points within it. I can move a point slightly and use the arrow keys instead of dragging it, which gives me better accuracy. Here I give the contour a little more curvature.

## Save the path and selection

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L79 · 03:50–05:27

When I am happy with the shape, I go to the Paths panel and save the path. A new path is initially called a Work Path, which is temporary, so I double-click it and rename it “Lips.” That keeps the path in the document.

I can create a selection from the path using the button at the bottom of the Paths panel. I can also save a selection through Select > Save Selection; Photoshop stores it as a new channel. I name that selection “Lips.” To reload it, I can use Select > Load Selection, or choose the Lips channel and load it as a selection. In this image, Lips is the only saved selection. It is useful to preserve a sharp-edged selection first, then make a softer edge afterward.

## Soften the edge

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L79 · 05:27–06:56

I open Select and Mask. The default Overlay view can make lips disappear, so I can choose On Black, On White, or whichever view suits the image; Black and White is also useful here. I soften the selection gradually while keeping it defined. For this image, a feather of about five or six pixels works. The path looks slightly tight around the lip edge here, but that is okay.

## Clean the lipstick edge outside the lips

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L79 · 06:56–08:49

I prefer to begin with the outside edge. There is some excess lipstick, so I inverse the selection; even though nothing seems to change, zooming out shows that everything except the lips is selected. I create a new layer called “Lips outside edge.”

If the marching ants distract me, I can hide them in the View menu. I usually keep them visible because a strange selection can be easy to miss and cause mistakes. I choose the Clone Stamp, make the brush a little bigger, and sample fairly close to the area I am copying. I am moving the edge. I use a soft brush with 0% hardness. If the match looks odd, I can add a little Healing Brush work on top. That extra work improves the match, and I continue around the edge to tidy it. If an edge needs more definition, I can use a little Dodge and Burn to darken it. If it isn't broken, don't fix it (captions: “on fixed words isn't broken”); I only tidy the areas that need it.

## Extend the inside edge, compare and group

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L79 · 08:49–10:57

I inverse the selection again with Shift-Command-I on Mac or Shift-Control-I on Windows, make a new layer called “Lips inside edge,” and use the selection to keep the work inside the lip boundary. There is not much to change, but I can extend this area a little. I copy part of it and move it down. I sample straight above and look for repeated patterns. Where patterns repeat, joining the lines removes the obvious repetition. I sample closer above another pale patch and move it upward a little; if it looks too light, Dodge and Burn can correct it.

I deselect with Command-D or Control-D and compare before and after. The outside layer changes the outer contour, while the inside layer extends it. Either can look unnatural, so I reduce their opacity to around 65 or 70% to bring some of the original edge back. I put the layers into a group with Command-G or Control-G and name it “Lips.”

## Add shape with Dodge and Burn

Anchor: 1343 · U52 · L79 · 10:57–12:27

I run the Dodge and Burn action with the brush ready. I paint white at low opacity, around 10%, through the center of the lips to give them more shape. Then I switch to black, bring the lower-left corner in a little, and work the edge inward. The before-and-after shows a small, natural increase in shape. I zoom out and compare the original and edited portrait by holding Alt or Option and clicking the background layer's eye.
