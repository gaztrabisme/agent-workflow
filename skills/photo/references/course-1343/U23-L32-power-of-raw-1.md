## Open RAW in Camera Raw and arrange the workspace

Anchor: 1343 · U23 · L32 · 00:00–02:45

This course is about retouching environmental images: learning to edit landscapes and other environmental images. We will start with the power of RAW compared with JPEG, and I will introduce ACR, or Adobe Camera Raw, and the raw converter's workspace.

Whenever you open a RAW file, it has to go through Camera Raw. Do not click through and open the image straight into Photoshop. While the image is still in the raw converter, we can make the most of its tonal range and pull as much information as possible out of the highlights and shadows. Camera Raw is continually updated with Photoshop. This version, Camera Raw 12.3, came out just the other day. I am recording this lesson after some of the lessons you will do later, so you may notice a small layout change.

The filmstrip is now along the bottom of the image. This layout is more in line with Lightroom and other programs. I still think the left side is more efficient, partly because I am used to it and because it uses the space better. Right-click the filmstrip, open its options, and change its orientation to vertical.

## Establish the tonal range before changing color

Anchor: 1343 · U23 · L32 · 02:45–09:20

White balance is not attached to a RAW file, so we can change it freely. I do not recommend starting with white balance unless the white balance is completely wrong. Work on the tonal range before color, because you cannot judge the image's true colors until its brightness is right.

The Exposure slider changes the image's exposure. Even when an image is well exposed, I often pull exposure down quite a lot first. That quickly shows me the highlights and their quality. In this mountain image, darkening exposure brings out the sky and clouds.

![1343 · U23 · L32 · 03:53](frames/U23L32-F0353.00.jpg)

On screen: "Exposure -2.25" [rest omitted]  
Shows: The mountain image darkened to inspect the sky and clouds, with Exposure at -2.25.

Then I move the other way, perhaps one and a half to two stops, to inspect the shadows. This gives me a picture of what I can and cannot get from the file before I begin editing. It is almost as important to know what you cannot do. If darkening the highlights reveals details that do not look good, those details may draw negative attention even though they have been recovered.

Contrast is an important, visually creative adjustment, but I do not make many creative edits in the raw converter, so I typically leave it alone. I can lower Highlights to improve the sky, open the Shadows, and compensate by darkening Exposure slightly. The result is already much better than the original. In the example, I pull Exposure to −0.15, Highlights to −100, and Shadows to +95.

Generally, I do not darken Whites. That can turn a true highlight into a washed-out gray. If the whites are not bright enough, brighten them instead of trying to retrieve details by darkening them. Blacks are important too. Here I use the histogram to make sure there is a true black in the image. I would not make a strong black adjustment here just for a creative look; that is better done in Photoshop, where masks make it easier to work and revisit an adjustment. The settings made in the raw converter become the permanent starting point. There are ways to reopen the image in Camera Raw from Photoshop, but they can also cause problems.

Texture, Clarity, and Dehaze are controls I will demonstrate on other images. Saturation makes colors more vivid. Vibrance is a kind of smart saturation: it tends to have a stronger effect on blues and tries to protect skin tones. I will cover that in more detail in another lesson.

After the tonal adjustments, I move to white balance if the image needs it. The student who took this mountain photograph gave me permission to use it. Its starting histogram has very little in the midtones, but editing brings more information into the middle. The tint is set to −4, adding green, which suggests to me that the picture was probably shot with auto white balance. Its colors are therefore unlikely to be very accurate. I do not want green in daylight, so I move the tint a little toward magenta and make the temperature slightly warmer. The comparison preview shows the original and the basic edit. Seeing that improvement after a few seconds of work helps show the power of RAW.

The white balance eyedropper can be used on a neutral gray area. I generally do not use it unless I have photographed a gray card or a ColorChecker chart with neutral gray in the image, though it can be useful for trying a few options. There is also an eye icon for previewing the edit. The keyboard shortcut shown for this preview is the backslash key. Unlike Photoshop layers, where we click to toggle a layer, here we click and hold the icon to turn the preview off, then release to turn it back on.

## Bring out texture and reduce haze carefully

Anchor: 1343 · U23 · L32 · 09:20–11:35

In this brick-building photograph, increasing Texture brings out the brickwork. Texture and Clarity are closely related: Clarity works on the midtones and increases contrast within them, which strengthens contrast in much of the image's texture.

Dehaze is a very strong filter. In the sailboat photograph, light is hazy because the image was shot over water toward the sun. Increasing Dehaze makes the background clearer and sharper, but the saturation rises strongly too.

I do not think this slider should go beyond 50, so use it sparingly. It can help to pair Dehaze with negative Saturation, since pushing Dehaze too far can look artificial.

Here is another hazy-light example, shot toward the sun over water in humid conditions. Haze gives the background a faded look. As Dehaze increases, the haze is reduced. In a landscape without people in the foreground, the added saturation may look a little better, but it is still too strong for my taste, so I would take the edge off with negative Saturation.

This one simple slider has a large effect on the picture.

## Use the tone curve when the image needs a stronger push

Anchor: 1343 · U23 · L32 · 11:35–13:02

The curve has been improved in this recent update. The parametric curve is not really to my liking: it is simplified and divides the tonal range into four sections, with sliders for Highlights, Lights, Darks, and Shadows. Curves is one of my favorite Photoshop tools, and I prefer the manual curve, where I place points and move them.

As I said earlier, I rarely make creative adjustments in the raw converter. I would work with the tone curve here only if the image needed a strong push. Otherwise, I prefer to continue in Photoshop, where I can keep working with masks and blend modes. We can also choose individual color channels. In the latest update, the curve shows the color being added and its complementary color when it is subtracted: cyan for red, magenta for green, and yellow for blue.

## Keep sharpening and noise reduction under control

Anchor: 1343 · U23 · L32 · 13:02–15:43

The Detail section contains sharpening. I prefer to sharpen in Photoshop, so I recommend leaving the default sharpening setting here. There are two noise-reduction controls: Luminance Noise Reduction and Color Noise Reduction. Use luminance reduction sparingly. It reduces noise, but it also softens the image. I would rather keep some noise and retain sharpness than remove the noise and soften the picture.

The default Luminance Noise Reduction value is zero, while the default Color Noise Reduction is 25. Camera Raw therefore removes some color noise by default, and it works well, especially in high-ISO images made in low light. Zoom in on a shadow to inspect the result. It is rare that you need to push color noise reduction further, but it can help with noisy, high-ISO files. Zoom to at least 100% when judging it.

There is an unusual shortcut change. Command or Control+1 used to zoom to 100%, but from the first version of the raw converter it added a one-star rating instead. In this new version, the shortcut finally changed; Command or Control+1 now returns to the Basic section. The shortcut for 100% magnification is Alt+Control+0 on Windows or Option+Command+0 on Mac.

### Key points (derived)

- Open RAW files in Camera Raw first, and evaluate the highlights and shadows before color.
- Keep RAW-converter edits restrained when you need the flexibility of Photoshop masks and layers.
- Use Dehaze, luminance noise reduction, and sharpening with care; inspect details at 100% or larger.
