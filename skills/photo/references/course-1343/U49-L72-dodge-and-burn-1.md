## Understanding dodge and burn

Anchor: 1343 · U49 · L72 · 00:00–03:48

Dodge and burn, also called brightening and darkening or light and dark (L and D), is one of the purest forms of retouching: it brightens or darkens pixels without changing their structure. The names come from the darkroom: to dodge is to brighten, and to burn is to darken. Whatever name you use, it is a powerful technique to learn properly.

I begin with a circle to show how light and dark can change an object's shape and make it look three-dimensional, a kind of tonal shaping we use often in retouching. I select the Brush tool with B, use the default colors and add a layer above the circle. To keep the paint inside the circle, I clip the new layer to it: I hold Alt or Option while placing the cursor between the two layers and click when the clipping symbol appears, or right-click the empty area of the layer and choose Create Clipping Mask. A clipped layer keeps the paint within the layer below, so I do not have to worry about going over the edge.

I lower the brush flow to its lowest setting with Shift+0+1. With light imagined above the circle, the sphere would have light on top. I enlarge the brush, undo the first stroke because the brush is too hard, set the hardness to 0% and lower the opacity. I paint white softly across the upper part. Then I press X to switch to black and darken the lower part; pressing X again switches back to white (captions: “Y”), and I add a little more light at the top. The light and dark begin to make the circle feel like a sphere. I add a layer underneath and paint black on it to make a shadow, so the sphere reads as a three-dimensional object against the background.

Simply painting with black and white in our images is hugely beneficial.

## Setting up a photo retouch

Anchor: 1343 · U49 · L72 · 03:48–05:43

In the photograph, I want to reduce the darker areas under the model's eyes, the shaded area above his lip and a few marks nearby. Cloning can do this, but it often removes some of the original skin texture. If the person has good-quality skin, dodge and burn can be a useful alternative.

I create a new layer and change its blending mode to Overlay. With Overlay, painting with a color darker than middle gray darkens the image underneath, while painting with a color brighter than middle gray brightens it. On a transparent layer it can be hard to see where the paint is, so one option is to use Edit > Fill and choose 50% gray (captions: “50% gradient”). That takes two steps, which I try to avoid. Instead, I create the layer through Layer > New Layer or use the keyboard shortcut, name it Dodge and Burn (captions: “Sarge and Burn”), set its mode to Overlay and tick the option to fill it with the Overlay-neutral color, 50% gray. This does the setup in one step.

## Painting gradually and preserving form

Anchor: 1343 · U49 · L72 · 05:43–09:44

I demonstrate the layer by painting a small white area, which becomes lighter, then switching to black to darken another area. Toggling the layer shows the brightened and darkened areas; hiding the background shows the black and white marks on the layer. The gray in the image is not necessary for the effect; it mainly helps show where I have painted, so I remove it.

Brush size matters. A soft brush is the right choice for work like this. Its painted area is a little larger than the circle shown in the brush preview, because the circle indicates the main body of the brush. If the brush is exactly the size of the dark area, the soft edge may brighten or darken beyond it. Use a slightly smaller brush than you think you need. Rather than treating a broad patch as one dark area, I notice several smaller adjacent dark patches and even those out first with a small brush. Once they are closer to the shade of the larger area around them, I enlarge the brush and work across the broader area.

I zoom out to check the result. The area is still slightly darker; I want it to stay natural. Matching the rest of the skin's tonality exactly would flatten the face and remove its three-dimensional appearance. The method works the same in color, but black and white makes the tonal differences easier to see. When working in color, I often put a layer at the top of the stack to remove the color temporarily so that I can concentrate on tonality.

## Refining details and creating shape

Anchor: 1343 · U49 · L72 · 09:44–12:13

I also brush a little over his lips. Comparing before and after, the adjustment is efficient. Once I reduce the darker area above his lip, I notice a light line that now stands out, so I zoom in, make the brush smaller and darken that line slightly. If I go too far, I can flatten the face, but that is not a problem by itself: I often start by flattening uneven areas, then make another dodge-and-burn pass to shape the image, as with the sphere.

For example, I can make the collarbone more pronounced by painting black in its shadow and white in the lighter area. I probably went a little too far in this demonstration and should have used lower opacity, but it shows how the method works. In the same way, I can create shapes that are not necessarily present in the original image, such as making the model's shoulders look slightly more defined.
