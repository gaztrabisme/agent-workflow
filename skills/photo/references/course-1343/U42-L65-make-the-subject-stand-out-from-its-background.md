# Lesson 65 · Make the Subject Stand Out From Its Background

## Choosing a clean frame and crop

Anchor: 1343 · U42 · L65 · 00:00–01:40

The aim is to make the subject stand out from the background and draw attention to the subject. I’ll do that with selections, adjustment layers and a layer mask. One straightforward approach is to brighten the subject, darken the background, or combine both; contrasting colors can also help. It is also important to look for distractions.

I took the first example on my friends’ wedding day in central London. I wanted an urban picture, but the tunnel made it almost impossible to get a clear background. Cropping is a good way to remove distractions. We could make the picture square or portrait-oriented, using the original aspect ratio. In the Crop tool’s options bar, I choose a square ratio and shift the crop a little until it fits. There is still a bright light at the end of the tunnel, but it reads as the end of the tunnel and is not very distracting, so I commit to the crop.

## Isolating the background and comparing edits

Anchor: 1343 · U42 · L65 · 01:40–04:52

The couple already look a little bright, so I leave them mostly as they are and darken the rest of the picture. I use the Lasso tool with feathering at zero, then control the softness in Select and Mask. I draw a loose selection around the couple, choose Select and Mask, and use a high feather value for a very soft transition. I do not mind if the feather extends slightly onto them. I invert the selection so that the background is selected. If too much of the couple is included, I adjust the selection; Shift Edge can make its edge larger or smaller.

I add a Curves adjustment layer called “Background darken” and keep the change subtle. I can build it up in several goes (captions: “goals”). One area on the left is still brighter, so I make another selection there, beginning at the top and bringing it in a little. I use Select and Mask again, this time with less feathering, then add another curve called “Darken L.”

Next I darken the whole image slightly and increase contrast. I call the first adjustment “Brightness,” then add a curve called “Contrast” and pull a point down all the way toward the bottom of the curve. This also increases color saturation more than I want, so I change the blend mode to Luminosity. That adjusts brightness without changing the colors as much.

I compare the edit with the original, then compare it with the very original, including before the crop. In the History panel, the camera icon creates a snapshot. There is a snapshot of the original by default, and I can make additional snapshots as the edit progresses. Before taking the image in a different direction, it is useful to save a snapshot so I can compare versions without going back through the history or undoing edits. History and snapshots are not saved with the image, though, so I cannot return to them after closing it.

## Removing a bright mark and repeated distractions

Anchor: 1343 · U42 · L65 · 04:52–07:39

I use a second picture of the couple. I see three distractions: a bright light on the tree trunk, the grass showing through the broken-up background, and a bit of path behind the tree. Our eyes are drawn to irregular things, so these areas keep pulling attention. Cropping would be tight here. I would clone the bright area out. I would also darken it to make it more similar to the rest of the tree trunk, then darken the tree a little more.

I first try the quickest removal method. If it works, that is great; if not, I can use the manual Clone tools. I create a blank layer with Shift-Command-N on Mac or Shift-Control-N on Windows, and call it “Tree Fill.” With the Lasso tool I select the area to remove. I keep feathering off because the selection is right beside a corner, where feathering can cause problems. Then I choose Edit > Content-Aware Fill.

The green areas on the left show where Photoshop is sampling. It is odd that it samples part of her hair. It does not seem to make much difference, but I would remove those areas anyway. It also catches his suit, so I paint over that edge and a little of her dress. The fill makes a slight improvement. If I want to include other areas in the sample, I can use the plus icon in the options bar. I am happy with the result and click OK. Because the fill is on a new layer, it remains editable. I compare before and after, then use Command-D or Control-D to deselect.

There is also a repeated pattern in this view. For speed, I use the Spot Healing Brush to paint over a couple of recognizable shapes. There may be other repeated shapes, but none stand out to me, so I leave them.

## Softly darkening uneven areas

Anchor: 1343 · U42 · L65 · 07:39–09:11

I darken this part of the tree with a Curves adjustment. I pull the curve a little farther down than I want, change the blend mode to Luminosity to avoid changing the colors, and select the layer mask. I invert it with the menu command or Command-I / Control-I. Then I press B for the Brush tool and D for the default colors.

I want low opacity, so I raise it from 10% to 30% and lower flow to 10%. The keyboard shortcut 3 sets opacity to 30%; Shift-1 sets flow to 10%. I use a soft brush and change its size for the area I am painting, starting smaller in the narrow parts and making it a little bigger elsewhere. With low opacity and flow and a soft brush, it is not a problem if I cross the edge slightly onto the couple; even when I do that on purpose over his suit, the effect is not large. I compare before and after.

## Making the couple stand out from the tree

Anchor: 1343 · U42 · L65 · 09:11–12:18

As with the previous image, I press L to select the Lasso tool and make a loose selection around the couple. I zoom out to see the edges and try to keep the distance between the couple and the selection edge consistent all the way around. I open Select and Mask and increase feathering to soften the edge. The couple are selected, not the outside of the tree trunk, so I invert the selection. This also lets me see whether the couple would be darkened. I increase feathering until I begin to see them, back off slightly, and click OK.

I add a curve called “Tree trunk” and rename the earlier adjustment “Tree highlights.” I darken the darker tones more than the highlights: I am not trying to brighten highlights to increase contrast, but to put a little more adjustment into the shadows. I use two points on the curve and set the blend mode to Luminosity to avoid color shifts. I tighten the mask around the bright area on the left by selecting the mask, pressing B for the Brush tool, and painting there. I can also darken the previous curve a little more.

The left side of the background is brighter than the right. To even it out, I add a curve and darken it only a little, keeping the left side lighter. I select the Gradient tool with G, make sure the colors are pure black and white, and apply the gradient from white on the left toward the right, over a short distance. I hold Shift to keep the gradient level. This darkens the left side without making it darker than the right, and the couple stand out more.

I then add a little overall contrast. I am careful not to lose the detail in the dress (captions: “desalination address”). I set this curve to Luminosity, then lower its opacity to fine-tune the adjustment.

## Applying the approach

Anchor: 1343 · U42 · L65 · 12:18–13:14

To compare the finished image with the original, I hold Alt or Option and click the eye icon for the Background layer. Across these pictures, I use a crop, cloning, and local or overall brightening, darkening, and contrast to draw attention to the subject. Each picture is different, so there is no single right way to do it. I experiment to learn what works best. Making the subject stand out can make a big difference. In the next lesson, I will sharpen portraits and make some parts of an image stand out more than others.

### Key points (derived)

- Separate the subject from the background with selections and masks, then use subtle brightness and contrast adjustments to guide attention.
- Remove or soften distractions with a crop, cloning, Content-Aware Fill, Spot Healing, local curves, or a gradient, choosing what suits the image.
- Keep edits editable on separate layers and compare stages with snapshots before closing the image.
