## Prepare the image and read its tonal values

Anchor: 1343 · U49 · L73 · 00:00–02:43

Before using dodge and burn to perfect skin, I recommend doing a normal cleanup first and removing the larger imperfections. After that, the image can be converted to black and white, or more precisely, shown without color. A Black & White adjustment layer renders each color at a different tonal value, and Hue/Saturation can also change tonality in an odd way. Instead, I add a Solid Color layer, make it middle gray with no saturation and set its blending mode to Color. This leaves only the image's tonality visible. You can work this way without making the change permanent, but I want to show another way to inspect the tonal differences.

I add a Curves adjustment and use the Target Adjustment tool with a 5 by 5 Average sample. Even with little practice, I can spot slightly darker tones on the model's forehead. If I darken one of those tones and brighten the lighter one next to it, the change in tonality becomes easier to see. I put these adjustment layers in a group called “reference.”

## Work the skin on a separate layer

Anchor: 1343 · U49 · L73 · 02:43–07:45

I keep the dodge-and-burn layer underneath the two reference layers. I select the cleanup layer and add the dodge-and-burn layer above it, so the painting is on the color image while the grayscale reference makes the tonal work easier to see. I lower the brush opacity by pressing 2 for 20%, check the foreground color (captions: “or why does my foreground color?”), make the brush smaller and begin painting. This is detailed work, and there is no shortcut or magic filter that will make skin flawless.

I check my progress regularly by toggling the layer. Here I have painted too far and the skin starts looking too light. My opacity was too high, so I lower it and paint black over some of the areas that became too bright. Looking at the image in color and zooming out, I can see that I have gone a little too far overall; reducing the layer opacity helps the result fit. Brightening can also cause a slight shift toward a more saturated color. If that happens, I can add a Hue/Saturation adjustment above it and paint in a little desaturation.

I continue with the reference visible because it makes the tonal differences easier to judge. The goal is not to remove the shadow but to even the area out. I look for a darker part, a nearby part that is slightly lighter, then one that is slightly darker, and I switch between black and white as I work. I check the before and after without the reference as well. Once I can see the tonal differences more easily, I can turn the reference off and work while looking at the finished image.

## Compare the Curves method

Anchor: 1343 · U49 · L73 · 07:45–09:34

Another option is to use two Curves adjustments grouped together: one curve brightens and the other darkens. I add a black mask to each, then use a white brush to reveal where I want brightness or darkness. Curves use a little less file space than a painted dodge-and-burn layer, and they can be fine-tuned afterwards. That is useful because after lowering the painted layer's opacity, further tweaks can be more difficult. The drawback of Curves is that I cannot dodge and burn in the same layer; I have to keep switching between the two. For that reason, the single layer is often more efficient, and it is generally the method I prefer.

## Use dodge and burn beyond skin

Anchor: 1343 · U49 · L73 · 09:34–12:00

Once you learn the technique, it can become a favorite way to edit. It works for many things, including evening out skin. Because it does not move any pixels, it preserves a natural-looking skin tone and texture. I can also use it to brighten the woman's eyes: I start with a fairly large brush and gradually focus the strokes on each eye, making the change subtle. I could add a little more, or switch to black to darken an area in the eye.

I can brighten the teeth as well. Dodge and burn can also shape features naturally, such as making lips look a little fuller. I add a darker line along the top and bottom of the lip, switch to white and give the lip more shape. The advantage of the single layer is that all the work stays together, and increasing the layer opacity lets me see exactly where I have painted. I can also add a little Gaussian blur to even out brush strokes that are too visible.

I hope you enjoyed learning about dodging and burning, since it is an important part of retouching. In the next lesson, we will look at Photoshop's digital scalpel: Liquify.
