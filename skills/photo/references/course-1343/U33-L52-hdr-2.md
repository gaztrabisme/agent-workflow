## Reveal the dark highlights gradually

Anchor: 1343 · U33 · L52 · 00:00–02:00

He sets Flow to 5% and switches Airbrush off. With the Brush selected, D restores the default foreground and background colors, pure black and pure white. X swaps the foreground and background colors. The square bracket keys next to P change brush size. He makes white the foreground color; painting on the mask then reveals the layer with darkened highlights.

He demonstrates on the small couch, sofa, and armchair (the captions render “sofa here” as “so far here”), then gives the ceiling lights more definition. He compares the result with the original and says the brushwork was easy and effective. At low flow the effect builds gradually, so painting a little over an edge is not disastrous. For him, working very gradually is the key to masking.

He is using a mouse, but would usually use a tablet for this work because it becomes easier once mastered. If a tablet feels difficult at first, he advises giving it time. With a tablet, lighter pressure can make the brush smaller or reduce the flow.

## Refine the window mask and draw a straight edge

Anchor: 1343 · U33 · L52 · 02:00–05:21

To darken the window, he makes the brush smaller. A tablet lets him press more lightly to get a smaller brush or lower flow. When he checks the result, the window looks patchy and needs more work; the captions read “before and often” where he says “before and after.” Sometimes it is easier to paint over the whole window, including its frame. He shows the frame becoming darker, then zooms in, chooses a smaller and slightly harder brush, and switches to black to paint the effect back off the frame.

For a sharp edge, he uses Shift. He Alt- or Option-clicks the layer mask thumbnail to view the mask and sees that his brushwork is not straight.

## Add global curves for brightness and contrast

Anchor: 1343 · U33 · L52 · 06:30–08:46

He recalls that he first showed how to access Merge to HDR Pro from Bridge; in Photoshop, the feature is under File > Automate. Since this work is a composite, he recommends putting adjustments above all the layers so they affect the whole image. He adds a Curves adjustment and makes the image slightly brighter, naming the layer Brightness. He adds another curve for contrast and inserts a point to pull the blacks down.

He can move curve points with the keyboard arrows. Plus selects the point above the current one, and minus moves down through the points; then he uses the arrows to move the selected point up, down, left, or right. He finds this useful when fine-tuning a curve. He pushes the bottom point down for very dark blacks, while the other two points control the rest of the tonal range. Comparing before and after, he thinks the image has more definition.

## Shape color with a Black & White adjustment layer

Anchor: 1343 · U33 · L52 · 08:46–10:09

For a more stylized color treatment, he zooms out with Command-minus or Control-minus and adds a Black & White adjustment layer. He jokingly names it Coloring and changes its blend mode to Soft Light. This maps the image’s colors to grayscale, letting him control each color in relation to its grayscale value. Darkening the yellows affects the tabletop. He also works with Greens, says Cyan may pick up a little of the sofa (captions: “Siam will probably pick up on that Sulfur a little bit”), and adjusts some Blue.

The coloring feels too strong, so he lowers the layer opacity. He prefers to keep the result fairly subtle, though he says there is nothing wrong with leaving the layer at 100% if that is what the viewer wants.

## Keep the room’s natural brightness relationships

Anchor: 1343 · U33 · L52 · 10:09–11:24

He compares the original with the current result and wants his manual HDR image to remain photorealistic. The living room naturally receives more light than the kitchen, so it stays brighter in the image; the outdoor area is brighter than the living room. Automated HDR software or Photoshop’s automated feature tends to make everything the same brightness, which can look hyper-real or slightly fake. This is why he prefers his manual version, though he encourages viewers to try Merge to HDR Pro. He closes by recapping the benefits of high dynamic range images and says the next lesson will cover multiple-exposure images.
