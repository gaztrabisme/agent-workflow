## Why I retouch with a tablet

Anchor: 1343 · U45 · L68 · 00:00–01:50

Welcome to the fifth and final course, *High-End Retouching*. We will build on what you have learned in the earlier courses, add a few new things, and raise the work to a higher commercial standard. The quality should be good enough for magazines, for example. In this first lesson, I will introduce my tablet, show how I set it up, and explain how I program its buttons and scroll wheels on the tablet (the captions say “on the table”).

I normally find that I cannot do proper retouching without a tablet. At first, using one can be confusing because it feels very different from using a mouse. It took me about a week to get from wanting to throw the tablet out the window to getting the hang of it. After that, I could not really do retouching without it. Brushwork in particular is much quicker and more accurate. It is like drawing with a pencil: a pencil gives you a more detailed drawing. I use a Wacom Intuos Pro, but even the most basic tablet will improve on using a mouse for most things.

## Assigning the tablet controls

Anchor: 1343 · U45 · L68 · 01:50–03:26

I set up the tablet and its functions in System Preferences. The settings have options for the device, which is the tablet I am using, and for the tool: the tablet functions and the pen. I can also control settings for different applications. If I use Illustrator or another artistic program, I might want to change the settings for that program.

The tablet I am using has six keys and a touch ring. I have set the bottom key to Pan/Scroll, which works like pressing and holding the spacebar: it temporarily gives me the hand tool. The next button is Option, or Alt. When I use the clone stamp or healing brush, that makes it easy to change my sample points. I have set the next button to X, which swaps the foreground and background colors. When I work with layer masks, for example, I switch between those colors often, so having that shortcut here is useful. The buttons at the top are Undo, Redo, and Toggle Last State. Toggle Last State effectively means undo followed by redo.

## Touch ring, gesture settings, and pen feel

Anchor: 1343 · U45 · L68 · 03:27–05:16

The next thing I can change is the touch ring. I often set it to control brush size. Zoom and rotate can be useful, but most of the time brush size is what I want to change, so sometimes I go in and disable zoom and rotate. I do not use the radial menu; for me, it loses the point a little. It is an additional menu that I can set up and access with one of the ExpressKeys.

The tablet I use has touchscreen gestures disabled, and I prefer it that way. I often have my keyboard in front of the tablet. When I put my arms down, they can move something and start a brush stroke, so I would rather leave gestures disabled.

Then there are the pen settings. Tip Feel changes how pressure-sensitive the tablet is. I like the idea of a firm setting, where I need to press quite hard to reach a full brush size. The drawback is that I have to keep pressing fairly hard. Although I like the idea of firm pressure, I set it closer to the middle, just a little on the firm side.

## Testing soft and firm pressure

Anchor: 1343 · U45 · L68 · 05:17–06:50

Let me show you the difference. I will start with a soft setting in Photoshop. I make a new layer, select the brush by pressing B on my keyboard, and make the brush a little bigger. To change the ring setting, I press the middle button and set it to Brush Size. Even when I use quite light pressure and then press quite hard, the marks look much the same.

Now I will go back to System Preferences and change the setting. With a firm tip, I start at quite low pressure, which gives me a really small brush. As I gradually press a little harder, you can see the brush size grow. I like that idea, but if I brush gently somewhere and am not paying attention, I end up with a really small brush. I have to keep pushing hard. That is why I prefer the setting nearer the middle, but slightly on the firm side. I will set Tip Feel back there. I can also customize the pressure, but I think this setting works quite well. If I do a lot of work with the art-based brushes in Photoshop, tilt sensitivity might be something to explore. Personally, I leave it on.

## Pen buttons, eraser, and mapping

Anchor: 1343 · U45 · L68 · 06:51–08:07

When I first got my tablet, I thought it was great that I could set the little buttons on the pen to do things too. Initially, I think I set one of them to X to swap the colors. What happened, though, was that I often pressed the button by accident and made mistakes. I decided to disable the buttons, which works better for me.

The pen also has an eraser. I turn the pen around to use it, and I can customize its feel as well. I use a setting that is slightly firmer. Finally, I leave Mapping on its default setting. This means that the proportions of the screen are represented by the tablet: the tablet’s top-left corner corresponds to the top-left corner of the screen.

## Brushwork with a tablet and a mouse

Anchor: 1343 · U45 · L68 · 08:08–10:06

There are many reasons why working with a tablet is better. If you watch a retoucher next to someone who is fairly new to retouching and working with a mouse, you will see how quickly the retoucher lays down brushstrokes. Each stroke may do slightly less, but the strokes gradually build up to much better results.

I do not know if I can show it here, but I will delete this layer and make a little scribble with the tablet. As you can see, I can use different pressure to make different brush sizes. Then I use the mouse. Keep in mind that the mouse still has a place: I use it quite a lot, but very rarely for brushwork.

I hope these tips have been useful. I strongly advise investing in a tablet if you want to do high-end retouching. It is very difficult to do good-quality retouching with only a mouse or trackpad. A tablet makes a huge difference to both quality and the time you spend. With the pen, I can make many more brushstrokes, and make them more quickly, than I can with a mouse. In the next lesson, we will learn how to plan the edit and create a roadmap for retouching.
