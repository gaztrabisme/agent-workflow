## Liquify and the Forward Warp tool

Anchor: 1343 · U50 · L74 · 00:00–01:30

Liquify in Photoshop lets you manipulate the pixels in an image and push them in any direction. It can be used to slim arms, nip and tuck, correct backgrounds, and remove creases in clothes, among other things. To begin, choose Liquify from the Filter menu.

The first tool is Forward Warp. Imagine the image is made of paint, or is in a liquid state, with black paint on top of white paint. Forward Warp lets you push that paint around. It is useful for evening out a shape. On the exercise canvas, he pushes a small squiggly line back together, then undoes the stroke.

## Brush size and density

Anchor: 1343 · U50 · L74 · 01:30–04:00

Before working further, understand the brush settings in Properties. Forward Warp is brush-based. Its options include size, density, and pressure; the default size is 100, density 50, and pressure 100. Forward Warp has no rate setting.

Size also depends on brush density. With density at 100, a brush of about 300 pushes across its whole area. At density 50, the push is more concentrated toward the center. The lower the density, the more the push focuses on the center. Set density to 100 to push with the entire brush, to zero to push only at its very center, or choose a value between those extremes.

For the line, he uses a fairly high density, around 70, which pushes its sides as well. After undoing, he chooses a slightly smaller brush and higher density, which works better. A small bump hanging down from the center of the brush needs a much lower density and a smaller brush.

## Lower pressure and gradual corrections

Anchor: 1343 · U50 · L74 · 04:00–06:00

Pressure is very high at 100. He compares it to opacity: lowering pressure lets him work more subtly. He undoes the last stroke and reduces pressure. He often works at a pressure of about 2, 3, 4, or 5. At that setting, he needs several brush strokes to make the push, which tends to produce a smoother, more natural-looking result.

To even out another part of the line, he uses higher density and a larger brush, then starts with a smaller brush on a bump and gradually increases its size. For the bumps here (captions: “lamps”), he uses a smaller brush and slightly lower density. He pushes the line up a little, and often works from opposite sides to even it out.

## Reconstructing an edit

Anchor: 1343 · U50 · L74 · 06:00–07:12

The Reconstruct tool gradually brings back what has been changed, a little like a History Brush. Pressure controls how gradually it works. Painting over the edited line starts restoring its original shape. This is useful when a push has gone too far: rather than trying to push the line back into place with Forward Warp or another tool, use Reconstruct. Here it gradually returns the line to a perfectly straight shape.

## Smoothing and twirling

Anchor: 1343 · U50 · L74 · 07:12–08:30

The Smooth tool evens out lumps or bumps made by other tools. It does not work on a line that was already bumpy; it smooths bumps introduced by editing. It can be useful as a finishing step, because pushing a line into place should not leave bumps.

The Twirl tool is not one he usually uses. It could be useful for creating reflections in water: holding the mouse button down spins the image around. It can create distortions, but is not often used for portrait retouching.

## Pucker, Blow, and rate

Anchor: 1343 · U50 · L74 · 08:30–09:21

The Pucker tool squishes things together. Applied to a line, it makes the line narrower. It could, for example, make someone's nose smaller (captions: “pocket”). The Blow tool does the opposite: use it to add volume or stretch an area. Pressure is disabled for this tool; instead, it has a rate setting, which controls how quickly the effect is applied while the mouse button is held down. The caption calls this tool the “pocket tool” at this point, though the description is of Blow.

## Push Left and stroke direction

Anchor: 1343 · U50 · L74 · 09:21–10:59

The Push Left tool seems to be named logically, but on a line it can move the line up and then down rather than obviously left. “Push pixels” might have been a clearer name. To move a line left, paint from the bottom up; to move it right, paint from the top down.

Imagine the two sides of a leg or arm that needs slimming. Push each line toward the center: move one right and the other left. He demonstrates those two directions. The example uses high pressure only to make the tool's action obvious; he would never use such high pressure on an arm. Higher pressure makes mistakes more visible, so lower pressure tends to give a better result.

## Protecting neighboring lines

Anchor: 1343 · U50 · L74 · 10:59–12:10

The lower part of the exercise has two close lines. To push one line down, he paints from right to left. As the brush approaches the next line, it starts moving that line too. He demonstrates the problem with Forward Warp by pulling one line down until the neighboring line also moves.

![1343 · U50 · L74 · 12:08](frames/U50L74-F1208.00.jpg)

On screen: "Size: 150" / "Density: 48" / "Pressure: 58" [rest omitted]  
Shows: The magnified inset shows both close horizontal lines bending down together under the brush.

In a photograph, the top line might be an arm or leg, and the next one could be part of clothing or the background. Moving both would make the edit obvious. Undo the unwanted strokes and use the Freeze Mask tool: paint over the neighboring line, then apply Forward Warp again. The protected line will not move, leaving a safety net while the other line is pushed.

## Freeze Mask, Thaw Mask, and face recognition

Anchor: 1343 · U50 · L74 · 12:10–13:36

Freeze Mask is useful for protecting an edge. To remove the mask, use the Thaw Mask tool (captions: “Thor mask”). Once the mask is removed, the bottom line can be pushed, or both lines can be pushed together. This exercise file gives a little more insight into how Liquify works.

There are a few more options, including Face-Aware Liquify. He will show it on another picture. This canvas has no face, so Photoshop will not recognize one here.
