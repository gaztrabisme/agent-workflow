# Course 669 — Intimate Photography Portraits

Working notes for later skill design. Lesson references use the section time anchors from the reading copy. “(frame)” marks an observation made from an opened course image rather than a spoken claim.

## Method steps

1. **Set a direction for the session.** Decide what kind of image or space you want, then think through the conversation, location, time, and equipment that can support it. The course presents these as the preparation before practice. (669 · U1 · L03 · 00:00–00:37; 669 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:39; 669 · U2 · L06 · 00:00–01:22)
2. **Build comfort with the model.** Meet and talk casually; listen, ask genuine questions, observe the person’s gestures and ways of moving, and let them choose how much to share. When conversation has run its course, ask what music they want and begin photographing without making a ceremony of it. (669 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:36; 669 · U2 · L04 · 01:36–03:24; 669 · U2 · L04 · 03:24–06:21)
3. **Choose and prepare a location for the intended picture.** A small or ordinary space can work; choose a background and light source, frame out distractions, and move furniture when needed so the light and room work together and the arrangement still looks natural. For outdoor work, account for time of day; for interiors, consider the window position and the available light. (669 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:39; 669 · U2 · L05 · 01:39–03:59; 669 · U2 · L05 · 03:59–05:58; 669 · U2 · L05 · 05:58–07:18)
4. **Choose equipment for the framing.** Use a wider lens when the space or multiple people should have prominence; for a one-person portrait where the setting is secondary, use the lens that lets the face dominate. A wide maximum aperture is her preferred portrait tool for shallow depth of field, but she says the named lenses are not a requirement. (669 · U2 · L06 · 00:00–01:22; 669 · U2 · L06 · 01:22–03:00)
5. **Explore available light and the model’s response.** Try positions, framing, and gestures; notice how the light falls and refine the pose around it. With shutters, place the eyes in a gap between light bands if that is the desired effect. Try different views and activities, and stop an idea when the conditions defeat it (the balcony wind dispersed the smoke). (669 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–02:45; 669 · U4 · L10 · 03:49–05:57)
6. **Use the room and found objects experimentally.** Move furniture, clear an area, and test the light again. Try a mirror table, a reflection, hosiery or plastic near the lens, a coaster or CD to redirect sunlight, or small flowers near the lens; compare results and remove or reintroduce an effect when it does not yet work. (669 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–00:56; 669 · U3 · L08 · 00:56–02:51; 669 · U3 · L08 · 04:01–05:56; 669 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:43; 669 · U3 · L09 · 01:43–03:15; 669 · U3 · L09 · 03:15–04:31; 669 · U3 · L09 · 04:31–07:05)
7. **Edit by scene, then adapt.** Start with a representative image from the first space and settle a base treatment for light and color. Carry it to other photographs from that space, adjust each one, then establish or adapt the treatment for another scene. (669 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–03:49; 669 · U4 · L10 · 03:49–05:57; 669 · U4 · L10 · 08:53–10:59)
8. **Export and make an initial selection.** Edit the images that seem promising, export them, then review the work by space. Mark candidates, preserve differences in distance, viewpoint, gesture, and moment, and compare near-duplicates for the more convincing expression or detail. (669 · U4 · L10 · 03:49–05:57; 669 · U4 · L10 · 15:06–17:56; 669 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–03:14; 669 · U4 · L11 · 03:14–06:44)
9. **Let the pictures rest and narrow the group over time.** Leave at least a day, or a few days, between editing and selection when possible. Use the first selection as a working group, return to it later, and gradually identify the images that continue to matter to you. (669 · U4 · L11 · 08:22–12:37; 669 · U4 · L11 · 12:37–15:44)
10. **Put the finished work in front of the intended audience.** Match the portfolio or project to the people or venue you want to reach, contact them directly, and keep practicing and pursuing opportunities. (669 · U4 · L12 · 00:41–02:21; 669 · U4 · L12 · 02:21–03:35; 669 · U4 · L12 · 03:35–04:18)

## Principles and claims

- A preliminary conversation is meant to create trust and a safe space in which the model can move and pose without feeling judged. (669 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:36)
- Listen for the model’s distinctive gestures, looks, posture, and ways of moving; recall them later so the photographed person remains recognizably themselves. (669 · U2 · L04 · 01:36–03:24)
- The model decides how far to open up. A light or superficial conversation can still lead to a good session if the person feels comfortable. (669 · U2 · L04 · 03:24–06:21)
- The teacher presents her own conversation style as personal, not universal; a photographer should find and test an approach that feels comfortable to them and creates trust and calm. (669 · U2 · L04 · 06:21–08:07)
- An unremarkable room may be enough when a neutral background and a light source serve the intended image. (669 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:39)
- Framing can exclude people or objects that do not serve the picture, making a busy place appear empty or different. (669 · U2 · L05 · 01:39–03:59)
- The time of day changes the light and therefore the feel of a place; strong afternoon sun can produce a large difference between light and shade, while low evening sun can be warm and side-lit. (669 · U2 · L05 · 03:59–05:58)
- In an interior, furniture can be moved to make the room, light, and frame work together; large spaces and extensive equipment are not required for the examples shown. (669 · U2 · L05 · 05:58–07:18)
- A 35 mm lens is her choice when the setting or several people need room in the frame; her 50 mm is her usual lens for a closer one-person portrait. (669 · U2 · L06 · 00:00–01:22)
- A wide maximum aperture gives shallow depth of field, which she uses to direct attention to the face. She presents lens choice as personal and says these specific lenses are not required. (669 · U2 · L06 · 01:22–03:00)
- Through glass, reflections and the person behind the glass can add information and visual layers to a photograph. (669 · U3 · L07 · 03:34–05:05; 669 · U4 · L10 · 08:53–10:59)
- Frontal light slightly above eye level places shadows on cheekbones and below the chin while flattening the appearance of under-eye circles, which the teacher calls a flattering, practical setup. (669 · U3 · L07 · 05:05–07:45)
- For balcony photographs, she prefers direct front light or backlight and finds mixed patches of sun and shade too contrasty and forced. At noon, high backlight may look forced, so she chose front light. (669 · U3 · L07 · 05:05–07:45)
- A finger near the lens can soften a corner and make the moment feel observed through an intervening object; the teacher says it can change the image’s effect. (669 · U3 · L07 · 02:45–03:34)
- For a small interior portrait through glass, she changed the door angle to remove her own reflection while retaining the park and trees, and used manual focus because autofocus could choose among several planes. (669 · U3 · L07 · 03:34–05:05)
- A light effect that is not convincing can be removed and reconsidered later; the teacher first set aside hosiery, then tried it again after changing her distance and framing. (669 · U3 · L08 · 00:56–02:51)
- The opened mirrored-table frame shows Andrea’s face paired with its inverted reflection beneath it. (frame) (669 · U3 · L08 · 04:01–05:56)
- Sunlight bounced from a glass table produced a rainbow on a balcony door and a colored patch across Andrea’s face and eye in the opened example. (frame) (669 · U3 · L08 · 05:56–07:00)
- Small objects near the lens can create effects: a coaster reflects a golden spot, a partly covered CD changes the reflected shape, clear plastic creates flares, and small colored flowers tint dark parts of the image. (669 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:43; 669 · U3 · L09 · 01:43–03:15; 669 · U3 · L09 · 03:15–04:31; 669 · U3 · L09 · 04:31–07:05)
- A clear piece of plastic with a hole over the lens can soften part of the image and produce bright flare shapes around a deliberately chosen subject. The opened example shows a diffused layer across the portrait while Andrea’s face remains visible. (frame) (669 · U3 · L09 · 03:15–04:31)
- Showing the model the photographs, praising specific poses the photographer likes, and celebrating small wins are part of building confidence and a collaborative session. (669 · U3 · L09 · 04:31–07:05)
- In editing, the first photograph from a space establishes base colors for the rest of that scene; the teacher may still adjust them later. (669 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–03:49)
- A little grain can add texture; too much is undesirable to the teacher. (669 · U4 · L10 · 03:49–05:57)
- Similar photographs can show a gesture developing and contribute a story when viewed together. (669 · U4 · L10 · 05:57–08:53)
- In selection, differences in expression, position, eye definition, clarity of smoke, and small details can distinguish near-identical frames; the preferred result is often an intuitive choice. (669 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–03:14; 669 · U4 · L11 · 03:14–06:44; 669 · U4 · L11 · 08:22–12:37)
- An image may be more convincing when a gesture is still developing, because the scene feels more natural to the teacher. (669 · U4 · L11 · 06:44–08:22; 669 · U4 · L11 · 08:22–12:37)
- Contacting potential clients, venues, or publishers is part of getting work seen; a rejection does not by itself prove the work is bad. (669 · U4 · L12 · 00:41–02:21)
- The teacher describes success as consistency combined with luck, and recommends continuing to work so one is ready when an opportunity arrives. (669 · U4 · L12 · 02:21–03:35)

## Checks the teacher uses

“Rendered-artifact measure” describes a possible check on the finished image; the course does not give numerical thresholds for these checks.

- **Desired framing and performance:** She checks whether the frame and model’s performance produce the image she imagined; if unconvinced, she changes the pose or returns to another position. **Needs judgement by eye.** (669 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–02:45)
- **Eyes within shutter light:** She checks whether Andrea’s eyes sit in a gap between the light stripes, so the light reaches the eyes. **Could be measured on a render** by checking eye-region overlap with the bright bands; she describes choosing it by looking. (669 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–02:45)
- **Smoke in the setting:** She checks whether the smoke stays visible in the air; wind dispersed it too quickly, so she stopped the idea. **Could be measured on a render** by visible smoke contrast and extent, though the decision also depends on the intended look. (669 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–02:45)
- **Reflection and focus plane through glass:** She checks that she disappears from the glass while the park remains visible, and chooses which plane should be in focus. **Could be measured on a render** by reflection visibility and sharpness at the intended plane; she sets focus by eye/manual focus. (669 · U3 · L07 · 03:34–05:05)
- **Light on a balcony:** She rejects the mixed sun-and-shade middle ground as too contrasty and forced, and checks whether backlight is too high/forced at noon. **Needs judgement by eye** for the desired mood and portrait effect. (669 · U3 · L07 · 05:05–07:45)
- **Whether an optical effect helps:** She compares images with and without hosiery and checks whether the blur and framing work; she removes it when unconvinced and tries again after reframing. **Needs judgement by eye.** (669 · U3 · L08 · 00:56–02:51)
- **Room and light arrangement:** She checks if a shot is flat or better defined after moving the sofa and opening a side window to fill the shadows. **Needs judgement by eye.** (669 · U3 · L08 · 02:51–04:01)
- **Image straightness:** She checks that vertical lines remain vertical and horizons do not make the photograph feel as if it is falling. **Could be measured on a render** by line angle and horizon tilt; her stated criterion is visual. (669 · U4 · L10 · 05:57–08:53)
- **Highlight and skin detail:** She checks that lowering whites does not create patches on skin, and balances reducing whites against allowing already-blown areas to remain clipped. **Could be measured on a render** by clipped-pixel area and local highlight transitions on skin; no threshold is specified. (669 · U4 · L10 · 05:57–08:53)
- **Focus:** She notices from a distance when a photo is out of focus and says this complicates editing; she considers editing sharpness or softness rather than using hosiery if that effect is hard to control. **Could be measured on a render** by edge/detail sharpness at the intended focus point; acceptable softness remains a judgement. (669 · U4 · L10 · 12:22–13:56)
- **Color balance and visibility:** She checks whether dominant colors or orange and blue feel too strong, and tries to make rainbow light visible without washing out color. **Needs judgement by eye.** (669 · U4 · L10 · 12:22–13:56; 669 · U4 · L10 · 15:06–17:56)
- **Whether a set of images adds something:** She compares images for gesture, perspective, expression, pose, visible smoke, reflections, and small details such as a hand, shoulder, open door, or position in frame. **Needs judgement by eye.** (669 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–03:14; 669 · U4 · L11 · 03:14–06:44; 669 · U4 · L11 · 08:22–12:37)
- **Distance from editing:** She checks whether the photos surprise her again after a pause, and recommends selection at least a day after editing. **Needs judgement by eye.** (669 · U4 · L11 · 08:22–12:37)
- **Selection count:** In the demonstration, she starts with 216 exported photographs, aims for around 80 or fewer in the first pass, selects 63, and expects the favorites to reduce toward ten over time. **Measurable** by counting selected files; the decision about which files remain is by eye. (669 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–03:14; 669 · U4 · L11 · 12:37–15:44)

## Examples

- **Mario Benedetti, *Antología poética*:** The teacher describes the anthology as her entry into poetry because its words named an emotion she had not yet been able to explain. (669 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–02:10)
- **Pedro Salinas, *La voz a ti debida*:** She associates his poetry with carefully worked metaphors and a more abstract treatment of love, pain, and fear. (669 · U1 · L02 · 02:10–04:02)
- **Chantal Maillard:** The teacher says Maillard showed her that writing from pain can follow thoughts as they come, without requiring coherence. She connects that sensibility to photography. (669 · U1 · L02 · 04:02–05:34)
- **Peter Lindbergh:** His portraits of famous people are an example for showing public figures as human, vulnerable, and fragile; she also values his quiet, gentle manner with subjects. (669 · U1 · L02 · 05:34–07:25)
- **Silvia Graf:** Her photographs remind the teacher of painting because emotion seems to sit on the surface of the image. (669 · U1 · L02 · 07:25–08:19)
- **Alessio Albi:** His work is her strongest aesthetic influence for color and light; she values real-seeming situations with polished color and light that do not feel overly staged. (669 · U1 · L02 · 08:19–09:23)
- **La Pedrera, Barcelona:** The teacher wanted an aristocratic-feeling period interior, obtained permission to photograph there, and framed out tourists so the room appeared empty. (669 · U2 · L05 · 01:39–03:59)
- **Andrea at the shuttered balcony window:** Bands of sunlight across the model led the teacher to place her eyes in a gap between the stripes. (669 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–02:45)
- **Andrea through balcony glass:** The teacher shifted the door angle to remove her own reflection while retaining the park and trees, then selected the focus plane manually. (669 · U3 · L07 · 03:34–05:05)
- **Blue armchair and small palm:** She cleared other furniture, retained the palm for its color, and moved the chair nearer the window for its patches of light. (669 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–00:56)
- **Mustard sofa:** Moving it closer to the window and opening a side window made the light more defined than placing it against the wall. (669 · U3 · L08 · 02:51–04:01)
- **Mirrored tabletop:** A slightly overhead view made Andrea’s reflection read as an upright portrait; the opened frame shows her face and its inverted reflection together. (frame) (669 · U3 · L08 · 04:01–05:56)
- **Rainbow from a glass tabletop:** Sun bounced from the table onto another balcony door, making colored light on Andrea’s face and eye. (669 · U3 · L08 · 05:56–07:00)
- **Golden coaster and CD:** The coaster created a small golden patch on Andrea’s face; covering part of a CD changed the shape of its reflected light. (669 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:43; 669 · U3 · L09 · 01:43–03:15)
- **Plastic from a croissant bag:** A cut-out hole over the lens helped define the area of the image while side light produced flare; the opened example shows a soft, hazy portrait. (frame) (669 · U3 · L09 · 03:15–04:31)
- **Lilac dried flowers:** Held a few fingers in front of the lens, small flowers tinted dark tones; reopening the window restored depth after the image looked flat. (669 · U3 · L09 · 04:31–07:05)
- **Smoke at the shuttered window:** The teacher chose it to see how smoke behaved in the light; its changing form could not be controlled, and she valued discovering the resulting image during editing. (669 · U4 · L10 · 05:57–08:53)
- **Balcony portraits:** She tried color and black-and-white, but some frames did not convey the intimate atmosphere she wanted because of hard light and visible surroundings. (669 · U4 · L10 · 10:59–12:22)
- **Portrait of Andrea on the orange sofa:** The dominant sofa color was a reason to test how a small color adjustment changed the overall photograph. (669 · U4 · L10 · 15:06–17:56)
- **Photographing a favorite band:** The teacher offered to photograph the band for free, contacted them directly, and later became their photographer for several months. (669 · U4 · L12 · 02:21–03:35)

## Tasks

(Unit tasks: `references/course-669/U02-unit.md`, anchor `669 · U2`)

## Tool-specific steps

- **Lightroom — import and prepare:** Import the session photographs; the teacher says she usually has her own filters/presets available. (669 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–03:49)
- **Lightroom — tone and color:** Lower highlights on a blown background, adjust blacks, use a three-point tone curve to veil darker tones and raise blacks for texture, and lower whites to recover visible information. (669 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–03:49)
- **Lightroom — HSL and color grading:** Reduce yellow saturation and shift yellow slightly toward magenta if it looks yellow-green; darken oranges for skin texture and sometimes reduce their saturation; adjust a prominent color such as blue; then work with highlight and shadow color and revisit reds. (669 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–03:49)
- **Lightroom — settings visible in figures:** One green-channel panel shows Hue −61, Saturation −39, Luminance 0; one grain panel shows Amount 14, Size 25, Roughness 50. These are example settings visible in the frames, not stated universal values. (frame) (669 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–03:49; 669 · U4 · L10 · 03:49–05:57)
- **Lightroom — carry edits through a scene:** Copy an edit with Command-C, paste it onto other photographs from the same space, then adapt each image. The teacher adds a little grain. (669 · U4 · L10 · 03:49–05:57)
- **Lightroom — crop, straighten, and retain detail:** Keep vertical lines vertical and horizons level; check that lowering whites does not create skin patches. (669 · U4 · L10 · 05:57–08:53)
- **Lightroom — alternate treatments and focus:** Try black-and-white for difficult balcony light; use sharpness/softness adjustments when the image needs them, rather than relying on hosiery if it produces an uncontrolled result. (669 · U4 · L10 · 10:59–12:22; 669 · U4 · L10 · 12:22–13:56)
- **Lightroom — adapt the look:** Shift color relationships when a sofa dominates; try reversing/rotating a table reflection; make rainbow light visible while preserving color. The lesson ends by exporting the photographs for selection. (669 · U4 · L10 · 13:56–15:06; 669 · U4 · L10 · 15:06–17:56)
