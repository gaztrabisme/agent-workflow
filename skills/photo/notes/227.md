## Method steps

1. **Decide the account's character and message.** Study accounts whose work you like, say why, and look for a coherent style across posts; then define the values the project should express before photographing. Existing branding should inform the photographic style; if there is no branding, decide colors, typefaces, and overall style. [227 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–00:56] [227 · U3 · L06 · 00:00–01:19] [227 · U3 · L06 · 01:19–03:19]
2. **Make a visual direction and prop list.** Gather reference images into a moodboard, choose a palette that expresses the values, and list materials and props that support the project. For an established brand, account for packaging that may not match its colors and choose supporting neutrals. [227 · U3 · L06 · 01:19–03:19] (The moodboard exercise gives a four-part practice sequence: values, palette, references, assembled board. [U3 Tasks, Practice exercise 3.1])
3. **Prepare the phone and simple set materials.** Use the phone's availability and speed; use abundant natural light, a white foam board as reflector, colored card as background, white Blu Tack to secure objects, reusable washi tape to lift or hold them, and project-appropriate props. [227 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:55] [227 · U2 · L03 · 02:18–03:01] [227 · U2 · L03 · 03:01–04:18] [227 · U2 · L03 · 04:18–05:35]
4. **Choose the place and light for the intended shoot.** At home, find the brightest room for the time of day and use a window; in a place you cannot control, move close to a window. Outdoors, prefer low sun or shade for softer light; choose hard light when its contrast and shadows suit the image. [227 · U2 · L04 · 05:17–06:50] [227 · U2 · L04 · 06:50–08:07] [227 · U2 · L04 · 08:07–09:31] [227 · U2 · L04 · 09:31–11:20] [227 · U2 · L04 · 11:20–12:14] [227 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–01:25] [227 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–00:57]
5. **Choose the format and frame before arranging.** Select vertical, horizontal, or square based on the intended image and destination. For a constructed scene, frame through the phone first, then arrange only what the frame will show; use thirds, a centered symmetry, or negative space according to the subject. [227 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:15] [227 · U2 · L05 · 01:15–02:27] [227 · U2 · L05 · 02:27–04:10] [227 · U3 · L07 · 01:25–02:25]
6. **Build the composition around the project.** Put larger objects in first and consider where their shadows will fall; arrange smaller props to support the message and the brand, check the frame as you go, and secure objects that roll, bend, or sag. For a flat lay, arrange against the selected color field on an imagined grid and leave room for the photograph to breathe. [227 · U3 · L07 · 02:25–04:33] [227 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–01:16] [227 · U3 · L08 · 01:16–02:25] [227 · U3 · L08 · 02:25–03:32]
7. **Balance light and capture.** Turn off set lights when possible; use a reflector opposite the window to soften shadows. Clean the lens, tap the subject to focus and expose, adjust exposure without blowing a region white, steady the phone, and take several angles or viewpoints to compare. [227 · U3 · L07 · 04:33–05:41] [227 · U3 · L07 · 05:41–06:28] [227 · U3 · L07 · 06:28–08:32] [227 · U3 · L08 · 03:32–04:07] [227 · U3 · L08 · 04:07–05:19] [227 · U2 · L04 · 03:07–05:17]
8. **For unfamiliar interiors, work from wide views to details.** Look for natural light, photograph the whole space and broad areas before details, straighten verticals as much as possible, move disliked or useful objects if appropriate, expose for the interior when the view outside is unwanted, and vary viewpoints of important subjects. [227 · U3 · L09 · 00:57–01:51] [227 · U3 · L09 · 01:51–03:29]
9. **Select the strongest image of each kind before editing.** Review the photographs, mark favorites, and edit the selected image from each type of shot. [227 · U4 · L11 · 00:08–01:38]
10. **Develop and finish the image in a deliberate order.** The demonstrated general order is geometry first, then overall light/contrast/color, white balance, local corrections if needed, removal of distractions if needed, and crop/export. The teacher leaves cropping late so the crop can hide perspective-fill areas and remain adjustable. [227 · U4 · L11 · 01:38–04:12] [227 · U4 · L11 · 04:12–06:57] [227 · U4 · L11 · 06:57–10:04] [227 · U4 · L11 · 10:04–11:36] [227 · U4 · L11 · 11:36–14:31] [227 · U4 · L11 · 14:31–16:48]
11. **Judge edits against the image and intended look.** Compare before and after, try controls in both directions, keep texture/sharpening/filter effects subtle, and use a filter only when it suits the photograph. Edit similar frames individually unless their lighting, background, and conditions match; then copied adjustments can save time. [227 · U4 · L11 · 04:12–06:57] [227 · U4 · L11 · 06:57–10:04] [227 · U4 · L12 · 04:37–08:37] [227 · U4 · L12 · 13:10–16:31] [227 · U4 · L12 · 18:15–20:06]
12. **Prepare a batch and review it as a gallery.** Set aside a block of time to shoot and edit multiple posts, draft captions, schedule or queue them, and use a preview to move similar subjects, colors, tones, or levels of visual busyness apart when that suits the account. Stay available to answer comments and messages when posts go live. [227 · U4 · L14 · 00:08–01:47] [227 · U4 · L14 · 07:09–09:44] [227 · U4 · L14 · 09:44–11:26]
13. **For Stories, work from the message and vertical frame.** Keep important content inside the 9:16 crop, favor a clear useful message, vary photo, video, and text screens, and use a larger set to make several framings. Stories can be quicker and less polished than feed photographs. ## Principles and claims

- A coherent visual style across a profile can help visitors stay on it. [227 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–00:56]
- The phone is portable, quick, and close to editing and posting; a dedicated camera makes larger, higher-quality files and offers more manual and equipment options. [227 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:55]
- Phone images tend to keep foreground and background in focus, so the background needs as much attention as the subject. [227 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:55]
- A phone's wide-angle view includes more of a scene and can distort nearby objects; stepping back matters especially for portraits. [227 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:55]
- Abundant natural light improves phone image quality; very low light can produce grainy, yellow-looking photographs. [227 · U2 · L03 · 01:55–02:18]
- White foam board can act as a simple reflector; use white rather than blue putty because blue putty can stain. [227 · U2 · L03 · 02:18–03:01] [227 · U2 · L03 · 03:01–04:18]
- Props should reinforce the values and uses the brand wants to communicate. [227 · U2 · L03 · 04:18–05:35]
- A higher-quality original makes later editing easier; enable the largest available image and RAW when the phone supports it. [227 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:27]
- The teacher advises turning off the built-in flash for Instagram because light from beside the camera flattens the photograph and removes interesting light. [227 · U2 · L04 · 01:27–03:07]
- Phone zoom generally enlarges rather than optically bringing the subject closer; cropping later preserves more quality in the original. [227 · U2 · L04 · 01:27–03:07]
- Dirty lenses can create flares, soften the image, and reduce sharpness; a steady phone and gentle shutter press help prevent motion blur. [227 · U2 · L04 · 03:07–05:17]
- Soft natural light is easier for the phone to capture across bright and dark areas; shade, cloud, low sun, and diffusion can soften light. [227 · U2 · L04 · 05:17–06:50] [227 · U2 · L04 · 06:50–08:07]
- Hard light can be used deliberately: shadows can form the subject, a diagonal, or divisions between light and shade. [227 · U2 · L04 · 08:07–09:31]
- In a large scene with sky, expose for the sky because a white, blown-out sky cannot be recovered; a dark foreground may be brightened later. [227 · U2 · L04 · 08:07–09:31]
- Indoor lamps can add a yellow cast in shadows; switch them off where possible, open windows and coverings, and map the brightest rooms at different times. [227 · U2 · L04 · 09:31–11:20]
- In a café, museum, or other place where lights cannot be controlled, getting close to a window makes natural light stronger relative to artificial light. [227 · U2 · L04 · 11:20–12:14]
- Vertical, horizontal, and square formats are all usable on Instagram; horizontal images occupy less of a phone screen, while vertical images can fill most of it. [227 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:15]
- The rule of thirds applies across formats; the four intersections are attention points for important subjects. [227 · U2 · L05 · 01:15–02:27]
- A horizon or table edge is less central when it sits on a third rather than dividing the frame at its center. [227 · U2 · L05 · 02:27–04:10]
- Practising the rule of thirds can help develop an eye for composition before breaking the rule. [227 · U2 · L05 · 04:10–05:15]
- Total symmetry can suit a subject that is itself symmetrical; center the axis and balance the elements on both sides. [227 · U2 · L05 · 04:10–05:15]
- An odd count of objects often works better for small groups than an even count. [227 · U2 · L05 · 05:15–06:59]
- Negative space can make a photograph more interesting, give a subject room, and leave space for later design or text. [227 · U2 · L05 · 05:15–06:59]
- An orderly arrangement can make differently sized objects fit together; apparent disorder can work when it is balanced, for example with even spacing. [227 · U2 · L05 · 06:59–08:25]
- Brand values guide decisions in making a photograph; photography should connect to the visual identity already in place. [227 · U3 · L06 · 00:00–01:19] [227 · U3 · L06 · 01:19–03:19]
- A palette can combine identifying brand colors with grays or neutrals that coordinate with varied packaging. [227 · U3 · L06 · 01:19–03:19]
- For overhead work, lean farther over the scene to view it more directly from above and reduce perspective distortion. [227 · U3 · L08 · 04:07–05:19]
- For interior photos, straight verticals reduce the difficulty of later editing. [227 · U3 · L09 · 00:57–01:51]
- Stories disappear after 24 hours, which the teacher says gives more freedom for quick everyday material; a clear message should offer something useful. [227 · U5 · L15 · 00:00–02:07]
- Stories are narrower than some phones' native camera frames; important content should not sit in side regions that the 9:16 crop removes. [227 · U5 · L15 · 02:07–03:20]
- A Story sequence is more dynamic when it varies the screen content instead of repeating similar photos or text-over-background screens. [227 · U5 · L15 · 04:52–05:59]
- (frame) The VSCO filter comparison screen presents four versions of the same tabletop photograph (A7–A10), making differences in color and tone visible side by side. [227 · U4 · L12 · 04:37–08:37]

## Checks the teacher uses

- **Capture setup:** confirm maximum image size and RAW toggle in camera settings; this is a device-setting check, not something a rendered image alone establishes. [227 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:27]
- **Screen and flash:** raise screen brightness and set flash off before shooting; the final image can be inspected for flat, frontal-looking light, but cannot reliably prove the setting used. [227 · U2 · L04 · 01:27–03:07]
- **Lens and steadiness:** clean the lens and check the camera area; inspect the rendered image for flare, softness, or motion blur. Lens cleanliness itself is a physical check. [227 · U2 · L04 · 03:07–05:17]
- **Exposure:** tap the subject and raise exposure only a little; do not let a part of the preview turn white. A rendered file could be checked for clipped white pixels/highlights, while the teacher makes the decision by watching the preview. [227 · U3 · L07 · 05:41–06:28]
- **Bright sky:** inspect whether sky detail remains; clipping in the rendered sky could be measured, though the teacher describes this as an exposure decision. [227 · U2 · L04 · 08:07–09:31]
- **Soft light:** compare cast-shadow darkness and hardness, and see whether a reflector makes shadows softer; the teacher judges the visual result. [227 · U3 · L07 · 04:33–05:41] [227 · U3 · L07 · 05:41–06:28] [227 · U3 · L07 · 06:28–08:32]
- **Composition:** use the camera grid to place key subjects at thirds or check symmetry; placement and axis alignment can be measured on a rendered frame, but choosing a pleasing arrangement is by eye. [227 · U2 · L05 · 01:15–02:27] [227 · U2 · L05 · 04:10–05:15]
- **Negative space and arrangement:** check the amount and location of open space, and whether props sit orderly or in balanced disorder; geometry and occupied area can be measured, while balance is judged by eye. [227 · U2 · L05 · 05:15–06:59] [227 · U2 · L05 · 06:59–08:25] [227 · U3 · L08 · 01:16–02:25]
- **Framing during set building:** repeatedly compare the phone's live frame to the prop arrangement so no work is spent on areas outside the picture. The crop boundary is measurable; the composition is judged by eye. [227 · U3 · L07 · 01:25–02:25]
- **Perspective:** use a grid and architectural lines near the center; level horizontals and verticals can be measured from the rendered image. [227 · U4 · L11 · 01:38–04:12]
- **Interior capture:** check verticals, work from general view to details, and compare viewpoints; line angles and viewpoint coverage can be measured, while which views matter is judgement. [227 · U3 · L09 · 00:57–01:51] [227 · U3 · L09 · 01:51–03:29]
- **White balance:** sample a neutral wall, floor, or black/white/gray object, then judge whether the material returns to its real color; neutral pixel values can be measured, but the correct reference and desired look require judgement. [227 · U4 · L11 · 10:04–11:36]
- **Tone and color edits:** hold to compare before/after; decide whether brightness, contrast, saturation, highlights, and shadows improve this image. Before/after pixel values are measurable; whether the change suits the image is judged by eye. [227 · U4 · L11 · 04:12–06:57] [227 · U4 · L11 · 06:57–10:04]
- **Local corrections:** check that a selective adjustment affects the intended wall without crossing onto a differently colored window; mask coverage can be measured, but the boundary and subtlety are judged visually. [227 · U4 · L11 · 11:36–14:31]
- **Healing:** inspect the removed lamp or plug area for a convincing fill and absence of a distracting object; artifact visibility is a visual judgement. [227 · U4 · L11 · 14:31–16:48] [227 · U4 · L11 · 16:48–18:06]
- **Amount of processing:** inspect sharpening, structure/clarity, grain, and filter intensity for an obvious or exaggerated effect; rendered texture and grain can be measured, but acceptability is judgement by eye. [227 · U4 · L11 · 06:57–10:04] [227 · U4 · L12 · 04:37–08:37] [227 · U4 · L12 · 13:10–16:31]
- **Feed preview:** look for similar subjects, dark/warm images, or busy images grouped too close together; counts and distances can be measured in a grid, while the teacher's preferred rhythm is by eye. [227 · U4 · L14 · 07:09–09:44]
- **Story crop:** compare the phone's native frame with 9:16 and keep important objects out of the side strips; their positions relative to the crop are measurable. [227 · U5 · L15 · 02:07–03:20]
- **Story text and overlays:** give spoken video context for viewers watching without sound; choose placement, color, highlight, and typeface so the text communicates clearly on the photo. Contrast and position can be measured, while readability and style are judged by eye. [227 · U5 · L16 · 06:29–10:11]

## Tool-specific steps

### Phone camera and physical setup

- Android: in camera settings, choose the largest image size and enable RAW saving if offered. The lesson's iPhone example uses Settings → Camera → Formats → High Efficiency. [227 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:27]
- Before capture, raise screen brightness, turn flash off, avoid in-camera zoom, clean the lens, hold the phone steadily, tap the shutter gently, and use a timer plus a stable object if the phone must remain still. [227 · U2 · L04 · 01:27–03:07] [227 · U2 · L04 · 03:07–05:17]
- For an iPhone exposure adjustment, tap the subject then move the exposure control up or down; the Android demonstration uses the plus/minus exposure slider. [227 · U3 · L07 · 05:41–06:28] [227 · U3 · L08 · 04:07–05:19]
- To soften window shadows, stand foam board opposite the light and support it with a stool, chair, book, or box. [227 · U3 · L07 · 06:28–08:32]

### Snapseed

- Import a chosen favorite. Begin in Tools → Perspective: rotate/tilt to level lines, then use Free and pull corners until central architectural lines align with the grid. Use white or black fill rather than smart fill if the invented edges are poor; crop those edges later. [227 · U4 · L11 · 00:08–01:38] [227 · U4 · L11 · 01:38–04:12]
- Develop with Curves or Tune Image. In Curves, raising the curve lightens and an S shape increases contrast; in Tune Image, swipe vertically to choose a setting and horizontally to change its value. [227 · U4 · L11 · 04:12–06:57]
- Tune Image controls shown include brightness, contrast, saturation, ambiance, highlights, shadows, and warmth; the teacher leaves warmth for White Balance and advises restraint with Ambiance. Details contains Structure and Sharpening, also used sparingly. [227 · U4 · L11 · 06:57–10:04]
- White Balance adjusts Temperature (yellow/blue) and Tint (green/magenta); the eyedropper can sample a neutral gray floor or another neutral object. [227 · U4 · L11 · 10:04–11:36]
- Use Brush for painted local exposure/temperature/saturation, or Selective for a soft point adjustment that can respect differences in color/tone. [227 · U4 · L11 · 11:36–14:31]
- Healing removes a lamp or plug by painting over the distraction. Crop after edits, choose the required ratio, keep perspective-fill regions outside the crop, then Export to save to the camera roll. [227 · U4 · L11 · 14:31–16:48] [227 · U4 · L11 · 16:48–18:06]
- The Snapseed lesson calls its Story crop “16:9”; U5 L15 describes the vertical Story frame as “9:16.” See Derived for this source inconsistency. [227 · U4 · L11 · 14:31–16:48] [227 · U5 · L15 · 02:07–03:20]

### VSCO

- Import one or several images into the private Studio drafts. Compare filters, inspect the tones at full preview size, compare before/after, and reduce filter strength rather than applying a full-strength look automatically. [227 · U4 · L12 · 03:14–04:37] [227 · U4 · L12 · 04:37–08:37]
- Under Tools, rearrange controls to put frequently used tools first. Start with Adjust for perspective and crop; use the grid, then crop to a format such as 4:5 for Instagram. [227 · U4 · L12 · 08:37–11:32]
- Try exposure in both directions, then consider contrast, white balance temperature/tint, saturation, Define, Clarity, Tone, Skin Tone, Vignette, Grain, Fade, and Split Tone. The teacher warns against excessive clarity in portraits, usually avoids Vignette and Split Tone, and recommends waiting for Grain to render before judging it. [227 · U4 · L12 · 11:32–13:10] [227 · U4 · L12 · 13:10–16:31] [227 · U4 · L12 · 16:31–18:15]
- Save as a draft or to both Studio and the camera roll. Copy Edits and Paste Edits can speed work on photographs with the same set, light, background, and conditions; review and adjust each result. [227 · U4 · L12 · 16:31–18:15] [227 · U4 · L12 · 18:15–20:06]
