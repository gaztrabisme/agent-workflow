## Choosing a visualization

Anchor: 3762 · U2 · L04 · 00:08–00:30

This lesson is about data and how to visualize it. Choosing the right visualization for our data is crucial to the final project. We will look at different kinds of data, from numerical data to more complex information connected with maps and geography.

## Beginning with numerical data

Anchor: 3762 · U2 · L04 · 00:30–00:52

For numerical data, we begin with the Cartesian plane (*piano cartesiano*). It is fundamental for displaying numerical data: it starts at an origin and extends along an X-axis and a Y-axis. Data can be positive or negative, and we can also move forward and backward in time.

## Making the chart part of an identity

Anchor: 3762 · U2 · L04 · 01:34–02:37

This links the data to the typography: the less data there is, the larger the typography becomes. The same idea runs through the festival's visual identity.

## Comparing proportional areas

Anchor: 3762 · U2 · L04 · 03:06–03:34

Proportional areas (*aree proporzionali*) are another way to show data. They help represent large quantities and differences, and offer an alternative to a simple histogram. In this example, proportional areas represent public spending. They make it immediately clear which value is larger and which is smaller.

## Pie charts and treemaps

Anchor: 3762 · U2 · L04 · 03:34–04:20

Another visualization is the pie chart (*grafico a torta*). Unlike a histogram, which can show an unlimited series of data, a pie chart has a finite number of values. It represents 100 percent of the whole, with each slice showing a category's share.

From pie charts we move to a treemap (*trimap* in the Italian captions). Like a pie chart, a treemap divides a finite space and shows the whole as distinct proportional parts.

## Bubbles and other proportional shapes

Anchor: 3762 · U2 · L04 · 04:20–05:03

Another important visualization uses proportional areas, often called a bubble chart. Bubbles are useful for highlighting large differences between values and making a web page more lively and dynamic. These areas do not have to be bubbles or circles: the same type of data can be represented with squares while preserving proportionality and visual clarity. Proportional areas make it easy to see how much larger one value is than another.

## Mapping democracy

Anchor: 3762 · U2 · L04 · 06:14–06:53

Data can also be shown on maps to give a geographic view. Here we look at a global heat map (*mappa di calore*) about democracy. A greater number of colored dots indicates a more intense value, and color shows the level of democracy in each country. Pink marks countries where democracy is at risk or absent, while blue marks democratic countries. A heat map gives a quick, clear view of geographic data and makes it easier to analyze and understand.

## Speculative maps

Anchor: 3762 · U2 · L04 · 06:53–07:48

This last example is a speculative map (*mappa speculativa*): it is not a real map and does not represent an actual geographic area. It represents internet giants. The data is numerical but hidden in the map. Internet companies are drawn as states: their size reflects the number of users and their borders show their earnings. This visual form helps make their influence and economic power understandable. Where a border is narrow, profits are lower; where it is wide, profits are higher. Amazon is the striking case: it is smaller than the others, but its borders are entirely purple, showing that it earns more than the others. That concludes the data-visualization lesson; the next lesson is about semiotics.
