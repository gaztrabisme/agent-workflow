## Gather and organize the research

Anchor: 3762 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–00:56

After finishing the visual research, gather the ideas and assess which ones matter for the final project. Keep the ideas that made an impression and set aside those that are unnecessary or irrelevant. A mood board brings together the images, artists, and works we have seen, as well as shapes, words, and colors, so we can see how they interact.

A mood board is personal. It can be physical or digital, such as a Pinterest board; for this project, we used an iPad. The important thing is to collect the necessary information in one place so that we can see the whole picture.

## Move from figurative images toward abstraction

Anchor: 3762 · U3 · L07 · 00:56–02:43

This part of the mood board explores the transition from a figurative idea (*figurativo*) toward a more abstract one. The first step was to study dance movements described by Kandinsky in the book *Point, Line, and Surface* (*Punto, Linea e Superficie*). Kandinsky moves from figurative art and dance toward an abstract intuition. This is an early step toward our work on cocktails.

We then chose painters and artists such as Paul Klee and Josef Albers, who reimagine a landscape using only basic geometric shapes. This belongs to the Bauhaus way of thinking, a conceptual revolution that changed art from figurative images to abstract ones. We wanted to understand that our cocktails would not be represented in a traditional way. Instead, we would examine behavior and movement to tell their story in a new way, moving toward almost complete abstraction. In other words, the progression is from a figurative concept to pure abstraction.

## Proportion and relationships between forms

Anchor: 3762 · U3 · L07 · 03:25–04:20

As the research continued, we found artists such as Ruprecht Geiger, whose works are based on proportions (*proporzioni*). Here, proportion suggests a relationship. Working with data means handling shapes of different sizes, and the relationships between them are essential for creating harmony and coherence in both artistic and scientific contexts.

## Use transparency as another visual reference

Anchor: 3762 · U3 · L07 · 04:20–05:00

Other images relate to the idea of relationships. Works by Ruprecht Geiger, like those by Karel Martens, also explore transparency. For that concept, we selected images by Olafur Eliasson. He uses mirrors, glass, and color to explore how the human eye, visual perception, and color interact, making works that challenge our understanding. The previously seen images by Ruprecht Geiger also connect to transparency.

## Connect behavior and color to data

Anchor: 3762 · U3 · L07 · 05:00–06:46

The final section of the mood board focuses on behavior and the different uses of color. We looked at Josef Albers's work on the behavior of shapes and the interaction of colors to understand these effects. We also connect Albers's work to Geiger's and Karel Martens's work seen earlier.

We have looked at a mind map of the whole visual part, but it is important to remember that we are working with data. To do this, we will connect the last two images on the mood board, bringing together a real infographic with the data we collected. One example is Andrew Kuo, who creates artistic infographics. The works of Ellsworth Kelly (captions: *Elzor Kelly*) also tell stories and look like visual, artistic infographics.

This is our mood board. Some things are not connected, but that does not mean they will not be useful for the final project. With the mood board finished, we can begin sketching in the next lesson.

### Key points (derived)

- A mood board gathers the research in one place and helps reveal useful connections among images, shape, and color.
- The project moves from figurative references toward abstraction while keeping proportion, transparency, behavior, color, and data in view.
