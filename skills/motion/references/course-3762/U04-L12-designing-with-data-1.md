## Give each ingredient a visual form

Anchor: 3762 · U4 · L12 · 00:54–03:03

The first step is to establish a visual language with shapes for the selected cocktail components. Each cocktail has an alcoholic base, usually a distilled spirit (*distillato*), an aroma (*aroma*), a colorant (*colorante*), and a decoration. Colorants are often nonalcoholic drinks, syrups, or soft drinks. Decorations can be fruit, salt, or other elements. We need a specific shape for each component because every cocktail contains a base spirit, a flavoring component, a colorant, and a particular decoration.

We chose a rectangle for the alcoholic base, such as rum, tequila, whisky, or gin, because it has a stable structure and appears consistently in the cocktails. Aromas are liqueurs (*liquori*). They also contain alcohol, but their alcohol percentage varies. We use the same rectangular shape as for spirits because both share an alcoholic base and a similar structure. To distinguish liqueurs by their varying alcohol content, we round their corners in proportion to that content. A liqueur with more alcohol has corners that are less rounded, so it looks closer to a spirit. With less alcohol, the corners are much more rounded, making the shape softer and moving it farther from the spirit form. Colorants use squares, while decorations use a detailed pattern (*pattern*).

## Encode the Americano, Boulevardier, and Negroni

Anchor: 3762 · U4 · L12 · 03:03–05:43

For example, the Americano contains 30 ml of Campari bitter, 30 ml of Sweet Red Vermouth, and a splash of tonic water. It is garnished with a lemon slice and an orange slice. The drink has two equal parts of liqueur: Campari is magenta and vermouth is red. A small amount of tonic water completes the mix. In this scheme, colorants are always black, so the tonic water is a black square.

Campari bitter and Sweet Red Vermouth are liqueurs rather than base spirits. The Americano therefore uses two containers with rounded corners. Campari has 25% alcohol, so its corners are only slightly rounded. Vermouth has 15% alcohol, so its corners are more rounded. Later we will enter the data and make the rounding precise and proportional to the alcohol percentages.

Next, the Boulevardier shares the Americano's structure but adds 45 ml of bourbon or rye whisky, which we color green. It does not have tonic water as the Americano does, so its design has no dark square for tonic. We can use the same method to recreate the compositions of the other cocktails and set the palette.

The Negroni has a composition similar to the Boulevardier, but it uses gin instead of bourbon. We chose lilac for the gin. Having shown the method with these first three cocktails, we can apply it to the rest.

For example, the Dry Martini contains 60 ml of gin and 10 ml of dry vermouth, so it still has the Martini element. In the White Lady, dry vermouth is replaced by triple sec, which we color purple to keep the palette harmonious. Triple sec is also in the next cocktail, the Margarita, where tequila replaces gin.

The Tequila Sunrise has tequila as its only alcoholic ingredient, accompanied by orange juice and grenadine syrup. It is known for its simplicity and colorful appearance, which recalls a sunrise. Orange juice is the shared ingredient with the Mimosa. The Mimosa introduces chilled Prosecco at the center of the menu; we gave that Prosecco a golden tone to make it stand out as the central element. Finally, the Spritz combines Prosecco and Campari, creating a loop back to the first cocktail, the Americano, because they share Campari.

## Create a column chart from the data

Anchor: 3762 · U4 · L12 · 07:22–08:41

Now we will use Illustrator's graph tools to generate the data visualizations. Choose the column chart (*grafico a colonne*) from the toolbar and drag it onto the Illustrator artboard. A window appears where you can enter the data. This tool generates histograms. With the Americano, enter 30 ml of Campari bitter and 30 ml of vermouth; Illustrator creates two columns. If we continue with the Boulevardier and add 45 ml of whisky, Illustrator creates a third column. The Campari and vermouth columns are the same height because each is 30 ml, while the 45 ml whisky column is taller. If we add gin for the Dry Martini, Illustrator creates a new column, and the gin column is the tallest because it represents 60 ml.

## Compare ingredient stacks and customize the graphs

Anchor: 3762 · U4 · L12 · 08:41–11:07

For this comparison, however, we need a different tool that also generates histograms, but creates stacked columns. The process is the same. Enter the Americano first with 30 ml of Campari bitter and 30 ml of vermouth. Illustrator creates two stacked columns, giving a direct comparison of the cocktail's two main ingredients.

To make the Boulevardier chart, add a new row with 30 ml of Campari bitter, 30 ml of vermouth, and 45 ml of whisky. Illustrator now shows two separate columns, one for the Americano and one for the Boulevardier, so we can compare their ingredients. We can also add the Negroni, which has three equal parts: gin, Campari, and vermouth. Enter 30 ml of gin, 30 ml of Campari, and 30 ml of vermouth.

To use the data, we need to separate the graph. Illustrator asks whether we want to proceed, and we confirm. Then we can customize the individual parts by changing their colors or rounding their corners. Before editing the cocktail histograms, it is a good idea to duplicate the base histogram so we keep an original version. Illustrator lets us continue editing the data from Object > Graph > Data. The White Lady contains 40 ml of gin, 30 ml of triple sec, and 20 ml of lemon juice. Once we understand the system, we can enter all the data in the menu and click once to carry out all the necessary operations.

### Key points (derived)

- The visual system maps spirits to rectangles, liqueurs to rounded rectangles, colorants to squares, and decorations to patterned outlines.
- The nine cocktails share ingredients that connect their compositions, including Campari, triple sec, tequila, orange juice, and Prosecco.
- Separate columns show ingredient amounts; stacked columns compare the ingredient makeup of each cocktail.
