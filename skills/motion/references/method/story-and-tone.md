# Story and tone: shape the message into a narrative

## Reader and scope

**Reader:** a planner turning supplied content into a story for animation. **Scope:** define premise, tone and event order before committing to detailed production technique.

## First idea, stated as the rule

- Separate what is being told from how it will be told. The course's story X-ray names premise, tone and structure as the links between input and output. (433 · U3 · L05 · 00:50–01:41; 433 · U3 · L05 · 01:41–02:24)
- A premise joins form, such as figurative or abstract, with function, such as explanatory, promotional or informative. It should make the subject clear while leaving room for visual discovery. (433 · U3 · L06 · 00:49–01:37; 433 · U3 · L06 · 02:14–03:08; 433 · U3 · L06 · 07:51–08:34)
- Break down the script or voice-over, gather loose ideas, remove or combine weak ones, group the remaining ideas, compare routes, then state a premise. The course uses Post-its as one easy-to-rearrange prototype. (433 · U3 · L06 · 03:08–03:50; 433 · U3 · L06 · 03:50–04:58; 433 · U3 · L06 · 04:58–05:19; 433 · U3 · L06 · 05:19–06:48; 433 · U3 · L06 · 06:48–07:51; 433 · U3 · L06 · 07:51–08:34)
- Tone gives the story its narrative personality; make the intended tone clear enough that the viewer does not misread it. (433 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–00:41; 433 · U3 · L07 · 02:08–02:55; 433 · U3 · L07 · 02:55–03:42)
- A character may be a person, shape or typography; define its narrative role, visual distinction, personality and behavior rules in service of the story. (433 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–00:49; 433 · U4 · L10 · 00:49–01:45; 433 · U4 · L10 · 01:45–02:28)
- Structure orders events and their importance. A chronological three-act shape and non-linear forms, including circular return, are options; the message still needs to reach the receiver. (433 · U3 · L08 · 00:08–01:08; 433 · U3 · L08 · 01:08–02:54; 433 · U3 · L08 · 02:54–03:31; 433 · U3 · L08 · 03:31–05:03; 433 · U3 · L08 · 05:43–06:50)
- A visual analogy can make a difficult concept familiar to its audience; the teacher uses a public pool to make population scale graspable and a chicken to contextualize velociraptor size. (433 · U4 · L12 · 01:14–01:56; 433 · U4 · L12 · 02:57–03:58)

## A rule you derived (derived)

Write a one-sentence story spine that names subject, intended audience effect and the visual premise, while leaving technique open. This joins the planning objective, the form/function premise and the requirement that structure preserve receiver understanding. (derived from 433 · U2 · L04 · 02:46–04:12; 433 · U3 · L06 · 00:49–01:37; 433 · U3 · L08 · 05:43–06:50)

## Failures

**Problem 1: the premise locks the production too early.**
- **The failure:** the premise specifies a technique or exact graphic style before the story is shaped, which closes options the course leaves open. (433 · U3 · L06 · 02:14–03:08; 433 · U3 · L06 · 07:51–08:34)
- **The fix:** keep the premise clear about input, function and form; leave technique and detailed style for later.

**Problem 2: structure becomes a puzzle without a message.**
- **The failure:** events are surprising but the receiver cannot understand what they add up to. (433 · U3 · L08 · 05:43–06:50)
- **The fix:** restate the intended message, then reorder or remove events until the structure supports it.

## Check before moving on

1. **T2 — Premise and tone:** pass when a reader can state the subject, function and intended tone without mistaking a visual technique for the premise.
2. **T3 — Structure:** pass when the chosen event order gives the receiver a readable start, development and resolution, whether chronological or non-linear.

**Practice the teacher sets:** state a clear premise, tone and structure from the provided voice-over. See [Unit 3](../course-433/U03-unit.md).
