# Storyboard and animatic: make sequence and timing visible

## Reader and scope

**Reader:** a planner turning a story into a timed sequence. **Scope:** prototype panels, framing, continuity, transitions and sound before detailed choreography.

## First idea, stated as the rule

- Use the storyboard as the first major tool for visualizing a story, treating it as a rapid-prototyping tool rather than only as an illustrated visualization. (433 · U4 · L09 · 00:00–00:48)
- Keep the storyboard modular and provisional; no element is guaranteed a fixed place as the prototype develops. (433 · U4 · L09 · 02:58–03:45)
- The point is the ideas and testing how they function in the story, not polished drawing; use only as much detail as needed to explain the story. (433 · U4 · L09 · 02:02–02:58; 433 · U4 · L09 · 03:45–04:19)
- Use small squares to think through the story and how to present the information; include what you are thinking of doing to animate each frame. (561 · U2 · L05 · 00:02–00:45)
- The panel order can follow the voice-over rather than event chronology. Keep previous panels visible while adding new ones so sequence and continuity can be reviewed together. (433 · U4 · L13 · 01:17–04:05)
- Frame each moment for the information it needs: a close view can emphasize expression or detail; a wide view can establish context. (433 · U4 · L09 · 04:19–05:52)
- Connect scenes through a shared form, color, speed, direction or camera movement; use a transition only when it strengthens continuity or narrative. (433 · U4 · L11 · 02:21–02:42; 433 · U4 · L11 · 02:42–03:52)
- An animatic places the storyboard on a timeline, adds scene durations and rough camera or transition movement, and tests whether the story works in time. Replay and revise it. (433 · U5 · L14 · 00:00–00:30; 433 · U5 · L14 · 01:32–01:50; 433 · U5 · L14 · 02:57–03:26)
- Sound can be prototyped alongside the animatic: test voice-over factors such as intonation and pauses, and sketch music and foley as narrative tools. Music can set the overall tone; foley can strengthen on-screen actions. (433 · U5 · L15 · 00:00–01:29; 433 · U5 · L15 · 04:22–05:09)
- Music can also provide a rhythmic base for animation, with a beat that can determine shot duration and support choreography timed to audio. (433 · U5 · L15 · 03:25–04:22)

## Failures

**Problem 1: a transition is a flourish without a reason.**
- **The failure:** movement between scenes is forced and weakens continuity. (433 · U4 · L11 · 02:42–03:52)
- **The fix:** state which visual or narrative element carries across; if none does useful work, cut directly.

**Problem 2: the sequence has no tested duration.**
- **The failure:** a still sequence feels plausible but runs too long or gives a key idea too little time. (433 · U5 · L14 · 00:00–00:30; 433 · U5 · L14 · 00:30–01:32)
- **The fix:** time the panels, replay with voice or narration, then remove, merge, shorten or expand scenes as the story needs. (433 · U5 · L14 · 00:30–01:32; 433 · U5 · L14 · 02:57–03:26)

## Check before moving on

1. **T4 — Storyboard and continuity:** pass when the panels explain the story from beginning to end, required narration beats have panels, and each transition has a continuity or narrative reason.
2. **T6 — Timing and reading:** pass when the animatic fits the intended runtime and the fresh reviewer can read the information before it changes.

**Practice the teacher sets:** build a storyboard from the voice-over, then distribute it over a timeline and review scene timing. See [Unit 4](../course-433/U04-unit.md), [Unit 5](../course-433/U05-unit.md), and [animatic](../course-433/U05-L14-animatic.md).
