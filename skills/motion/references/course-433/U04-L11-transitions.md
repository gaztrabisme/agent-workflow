## Continuity and common transitions

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–01:18

Next we will look at what transitions are and what you need to consider before using them in your story. Graphic transitions (*transiciones gráficas*) are another very useful tool, but they require a delicate balance between ingenuity and judgment.

To enter the world of transitions, we first need to understand a key concept: continuity (*continuidad*). Narrative continuity is the natural flow of information we see on screen as we move from one scene to another. The most common way to maintain continuity in a story is to keep certain details consistent, both in time and in space. If continuity is handled carefully, the message will flow naturally.

Sometimes, to maintain continuity between two scenes or situations that are not entirely related, we use a transition. In film and music videos, people mainly use basic but effective transition devices such as fading and fade-out (*fading y fade out*), the iris effect (*efecto iris*), a wipe (*barrido*) and editing tricks (*juegos de montaje*). The work of Edgar Wright, whom we saw as a reference earlier in the course, is one example. Another is the video directed by Hiro Murai (captions: *Hiromu Rai*) for the song “This Is America.”

## Transitions as a narrative tool

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L11 · 01:18–02:21

In Stanley Kubrick’s film *2001: A Space Odyssey* (captions: *2001 y 100 al espacio*), we see what is perhaps one of the most classic transitions in film history. It is quite simple: a primitive man throws a bone into the sky and, with a cut (*corte*), it becomes a space station floating among the stars.

Why is the transition important? What is special about it? It is a simple detail, but a very important one. It is important to understand that a transition is a narrative tool (*herramienta narrativa*), not a pyrotechnic trick meant to pull you out of the story (captions: *hacerme atirarte de la historia*). In other words, it has meaning: there is a metaphor behind it, and it has a reason to exist. Used well, a transition can strengthen the message, adding a pleasant element of surprise that brings nuance to the overall narrative.

Although most of the examples we have seen so far use transitions in film, many people, including me, think transitions really shine in animated storytelling. Animation takes advantage of unique features of the format that let us take the message and its “wow” factor to another level. Why? What makes it special? Unlike live action, animation lets us use the image itself as the DNA of the transition.

## Using the image itself to make a transition

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L11 · 02:21–02:42

Anything can happen between one animation frame (*frame*) and the next. We can transform an eye into a planet using the basic geometries that make it up, without needing resources beyond the visual style itself. There are certain keys we need to keep in mind to make a good transition.

## Choosing and controlling a transition

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L11 · 02:42–03:52

The first key is to look for formal elements that are common to the two scenes we want to join. Look for elements such as geometry or colour, or any other element that can serve as a point of consistency between the two frames.

Another key is to pay attention to the speed and direction in which elements move on screen. The camera recording the action can also be an excellent bridge connecting the two scenes.

Finally, and perhaps most importantly, never force a transition. Ask yourself whether using a transition in your story is justified. Is it necessary? If it really strengthens your narrative, find the best transition and execute it. But if it feels like a whim and you realize you are forcing it, it is better to avoid it.

A transition is a great tool and can be fun to use, but always remember that continuity is what matters, along with maintaining or strengthening the piece’s narrative. It is like cooking: do not overuse the seasoning, or your dish could turn out to be a real failure.
