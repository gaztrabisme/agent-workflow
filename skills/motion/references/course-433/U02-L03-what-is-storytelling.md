## Storytelling as a human capacity

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:14

In this lesson, we will look at what storytelling is and why it matters for motion design. We will also examine a project by Smog as a case study (the captions initially read the studio's name as *Smoke*). In this unit, we will get directly into storytelling as the basis for a good story. Let us begin with the basic question: What is storytelling, and why is it important? The answer is in the word itself: storytelling is the practice of telling stories.

This ability to tell stories is among the oldest things in human life; it is part of human behavior. It lets us connect emotionally through a story. It lets stories reach you, enter your heart, take over your head, fill your body, and fill your spirit. Storytelling has the ability to join the rational with the instinctive.

## Stories in everyday interactions and design

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 01:14–02:19

The clearest way we may encounter storytelling is through books, poems, films, songs, and similar forms. But storytelling is not limited to artists working in those forms. We all tell stories every day: how work went, an anecdote at the supermarket, how you met your partner, and so on.

One might think storytelling is just a tool for entertainment. It is not that narrowly limited; it can be part of many disciplines. In my area, design, there is storytelling when we prepare a keynote, make a presentation to an audience, or give a pitch. There is storytelling even in the layout of Lego instructions, which is designed to help us understand how to assemble something. An airport layout lets you find your boarding gate without getting lost. We also encounter it when using an app interface or an ATM. Each of these situations contains a narrative in the interaction between the communicator and the receiver, helping the message reach the receiver appropriately.

## How stories communicate and stay with people

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 02:19–03:40

These are different ways of applying storytelling, but what are stories? They are a particular way of presenting facts and data so they are easy to tell and remember. This is directly connected to the respective aims of the sender or communicator and the receiver. A good story creates a strong connection between sender and receiver. Telling and remembering lead to understanding the message. If the receiver understands it in a memorable way, they are likely to share it later and become a sender too. Put more simply, people love good stories, which is why we share them.

Storytelling is a powerful communication tool that lets us transmit values, emotions, instructions, and practically anything we want. In this course, we will focus on applying it to motion design. There is a saying that people may forget what you said and what you did, but they will never forget how you made them feel. I think that sums up what I have just said: a good story creates a strong connection.

## The campaign’s problem and goal

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 04:43–06:24

The case study is a campaign that our production company, Smog, made for the Amparo y Justicia Foundation. Among its aims, the foundation seeks to reduce what is called secondary victimization (*victimización secundaria*) among children and adolescents who have suffered sexual abuse. Secondary victimization is the additional suffering inflicted on an abused child by institutions and other people in the criminal process. Between the abuse and the child’s recovery, an error in the criminal process causes secondary victimization: the child relives the moment of abuse over and over.

The foundation proposed a bill to simplify the process through video-recorded interviews (*entrevistas videograbadas*). These aim to reduce the instances of questioning so the child does not have to relive the abuse in their mind. The foundation approached us to make a video presenting the initiative. The subject is extremely delicate to show. It is not easy to talk to people about it, and it is not easy to show it on a screen. So we used a good animated story to present the facts and invite people to be part of the solution.

The campaign’s final goal is to present a bill: an ambitious goal. We trusted that animated storytelling could help us reach the right result. And this was the result of that process.

## What the campaign shows

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 06:24–07:46

The campaign film says that thousands of children in Chile are victims of sexual abuse. Each year, 20,000 cases are reported, and thousands more are not. Abuse marks a before and after in a child’s life, and the traumatic experience does not end when the crime is reported. The child has to go through a long process in the penal system. They tell different people their nightmare, answer the same questions, and relive the pain repeatedly. The child feels that nobody believes them. This is called secondary victimization.

The process can last more than a year without reparative psychological support. The film asks whether this is fair and how many more children must suffer. It then presents video-recorded interviews in a place suited to the child, with a trained specialist. This can reduce the number of interrogations and gather better evidence in a timely way, while safeguarding and optimizing the rights of those involved. The system includes guidance for the child and family and, most importantly, respects the child’s rights as a victim. With video-recorded interviews, the child can receive psychological support in time and begin recovery.

## A protagonist and a shadow

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 07:46–08:43

Next, I will review in detail, step by step, the instances and decisions that went into building the story we have just seen. First, we see this space taking shape: a very minimalist setting. We chose it because we need to focus our attention on the protagonist who has just entered the screen. The character is an orange child with a somewhat fragile anatomy, almost like a bird, playing with something from childhood: a paper airplane. That says something about the character’s innocence.

The other element on screen is the shadow the child casts over the world. From this point on, we are saying that these two elements—the character and the shadow—will be fundamental to the narrative. The child now takes the paper-airplane game out of the frame.

## From childhood play to abuse and the scale of the problem

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 08:43–10:53

The paper airplane is quickly replaced by another element from a child’s imagination: a soccer ball. We know the ball came from outside the frame. We do not know who sent it, but we know somebody did. That person suddenly materializes as a shadow. The shadow is quite generic, so we do not know whether it belongs to someone the child knows or to someone entirely outside their life.

The important thing is that we begin to see the shadow has thrown the ball as a kind of bait. We see its real self when it transforms into a threatening monster stalking the child. The shadow waits for the right moment to pounce. In this moment, we represent the abuse very abstractly. The shadow takes over the child’s image; the child loses their personality, stops playing, and becomes frozen by what has happened. The abuser figure has made the child’s personality disappear. The child has lost their real identity.

Once we have shown what happened, we give hard data to help people understand and contextualize the problem. We say that 20,000 children are reported as victims of abuse each year. How do we represent what 20,000 means? It sounds like a lot, but how many children is that really? We decided to use the analogy of how many children fit on a soccer field. We imagined a soccer field full of children: that is the annual number of children declared victims of abuse.

Then we show that some cases are not necessarily reported or declared. There may be many more cases than we know about, and they are increasing. That is why we put a counter in the upper-left corner of the screen that keeps going up. The image of the abuser appears again, as a constant, threatening presence. Through a transition, we see the child begin to look for a way to report the abuser.

## The justice process and secondary victimization

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 10:53–12:32

In the system, the first thing that happens is that the child goes to the police. This is the first stage: the child gives a statement and finds a trusted person to tell what happened, whether recently or some time ago. It is the beginning of a tortuous journey.

We symbolize that journey with what looks like an endless rotation. The child passes in front of different people and through different stages, feeling completely out of place because they are forced to recreate the most terrible moment of their life. This is secondary victimization: having to go through the same situation again and again. We add a calendar on the left to show time passing. Instead of finding the recovery they need, the child only relives the same process of abuse in their mind.

We reach a moment of deepest desolation, when the child falls into an abyss of despair and believes there is no solution to what is happening. Here we show that the child is not alone: many children are going through exactly the same thing without receiving the help they need. At this moment of greatest desperation and desolation, the first possibility of a solution begins to appear.

## The recorded interview as a solution, and the story’s structure

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 12:32–14:38

The foundation’s option is a video-recorded interview, which we show with a change of color and the child being released. Instead of several intimidating interviews with different professionals, the child has one interview with someone they may be comfortable with, and a recording can be made.

Copies of that audiovisual record can provide the material needed for the different people involved in the case to take part in it. The child then does not have to keep sharing the story with them and can focus on their family, where they can find the support and keys to recovery. We show recovery as gradual: the child begins to trust the soccer ball again—the ball represents the moment of abuse—and regains some confidence, recovers their identity, and finally becomes a child again.

We are now looking at the storyboard of the film. It has three main areas. At the top, Act 1 presents the character and the conflict. In Act 2, the color and behavior of the characters change noticeably; this is where the process malfunctions and the problem that must be solved appears. The problem begins to be solved gradually at the connection between Acts 2 and 3. There we return to bright, natural colors and find the solution the foundation offers through video-recorded interviews.

## Character design and a visual DNA

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 14:38–15:45

For the process, we had to create the characters and elements the story needed. The threatening figure, the shadow and abuser, had to be a rather macabre design, both organic and deformed. The adults needed the same simplicity of form as the child, but they had to appear more distant and unfamiliar—almost as a child sees adults, mainly as legs and larger bodies.

The child’s particular design includes a small nod to the foundation’s logo, which we can see at the upper right of the screen. Working with a well-built story and clearly marked rules—a marked-out playing field—lets us establish a DNA we can use to transform and create new stories from this starting point. This beginning lets us build new stories and narratives that belong to the visual world we have just created.

## Extending the campaign’s visual rules

Anchor: 433 · U2 · L03 · 15:45–18:05

In this campaign, the first film let us keep working on a series of videos that build on the same imagined world of the abused child. On the left of the screen is video 2, where we go directly to the bill. Some analogies are the passage of time, shown by a tree growing leaves to signal that a year has passed, and a light-blue bird representing the community’s companionship through social media. The main element is a machine: it represents the criminal process as overdesigned, which the law aims to improve and simplify for the personal benefit of the victims, so they can recover their childhood.

For the third stage, we made a video about the vote. We had already presented a bill and got it to Congress; now we needed the deputies to vote for it. We used a new analogy based on hands: the hands at the moment the child faces an abuser, the hands that judge the child, the hands that can protect the child, and the hands of the deputies who could change the political system so the child can recover. We treated it as an open letter from the child to the deputies. It all follows the same visual DNA we created in the first advertisement. We established rules that let us create real worlds around our character and the setting around them.

I hope this analysis has helped introduce storytelling in motion design more directly. In the next units, we will take up each of the tools that can help us tell this story. We will look more closely at several things from this case study in the following chapters. Now that we know what storytelling is and how it is applied, we can move to planning.
