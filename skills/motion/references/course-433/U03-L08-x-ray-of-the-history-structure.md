## Structure as the story's skeleton

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L08 · 00:08–01:08

Finally, the last part of our equation is structure, which is, in a way, the skeleton that lets us build our story. As a concept, structure is the arrangement and order of parts within a whole. Every story needs a structure to support it. So when we talk about narrative structure (*estructura narrativa*), we mean the order in which we present a story's events, as well as the temporal importance of each event. Put simply, structure is the way the narrated events unfold.

One very graphic way to understand narrative structure is to think of it as a roller coaster: we have a clear starting point and a clear endpoint. These are our only certainties, our only fixed points. Everything that happens in between is held together by the structure. We can have endless turns, highs and lows, steep plunges, or anything else in between. What matters is that a structure lets all this happen without our story losing its meaning.

## The three-act structure

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L08 · 01:08–02:54

People usually talk about a three-act structure (*estructura de tres actos*). Why? Because it is the most common and has been used the most since people began telling stories, from Aristotle to Hitchcock. What does this structure consist of? Let's look at it and explain it simply. We can divide any story we work on chronologically into three stations (*estaciones*), each connected to the next by an important point in the story, a small climax.

In the first station, we introduce our characters, the rules of the world they inhabit, and a conflict that needs attention. This is what we know as Act 1. Then the decision is made to address this conflict, and the journey begins. This is our first climax.

The second station is the search for a solution to the conflict. At first we encounter the pros, and then the cons; the cons grow progressively more severe. This is the middle of Act 2, until we reach a point where we are completely cornered, see no clear solution, and feel defeated. Even so, we stake everything on one last attempt to reach our final solution. That is our second climax.

Finally, in the third station, we find the solution to our search and close the cycle. Think of any film or book you've seen or read lately and see whether you can find this three-act structure behind it. It probably won't be difficult, because, as I said, most current narratives are built on this structure.

It's easy to get confused and think this structure is only for stories with hero-type characters. But it is just as valid for presenting animated infographics, motion-graphics videos, and so on.

## Linear and non-linear structure

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L08 · 02:54–03:31

The three-act structure is a type of linear narrative (*narrativa lineal*): it has an established chronological order and presents events continuously. But what if we want to break this linearity? What if we want to disrupt the conventional narrative we're working with? That's where non-linear structures (*estructuras no lineales*) come in. Events break chronological order instead of following the predetermined pattern of the timeline. This approach is riskier, less conventional, and freer to work with. But that doesn't mean it rejects an underlying structure.

## Non-linear structures and their examples

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L08 · 03:31–05:03

Let's look at a few types of non-linear structure. The first is a circular structure (*estructura circular*), in which the story ends at the same point where it began. Anything can happen between the two points, but we return to the beginning. A classic example is *The Wizard of Oz*: it starts with Dorothy in Kansas, where a tornado occurs; she enters the world of Oz and returns to Kansas. The film also makes this break visually: both the beginning and the ending are in black and white, while the whole journey through Oz is in color.

Another kind of non-linear narrative is the puzzle structure (*estructura de rompecabezas*). Its pieces are rearranged so that clues to the ending are hidden. A clear example is *Memento*, by Christopher Nolan. Another is *Irreversible*, by Gaspar Noé. That film is actually divided into three scenes told in reverse, which gradually clears up the doubts as the running time advances.

One final type is the bead-necklace structure (*collar de cuentas*). It uses a connecting element to join different elements that do not have much relation to one another on their own. Music videos are one example: the music track, the performer's song, lets us join a series of images that work as a music video in context. The same could happen when visualizing a presidential candidate's speech.

## Keep the story's goal in view

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L08 · 05:43–06:50

Let's recap what we have so far in our storytelling exercise. On one hand, my premise (*premisa*) is defined, based on a set of ideas and concepts I want to work with visually later. On the other hand, I've defined the tone (*tono*) I'll use for my narrative: mystery with some epic overtones. Finally, we've identified a possible three-act structure, and I'll add a circular element to it.

One final note before we finish: however interesting it is to play with structure, it's important to fulfill the story's ultimate goal. That must be your north star; don't lose focus. When we build a structure, we're clarifying how we're telling our story. The ultimate goal is for a receiver to understand our message. That must always be our focus; we can't lose it, however entertaining it is to play with these tools.

Now that we understand structure, we've reviewed our equation in full. With this, we can move on to the next unit, where we'll start building our story.
