## Revisit the premise and keep the prototype open

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–01:17

In this lesson I will put all the tools and concepts we have seen so far into practice. Let’s recap what we know. We have a voice-over with three acts, and we have identified concepts that seem interesting to explore. From this voice-over (captions: *boiso*), I have developed a premise (*premisa*) clear enough to express the essence of the storytelling, but open enough not to restrict how I visualize it. I know I want a mysterious tone, with some epic touches, and a circular narrative (*narrativa circular*) to get more out of the story.

Before we begin, there are two things to remember. First, however long it took to make each decision, nothing guarantees that decision will stay in place permanently. It can still change. That is part of the process and part of the pleasure of telling a story: this is constant prototyping (*prototipado*). Second, this is my way of approaching this storytelling, and yours can—and should—be completely different.

## Set the opening, protagonist, and unknown world

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L13 · 01:17–04:05

With those points clear, I start prototyping the storyboard (*storyboard*) so I can see the story progress. I follow the voice-over in order. That does not have to be the story’s chronological order; it is simply the voice-over’s sequence. First I need to introduce the world in which the protagonist and conflict will play out. In my premise and idea list, I chose a campfire as the place where stories are told. It seems fitting for presenting a legend, so my first panel (*viñeta*) is the fire. There is a little context, but I do not want to detail what is happening around it; I only suggest that people are nearby. The opening panel is about the fire.

In the next panel, I introduce the protagonist, lit by the campfire. He is the one who will tell us the story (captions: *¿Int?*). We know nothing about him yet (captions: *Nada de la 1*); we only know that he is inviting us on an adventure. The voice-over says, “Legend has it.” This image is about him inviting us; he is going to tell us something.

I keep the earlier designed or sketched panel beside the new one. It is important to see the overall story, not just an individual scene. Always keep the context in view. When you use Post-its to assemble your storytelling, place them side by side so you can keep checking where you are on the timeline.

If the fire is what takes us on this adventure, then we need to show the elements that will tell the story. In my case, I want sparks and the trails of light they leave as they break away from the fire. I use this abstract, almost poetic image to introduce the unknown place. The voice-over says, “A place unlike anything you have seen before.” Here the magic is revealed and the adventure begins: the sparks and their light trails form an abstract landscape. We may recognize elements of nature, but no particular place. At this point I wonder whether to put a mysterious traveler physically into the scene, perhaps our protagonist. I add him for now and will decide later whether he stays. Perhaps he should enter from the camera rather than already being shown full-body, so we can notice him entering this world. I try a version, but leave the choice open. It may work; it is still in question. This is constant trial and error.

## Sequence color, aroma, and inhabitants

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L13 · 04:05–06:34

The voice-over now says that the place’s colors, aromas, and inhabitants are foreign to our reality. I think each deserves its own panel: one for color, one for aroma, and one for inhabitants. During brainstorming I had an idea for an analogy about color: an eye whose pupil changes color while the voice-over describes the different colors. I add it because it could work well, especially with a transition (*transición*) into it.

I look at the previous panel and ask whether a transition belongs here. It seems like an ideal place for one, perhaps a magical moment. The two panels need an element that joins them. As we saw when discussing transitions, a geometric or physical element could make the change feel more organic. A circle would work; perhaps a sun in this landscape could create the transition. I turn the character to face forward as he arrives. That works better, though I am still unsure about the idea. When I see the transition incorporated, I think it can work and decide to keep it for now.

The voice-over presents color, aroma, and inhabitants in sequence, so the visual sequence might also feel like a journey. I consider a left-to-right camera pan (*paneo*), with the eye transforming into an abstract form of aroma, and the movement continuing into a space where silhouettes appear to suggest inhabitants. I had mentioned silhouettes during brainstorming, and I think they will work well in this firelit scheme by playing with the figures’ backlighting against the background. I like the result and leave it in.

## Establish a boundary around the parallel world

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L13 · 06:34–08:01

We continue with the voice-over line, “But you should not be afraid to go to that place.” In my ideas I had the theme of a parallel world. I like framing this mysterious world as a space that cannot be reached. Perhaps it becomes a cube containing that unreachable space. The camera could pull back out of the frame to reveal the mysterious space as a cube, then introduce our traveler, who is foreign to that space and looking across the distance.

I liked the idea more in my head than when I made it. We have been moving in a steady pan from left to right; arriving here would create a change of axis that might feel awkward or contrived. So perhaps we should maintain the pan and find our character at its end, with a wall separating him from the fantasy world. We can use the profile face from the earlier image: just as the camera reaches our character, the face turns toward camera. That creates an encounter between our traveler, who is outside this world, and another being inside it who looks back at him. It could be a poetic meeting.

## Cross the boundary, fall, and return

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L13 · 08:01–09:42

The next voice-over line is about leaving insecurity behind. I could use a close-up (*primer plano*) of the protagonist’s hand as he hesitantly tries to cross the line. He is curious about the parallel world. He touches it, crosses over, and falls into the free-fall idea I had proposed earlier. That seems like a good way to represent what happens.

When I draw it, however, it does not work as well. A free fall goes from top to bottom, not across from side to side. I like the idea of the leap into the void, so perhaps the answer is to return to the previous image and break the axis as the hand crosses, rotating the composition to give us the horizontal space needed to show the fall. That could add useful movement to the sequence: we travel left to right, arrive here and stop; the crossing hand breaks the axis. Then we show the character falling in a much wider shot (*plano*), making him feel small in the immensity of the unknown. I think this could work.

From there we can cut to the falling character, then return to the opening image of him at the campfire and close the cycle. But that return has a problem: the character comes back without having advanced or changed, while the voice-over is about change and the character’s evolution. I need to solve that.

## Show the traveler changing, then reveal the family

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L13 · 09:42–11:46

In my initial notes, I had written down a growing beard as a sign of wisdom, change, and growth. I think we should use it. If this character is one person during the journey and another when he arrives, it makes sense for him to have a beard at the beginning, disappear from view during the journey, and then show beard growth when he returns. I put the beard into the opening scene so it also becomes a recognizable feature. Then, during the free fall, we might see the beard growing as he becomes more comfortable and gives himself over to the journey. Finally the beard is complete, and we see him in front of the fire, closing the cycle. That seems much more interesting and appealing.

The ending says the journey is only just beginning. Here we reveal the true context and nature of the character, an idea I had from the start and what motivated me to take the story in this direction: he is a father camping with his children in a forest. This father figure tells the story as an expression of wisdom and an invitation to his children to take a journey that is both physical and spiritual. We now recognize that the silhouettes we saw at the beginning are his children. We understand the ending, and the circular narrative is complete.

## Review the storyboard and hand it forward

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L13 · 11:46–14:33

Now I look at the storyboard as a whole after removing the panels I do not want to use. The only part I am still unsure about is the fourth panel, with the character entering the landscape. I do not think he belongs there yet. It is better to show him entering in panel eight, where we recognize a character arriving. That lets the world stay more abstract, so I will remove the earlier panel.

When you look at the full storyboard and feel good about the story, remember that yours will have its own nuances and characters. You could use squares and circles, for example, or it could even feel like a commercial for a travel agency; the voice-over allows those possibilities. Looking at the whole thing also lets us see the tools from the course at work. We have used a range of framing shots (*planos de encuadre*) to give different weight to the stages of the story. The main character has a distinctive trait that makes him easy to recognize. Two transitions strengthen the story and add surprise. Visual analogies represent color and aroma. The secondary characters are less developed than the protagonist but share the same visual DNA. There is an interesting directional transition that gives the piece movement. It adds tension leading into the climax (*clímax*) (captions: *clima*); that is probably my favorite part of the exercise. Everything follows a classic three-act structure with a circular ending, which gives the narrative a satisfying close.

Now that the prototype works, I can take it to a storyboard artist for a more finished version, use that to make an animatic (*animatic*), and move into production. This is my prototype; I can give it to a specialist to make the definitive version. As I said at the beginning, we are working on the story, not its final appearance. Whether it eventually uses 3D or 2D characters, stop motion, or vector illustrations is a decision for another stage. We will decide then which form best represents the story. What matters now is that the story works and has distinctive, engaging storytelling. The project has taken shape; in the next unit we will see it begin to come to life.
