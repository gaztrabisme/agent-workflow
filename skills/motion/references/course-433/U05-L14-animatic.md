## The animatic tests the story’s timing

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L14 · 00:00–00:30

To bring our story to life, the first step is to make an animatic. Up to this point, the time and duration of the audiovisual piece have been ambiguous and abstract. We cannot keep avoiding this factor, though, because it is fundamental. The animatic is the last link that lets us validate whether our story works.

## Diagnose the storyboard by imagining it in motion

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L14 · 00:30–01:32

First, take the storyboard for the project you are working on and try to picture it mentally. Imagine the story in motion and tell it to yourself. This may sound a little ridiculous, but it is a good way to approach the actual narration time. Remember, this is your story and you are the one telling it, so you are best placed to gauge its timing. You already estimated the project’s approximate duration during the initial planning.

Be completely honest with yourself in this first mental run-through. If the story already feels too long for the time available, review it so you can edit or rethink parts. Are there unnecessary scenes? Can we merge ideas? Is there a way to show something more briefly? Or, on the contrary, do we need more time to make something clear? These choices have side effects, which we have to accept. As we have said several times, this is a continual exercise; no part is guaranteed to stay.

## Put the storyboard on a timeline

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L14 · 01:32–01:50

Once we have worked through these questions, we can move on to the animatic. Basically, we distribute the storyboard across a timeline and give each scene the time it needs. The result is a video with timings that can serve as a basis for the animation stage.

## Choose the level of detail the project needs

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L14 · 01:50–02:16

An animatic can be elaborate and detailed, or rough and general; it depends on what you need and how complex the project is. A good example of an elaborate animatic is the one directed by Scott O’Brien (captions: “Scott O’Ryan”) for the *Teen Titans* series. We can practically see every movement each character will make in the animation. It is an excellent lead-in to the animation process, which is a major factor to consider while developing an animatic.

## Review camera movements, transitions, and versions

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L14 · 02:57–03:26

Besides helping us estimate the approximate duration of each scene, an animatic lets us review and visualize the camera movements and transitions we want to use in the final animation, even if only roughly. Remember, the animatic’s purpose is to plan the animation: this is where we make many of the decisions we need before moving to animation.

It is likely that your first animatic will not give you an immediate result. You may have more than one version—in fact, many versions—before you reach the final one. That is exactly the point. Adjust or replace every item that does not work until you are satisfied with the result.

## Let the story, rather than pride, guide the edits

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L14 · 03:26–03:51

With the animatic, your story begins to come to life, but the process can be frustrating. I warn you now: set your pride aside and focus on making it work. Having seen all this, we can move on to the next lesson, about sound.
