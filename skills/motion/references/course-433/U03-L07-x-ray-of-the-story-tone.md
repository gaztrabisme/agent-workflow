## Tone and a story's identity

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–00:41

The second link in our equation is tone (*tono*), which helps define the personality of the story we want to tell. It is probably a concept you already know, either from music or from talking about the tone someone used with you. It is this latter sense that I mean. In language, tone is how the narrative style is expressed and what it intends to do. Tone helps define and understand a story's identity.

## How tone changes and connects a story

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L07 · 00:41–02:08

The same story can be told in different tones. The tone we choose gives it its personality. For example, in film, we can tell a romantic story in a comedic tone (captions: *comer*). The romantic comedy is a narrative classic, but a romantic story can also be told dramatically or ironically. One example is *Deadpool 1*: although it is dressed up as a violent antihero film, it is really a love story told from a different angle.

The most important function (*función*) of tone is to trigger a direct relationship between the sender (*emisor*) and the audience (*audiencia*); it creates a bond. Tone is key to immersing the receiver (*receptor*) in our story.

In the previous lesson we talked about function. As we saw at the start of this unit, these are complementary ingredients. Function and tone complement each other in our equation and nourish one another, opening up endless narrative possibilities. We can list functions on one side and tones on the other, then cross them. The combinations give new dimensions to what might seem obvious. For example, an explanatory video does not have to use a serious or neutral tone, as explanatory videos often do. It could be comedic or ironic. That depends on how you want to tell the story.

## Make the intended tone clear

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L07 · 02:55–03:42

The narrative tone we want to impose must be completely clear to the viewer. We have to avoid misunderstandings. A classic example is misreading a WhatsApp message: you can misunderstand what someone means or the tone in which they are saying it. Written text has no tone by itself; if someone writes in uppercase, that alone can make you think they are shouting. Reading it with a different intention can make the message feel completely opposite. You might hear irony where there is none, or anger where there is none. We have to avoid misunderstandings.

That is why, in audiovisual storytelling, when we work with tone we must be precise about how we approach it, so the viewer receives the message and can take in the story in the intended way.
