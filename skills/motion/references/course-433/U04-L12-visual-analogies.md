## What a visual analogy does

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L12 · 00:00–01:14

This is the final tool in the unit: visual analogies (*analogías visuales*). I like using them because they have great potential for helping tell a story. An analogy (*analogía*) identifies similar attributes in different beings or things. In conversation, we use analogies to explain points more clearly when they might be too abstract for the person listening. To talk about sizes, distances, quantities, and similar ideas, we use examples that help someone grasp the scale. People often say, “explain it with pears and apples.” That means representing something complicated in a simple way by comparing it with everyday things. An analogy makes the material familiar, so the receiver can take in the intended information.

In motion design, we can use this visually in many ways to give a story a more distinctive and attractive language. Here are examples of different kinds of analogy.

## Use a different context to explain the same thing

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L12 · 01:14–01:56

First is a contextual analogy (*analogía por contexto*). Imagine that we need to represent people working in a factory. One way to tell that story more attractively is to show bees producing honey in a hive. We are telling the same thing, but from a different, nonliteral context.

A few years ago in Chile, an advertising campaign discussed the types and numbers of Chileans living in the country. It used people in a public swimming pool as an analogy. The pool represented the country; a diverse group of people represented the population proportionally, but on a smaller scale, with figures that were easier for a viewer to take in.

## Give characters visual traits that suggest personality

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L12 · 01:56–02:57

Another kind is the character analogy (*analogía de personaje*). We can give characters physical or visual codes that act as analogies for their personalities. A classic example is Zootopia (captions: *Utopía*), where each animal clearly represents a kind of personality. The fox is clever; the rabbit is quick but naive. The film also has clever analogies such as the slowness of the public-service sloths, the overweight cheetah (captions: *cita*), and—spoiler—the villain hidden beneath the appearance of an innocent lamb.

In Pixar’s The Incredibles, each family member’s power is connected to a personality trait. The father has superhuman strength to carry the family burden. The mother is elastic so she can meet each of her family’s needs. The daughter becomes invisible, a clear representation of adolescent shyness. The son is fast and active (captions: *veractivo*), like a child his age. The youngest, Jack-Jack, is unpredictable and has no control over himself, like a baby. It is a wonderful exercise.

## Use scale to make hard-to-imagine quantities concrete

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L12 · 02:57–03:58

Finally, dimensional analogies (*analogías por dimensiones*) help us visualize quantities and measurements that are hard to imagine at first. Jurassic Park gave all of us the wrong proportions for the Velociraptor. Even if someone tells us that a Velociraptor was about 0.5 meters tall and 1.2 meters long, we still picture one chasing children its own size through the park’s kitchen. But if I clarify that a real Velociraptor was the size of a chicken, the picture changes—and the fact is genuinely surprising.

One great advantage of visual analogies is that they bring us closer to the receiver by making that person a participant in the information. The analogy therefore needs to feel familiar to the specific audience for the story. Only then can it become a decisive part of the narrative. These are the tools I personally like to work with. In the next lesson, we will put them into practice and build our story.
