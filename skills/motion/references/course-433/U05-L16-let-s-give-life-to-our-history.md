## Estimate the timing, then refine the storyboard

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L16 · 00:00–01:31

That is all for the theory. Now let’s put what we have learned into practice and bring our story to life. We will turn the material we have been working on into an animatic (*animática*). As we saw in the first lesson of this unit, the first step is to compare the time we have estimated with our storyboard and see whether anything needs to change.

I begin by imagining how much time each scene needs. How long do I need to see the character sitting in front of the fire? How long should we see the little flames and their trails before we begin to form the different landscapes? Roughly how long should the fall last? This is very basic, nothing sophisticated, but at least it gives you a sense of how long it is taking. In this case, I will not make any changes because there is no duration restriction; I can play freely with this variable.

So I ask a storyboard artist to redraw my storyboard cleanly, giving me more finished material to work with. Andrés Rodríguez made this storyboard. He is a good friend and illustrator who was part of SMOG (captions: “SMOKE”) for a long time, and I trust him to interpret the essence of my source material. That is important so the essence of the storytelling is not lost. Remember, we are telling your story, so the storyboard artist must respect that essence. Now let’s go step by step.

## Number the scenes and set up the editing project

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L16 · 01:31–02:42

We begin assembling the storyboard from the material we have. First, number and order its scenes. It does not matter whether you call them scenes or panels, or simply assign each one a number or a letter. What matters is having an order that lets us identify them quickly and gives anyone who later uses the animatic a guide for the animation.

Once the images are ordered, we open video-editing software. I will use After Effects because it suits me and I feel comfortable with it, but you can use Premiere, Final Cut, or any software that lets you distribute images along a timeline. I open a new file, import the storyboard images, and then make a new composition. I will use HD dimensions of 1920 by 1080 at 24 frames per second. The frame rate concerns playback speed: 24 images are shown for every second that passes. Digital cinema usually uses 24 frames per second, so we will use the same setting.

## Add and pace the voice-over and images

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L16 · 02:42–03:32

Along with the images, I now import my voice-over (*voiceover*). I recorded the text of “The Journey” beforehand for this montage. I gave the voice-over a slow pace because, as I mentioned in the previous lesson, it is a narrative tool that lets us tell the story on our own terms.

When you record your own voice-over for “The Journey,” look for the way that suits the telling. Record it several times and try different tones. Imagine that you are the project’s real narrator, even if a professional voice actor records it later. For your mock-up (*maqueta*), you need to set the pace. Once my audio is in, I distribute the images along the timeline and arrange them against the audio using estimated timings. I do this roughly at first; we will order everything and then adjust it.

## Test scene durations and camera moves

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L16 · 03:32–04:40

First, I give every scene the same arbitrary base duration. There is no rule behind it; I am just making an initial distribution across the total time. Once the scenes have been distributed, I start taking their durations more seriously.

With the material ordered, I test how long I think each scene should stay on screen. This is personal: you decide how long to show each scene. How long do I want the fire to stay on screen before the character appears? How long should we see the eye before moving the camera to the next panel? And so on.

Depending on your knowledge of the software, use it to rough out some camera movements: zooming in or out, pans, or whatever you think will help people understand what you want to tell. However basic, a movement can help. Then we review it in real time to see how it looks—or, better, how the story feels.

## Revise the rhythm and edit the audio

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L16 · 04:40–05:29

Frustration usually appears in these first viewings. The animatic probably will not work yet, but do not worry—that is what this stage is for: making it work. It would be very unusual for it to work immediately. So we review the duration of each scene one by one. There is no formula to follow; you have to find the rhythm you want.

You need some abstraction to imagine the actions in each panel moving, but the most important thing is that you feel the rhythm works for your story. Be patient; it will work, and you do not need to despair. You can edit the audio too, leaving more breathing room or tightening spaces. Do not assume it has to stay at the timings you first recorded. Everything is a continual layout (*maquetación*) process.

## Add useful notes, including ideas for sound

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L16 · 05:29–07:06

It is a good idea to put the names and durations of your scenes in the editing program, along with any information that will be useful to the people who continue working on the material. What we are doing is editing, and this is where the secret is, where the magic is. The process should be more organic than mechanical. Trust your instinct and test the edit over and over, as many times as needed. If a scene has to be removed even at this stage, accept it. It is for the good of the story. Check the reading, clarity, and cleanliness; those are the most important things here.

For the audio, I want to use the sound of the campfire as a base because I think it will give the piece a rich atmosphere. I look online for a fire sound to include in my mock-up. There are several free libraries online. Their sounds may not be the best quality to work with, but they are good enough for making mock-ups.

When I listened again to the campfire sound I had chosen, I had a fun thought. While working on the music, when we send this to a musician to work on the final animation, perhaps we can use the crackles and little pops as a rhythmic base. Making music from those small, organic beats could be interesting. I start adding ideas like this as notes in my animatic: notes for the musician, for myself, and for the project to become more and more its own thing.

## Keep reviewing, then render a version

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L16 · 07:06–08:12

I keep testing to see how the animatic is going. I make more versions, test the timings again, and change durations until I am more or less satisfied. I feel that what I have is beginning to work, and I like what is starting to happen. So I also begin to refine the other notes and see what other durations I want. I insist: review it continually and be very self-critical. Let it rest for a day, come back to it another day, and watch it again. If it needs to go back into the operating room for more surgery, do that.

I think I will leave it here because it is working well. But, as in any process, I will stay open to adding or changing things later. It will always be for the good of the story. Now I render the version of “The Journey”—my animatic—to take into animation.

## Watch how the finished animatic brings the story to life

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L16 · 08:12–09:26

The voice-over opens with a fragment the Spanish captions render as “Cuéntale el viento” (literally, “Tell it the wind”), followed by “de un lugar desconocido” (“from a place unknown”). I read the two fragments together as describing a wind from an unknown place. It is a place unlike anything you have seen before, where its colors, scents, and inhabitants are foreign to your reality. But you must not be afraid to go there and leave your safety behind, because discovering that new horizon of change transforms you and helps you discover that the journey really is about to begin.

Here I have a version that I really like, and I am pleased with how it works. I am happy with the way it brings my vision to life and with my version of the text we have worked on throughout the course. Perhaps most important, this storytelling feels completely mine. The same should be true of your version: it should feel like your own. You are the only person who can tell it this way, and you should feel that when you see the result. My story has just come to life. I hope yours does too. This is where I finish our work. The material can eventually be handed to a design and animation team to start work on an animated piece.

## A well-told story can take many visual forms

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L16 · 09:26–10:20

For this, I asked Sebastián, a designer at SMOG, to make a visual proposal. He gave me this direction, and it looks good. Perhaps the most interesting part of this exercise, and one of the goals of the course, is that having a well-structured, well-told story lets us interpret it in any visual language. We could use 3D characters, vector characters, traditional animation, or even live action. That is another subject, though, because in a way our story has gained a life of its own.

This was my personal approach to the project. Now I invite you to play with all the tools we have learned during the course as you work on your final project.
