## Storyboarding as a rapid-prototyping tool

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L09 · 00:00–00:48

At the beginning of this unit, we will start putting our story together, beginning with a fundamental process: the storyboard (*storyboard*). We will learn some of the tools and techniques for building our story on the foundation we developed in the previous units. The storyboard is the first major tool for visualizing a story. I know that if you are taking this course, you probably know what a storyboard is, but do not skip this lesson: I want to show you how to use it as a rapid-prototyping tool (*prototipado rápido*), rather than only as an illustrated visualization of the story, which is how it is normally used. First, let me give you a little context.

## Where the storyboard came from

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L09 · 00:48–02:02

The storyboard (captions: *el histórico*) was invented by an old acquaintance, Walt Disney, around the 1930s. Disney was a visionary for his time and invented this format to use as a rapid-prototyping tool. It is the simple process of drawing quick sketches, framing them and sequencing them so you can assess how a story works. It is fun to discover that the tool began in service of animation, and was later adapted and, in a way, professionalized by Hollywood into the storyboard format we know today.

One of its main exponents was another familiar name to designers, Saul Bass, famous among other things for his title sequences, especially the ones he made for Hitchcock. His work as a storyboard artist is less well known, but he was a true master of the technique. To this day, there is controversy over whether the famous shower sequence in *Psycho* was Hitchcock’s work or Saul Bass’s; Bass detailed it perfectly in the film’s storyboard. In this storyboard we can see Saul Bass include a device that was common in his work and that we will examine in the next lessons: the transition (*transición*). In the storyboard’s final column, we see the transition from the blood in the drain to the victim’s eye (captions: *ojo de la victoria*). But let us return to the point.

## Rough ideas and flexible tools

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L09 · 02:02–02:58

It does not matter here whether you know how to draw. I think that is the main difference from the polished storyboards we are used to seeing. Some are real comics, but here the point is not the drawing; it is the ideas and testing how they function in the story. The key to this technique is not to feel especially attached to the drawing you are making, so it will not hurt to discard it if it does not work. We will practise this technique in the last lesson of the unit.

For this, we can use our faithful friend the sticky note again; I have already talked about its advantages. As with brainstorming ideas to find our premise, there are many other techniques and tools for storyboarding (*storyboardear*). Another tool I use is a custom rubber stamp that I had made so I could draw ideas on loose sheets, then frame the ones I find most interesting. The idea is for you to try different tools and find the ones that suit you best. None is better than another; use whichever one makes you feel most comfortable and confident working.

## Choosing a format and keeping the storyboard modular

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L09 · 02:58–03:45

One important point is the format of our storyboard. In the past, film and television were the only formats, so people worked with standard proportions (captions: *oliver de Alba*). Today, with phone screens, VR, and platforms such as Instagram or Vine, the range of screen formats has multiplied. It is important to know the final output format so you can tell your story appropriately and take advantage of that frame.

In this lesson’s resource links, I will leave a series of storyboard templates that you can use for the final project or any other project you do later. It is important to see storyboarding as a modular game: nothing is guaranteed a fixed place in the story. Remember, it is always a prototype. Let it flow organically until the very last moment of your process.

## Reviewing the storyboard and choosing its level of detail

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L09 · 03:45–04:19

An important part of making this storyboard is to question everything. Is it engaging? Can people understand what I am doing? Are there gaps? Are there unnecessary things? Does it make sense? Is it too obvious? Is it too convoluted? Review it, look at it from a distance again and again, test it with other people, and be prepared to hear the most brutal comments about it. Remember that the storyboard lets both you and everyone else understand the story from beginning to end.

How much detail should this storyboard (captions: *este estudio*) have? The answer is a little tricky: the right amount for each specific case. The truth is that you need only as much as is necessary to explain the story.

## Using framing to guide the viewer

Anchor: 433 · U4 · L09 · 04:19–05:52

Let us talk about framing (*encuadre*). It is about giving the viewer the information they need at the right moment. Basically, it guides where the viewer should look. Let us see what the different framings (*encuadres*) are and what they are for.

We will begin with the close-up (*primer plano*). It lets us show our characters’ emotions and expressions clearly. If we move closer to the character, we get an extreme close-up (*primerísimo primer plano*), which lets us adjust the level of drama in the narration. If we move even closer, we reach the detail shot (*plano detalle*), which is useful for emphasizing a particular element in the scene.

Let us move back to the medium shot (*plano medio*). It can be composed in the two ways shown on screen, and gives us a view of the character separated from their surroundings. Next is the American shot (*plano americano*), which comes to about the character’s knees. It gives us gestural information and lets us show the character’s hands, for example in a conversation or when a couple holds hands. Its unusual name comes from cowboy films, where this framing was used to show the characters’ pistols.

Next we have the wide shot (*plano abierto*), which lets us present the character’s body language. Finally, the long shot (*plano general*) gives us context. With this basic understanding of framing, we now know how to use the screen to our advantage in a narrative. Now that we understand storyboarding as a prototyping tool, we can move on to the next tool: characters.
