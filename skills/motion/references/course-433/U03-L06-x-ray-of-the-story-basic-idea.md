## Brainstorming toward a premise

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L06 · 00:00–00:49

The first link in the chain that we are going to examine is the base idea (*idea base*), which will let us define the premise (*premisa*) of our story. This first part of the formula is the base idea. To find it, we will work with a concept you have probably heard many times: brainstorming. Here, anything goes. Any passing thought, vague idea, or creative concern you have had is welcome. The aim of brainstorming is to find this base idea, formally known as the premise.

The word *premise* is used for a symptom or conjecture that lets us infer something and draw a conclusion. That may sound a little complicated, but it is not so complicated.

## Form and function

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L06 · 00:49–01:37

A premise is the result of a fairly simple equation: the union of form (*forma*) and function (*función*). Form tells us whether we are dealing with something figurative or abstract. Function has to do with the expectation the idea must fulfill. It can be explanatory, denunciatory, promotional, or informative, among many other possibilities. The choices of form and function depend entirely on who will tell the story.

In short, as the communicator, you must define the form and function you will use with the input you receive in order to state the premise of your story. I know this may sound complex, but it is quite simple to apply and happens almost naturally. It is just a matter of practice. Let us look at some premise examples to make this clearer.

## Water scarcity and a social network

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L06 · 02:14–03:08

The second example is an informative video about water scarcity, presented by a child in Tanzania. The input is probably water shortage (captions: *es que se te agua*). The narration is given an informative function. For its form, we can use a child who sees how not having water at home affects life in Tanzania, a place with some of the greatest water scarcity on the planet.

The third example uses a real case: an explanatory video about how a social network works, as if it were a mechanism. The input is to show how a social network functions. The tone (*tono*) is explanatory, and we use abstraction to make an analogy with a mechanism. Notice that the premise does not have to specify the animation technique or graphic style. Its job is to be clear enough for us to understand what we will discuss, but ambiguous enough not to tell us what we will find.

## Writing and sketching ideas

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L06 · 03:08–03:50

There are many exercises for building a premise; I will show you how I work at this point. First, I write ideas—or rather, I voice ideas. This means throwing out loose ideas in different formats so we can build something later. I use my faithful friend, the Post-it—or rather, my faithful friends, the Post-its—because I use a lot of them to make these first narrative sketches. You can use a notebook or an app; there are many ways, and each person develops their own method. I especially like Post-its because they are an easy tool for prototyping: they are easy to visualize, and easy to add, remove, or replace ideas with. They are very versatile.

## Breaking down the journey voice-over

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L06 · 03:50–04:58

Now we will find the premise for our journey project. We take the voice-over (*voiceover*) and, first and most importantly, analyze it carefully and break it down to see what it is made of. It contains concepts and images that we can use to sketch and shape something. In this case, I highlighted the words that I think could be visualized as ideas.

I look at these ideas on separate Post-its. Since the voice-over talks about a journey and says “the legend,” I start adding elements. The legend might come from a book, or from someone telling a story around a campfire. The journey could be a literal trip by a backpacker, a spiritual journey, or a specific route from one place to another for a vacation. It does not matter yet. The point is to put down every possible idea that comes up, whether or not it seems interesting. First we find possible paths to take; these are ingredients we can work with, like our pantry.

## Cleaning and grouping the notes

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L06 · 04:58–05:19

Once all the loose ideas are on the table, the first step is to clean them up. We select the most interesting ones, remove those that do not seem to add anything, and sometimes combine ideas that are similar. Then we begin to classify ideas that belong to similar categories and form groups of related ideas. At this point, the material starts to take shape.

## Comparing paths and choosing one

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L06 · 05:19–06:48

I now have two possible paths that share some points. On the left side of the screen is a more outdoor journey: camping, a character who grows a beard, a campfire where a story is told, climbing, the desert, the highway, and other parts of that outdoor life and those kinds of trips. On the right is a journey that may be more about migration, with birds flying; that can also work as an analogy for life. For now, I am working with those concepts: birds on one side and outdoor life on the other. In the middle I have left the ideas that could belong to either path.

We can make analogies, for example, using climate to talk about time, the eye to talk about color, or parent and child relationships, which could work with either birds or the outdoor theme. These ideas might fit both paths. At this point we have one of many decisions to make in building a good story: we must choose one of the paths. Both could be interesting, but I advise you to follow your instinct and choose the one you think you will enjoy more. You could also develop a premise for both ideas and then decide; the method is up to you.

For this project I will lean toward the more personal path. I like camping and outdoor trips with my children. If there is a personal element in what I tell, the story might become more interesting, and I may be able to contribute more to it. So I will take that path.

## Ordering the selected ideas

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L06 · 06:48–07:51

I select my base idea and arrange it in an almost chronological order of what we might talk about. The parents and children are the protagonists. Perhaps the story takes place at a campsite, where a campfire can show the story. We might see abstract landscapes and a parallel world. We can use the eye as an analogy for color, and silhouettes for people we do not know. Free fall can stand for taking a leap. A growing beard can mark the passage of time and perhaps a character's evolution and growth, and how that changes your life. These are things the script mentions in one way or another.

I like approaching it this way: a more testimonial story of a father telling his children, through metaphors, how important a journey is, both physically and internally. I have just sketched this and hit on the basis of my premise without realizing it; I have put it down, so now I will shape it into a complete statement.

## Stating and testing the premise

Anchor: 433 · U3 · L06 · 07:51–08:34

The premise might be: the testimony of a father who tells his children how important it is to discover new places, represented abstractly through a campfire. I think this is a good premise to work with because it is clear about the context and the kinds of characters we will work with, but ambiguous about what it could look like. I think that balance between clarity and ambiguity is the key to a premise.

The more novel the premise is, the more appealing the result will be. But being novel does not mean being convoluted. Now that we know how to define our premise, we can move on to the second stage of the equation: tone.
