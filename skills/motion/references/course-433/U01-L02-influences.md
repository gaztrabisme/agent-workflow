## Influences and Terry Gilliam

Anchor: 433 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–01:52

Now I’m going to show you the references that have influenced me. They do not necessarily have to do with the discipline of design; they can come from many places. Influence, as a concept, is the effect one thing produces on another. In the creative field, we could say it is each of the ingredients that have shaped our vision as professionals, whether consciously or unconsciously. These influences may be recent or may even go back to childhood.

In my case, one of my earliest influences was, and still is, Terry Gilliam. A designer by profession, he is known as the animator and art director of the comedy collective Monty Python. His visual and narrative style has been a major influence on many design studios. Gilliam has the particular distinction of being a designer who became a film director, which gives his films a unique point of view. Examples include *Brazil*, *12 Monkeys*, *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas*, and his troubled vision of *Don Quixote de la Mancha* (captions: *tramitada visión del Quijote de la Mancha*).

But there is one film in particular that marked a before and after for me: *The Adventures of Baron Munchausen* (captions: *Verón Munchausen*). Gilliam once called it the triumph of fantasy. The film is full of fantastic vignettes where we can recognize the visual heritage of his early work: giant heads, extravagant characters, and even the reworking of classic icons, such as Venus, played by Uma Thurman. In my view, the film’s great landmark is a particular character who appears in a couple of sequences: Death.

I remember seeing it and being stunned. I was very young, and it was the first time I had seen such a striking representation of death. It was surreal, macabre, imposing. I remember wondering how it had been made: was it animation, or was it a puppet? This winged figure with a scythe, flying through the darkness, was truly striking.

## Charles and Ray Eames: *Powers of Ten*

Anchor: 433 · U1 · L02 · 01:52–03:52

Later, when I was older and in my first years studying design, I learned about this extraordinary pair of designers, Charles and Ray Eames (captions: *los IMSS, Charles y Ray*). They developed a series of iconic design products, from chairs and decorative objects to houses. They also made numerous audiovisual pieces. One in particular is called *Powers of Ten* (captions: *Power of Ten; Potencia de Día*).

The film shows the relative scale of the universe through powers of ten. It starts with a couple having a picnic beside a lake in Chicago. The camera begins to move away from them, and every ten seconds we see our starting point become ten times farther away. The journey progresses until we are beyond our atmosphere. We see our galaxy, and even see our galaxy become a white dot among many other galaxies. Eventually the camera stops, and you think that is where the experience ends.

I remember watching this video at university and saying, “Wow! This is incredible.” With this video, I discovered that animation was a communication tool without comparison. But what is interesting is that just when you think the sequence of powers of ten has ended, the camera begins to return. It starts a journey in the opposite direction from the one we took at first. We travel back to the couple in the park in Chicago, having their picnic.

But once the camera reaches them, it does not stop. It begins to plunge into the main character. We repeat the same dynamic that took us outside our planet, but now it takes us inside a human being. Our journey ends inside a proton of a carbon atom in a DNA molecule. What the Eameses achieved with this video is truly astonishing, especially considering the period in which it was made. It is a masterful work of information design (*diseño de información*).

## *Rick and Morty*: narrative across episodes

Anchor: 433 · U1 · L02 · 03:52–05:25

A somewhat more contemporary reference and influence is *Rick and Morty* (captions: *Ricky Morty*). You have probably seen it, and if you have not, it is a series you should watch. It has a reputation for being irreverent, funny, chaotic, even grotesque. Beyond that, it uses narrative very intelligently: every detail matters and is placed precisely. It is full of second readings and small clues that build up into a larger narrative across the series.

This particular episode, at first glance, is a recognizable homage to *Mad Max* and *Mad Max Beyond Thunderdome*. But the episode’s story is really about the way Morty and his sister Summer face their parents’ separation. It is a wonderful analogy for what goes on inside a teenager facing a situation of this magnitude.

The series’ impeccable narrative style is the work of Dan Harmon (captions: *Dan Horman*), one of its co-creators. He was not comfortable with the hero’s journey, the structure commonly used in animated series and even in storybooks. It was created by Joseph Campbell and can be found in his book *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*. Harmon adapts it, creating a new narrative structure based on quadrants that organizes a circular narrative and makes it more versatile than Campbell’s. In this unit’s links you can find studies of Dan Harmon’s structure. It has allowed him to create memorable episodes such as this one, “Pickle Rick,” which earned the series an Emmy.

## Edgar Wright: montage in *Hot Fuzz*

Anchor: 433 · U1 · L02 · 05:25–06:26

Edgar Wright, a British film director, is a true master of audiovisual storytelling. His timing for delivering messages is impressive. He knows how to make the most of montage (*montaje*) and editing as narrative tools. Here we see the opening sequence of his film *Hot Fuzz* (captions: *Hot Fuse*). In a few seconds, through a very dynamic montage of short scenes, it shows us how the main character feels about being transferred from the big city, where he works as chief of police, to his new role as a police officer in a small town.

As we will see later in this course, managing time is key to telling a good story, and Edgar Wright knows how to do that. Short vignettes show us context, feeling, the use of humor and the use of sound. They are small pieces of a small puzzle that form part of a much larger piece: the film.

Along with his mastery of montage, the director lets himself play with narrative. He feels comfortable. Edgar Wright (captions: *Edward Wright*) likes telling stories, and you can see that when you watch sequences like the following one from his film *Shaun of the Dead* (captions: *Chown of the Dead*).

## Edgar Wright: implied vignettes in *Shaun of the Dead*

Anchor: 433 · U1 · L02 · 06:26–07:32

“What should we do tomorrow?” “Keep drinking.” “I have a wonderful first time.” “A bit of the king’s head, a bit of the princess.” “It’s here, so that… bam!” “What about that girl with beans? No.”

What is interesting about this sequence compared with the one we saw earlier from *Hot Fuzz*? There are really interesting shots, dynamic editing, and so on. This one, however, is only a dialogue between two characters, almost a monologue by Nick Frost’s character.

In truth, the sequences are not so different. This one also has a series of vignettes, told very quickly and in short cuts. The thing is, we do not see them, but the character is describing them. And those vignettes are not random or meaningless within the film. Quite the opposite: they are small spoilers for what we will see in the next hour and a half of the movie. Edgar Wright allows himself to tell us what his film will be about in a small sequence that seems inconsequential, but in the final context has a little more importance to the story.

## *Inside Out*: teaching through story

Anchor: 433 · U1 · L02 · 07:32–08:50

Pixar, I know: somewhat obvious, almost a cliché. Practically all Pixar films have something worth discussing, and in the world of animation it is probably the most referenced studio. But I want to pause and talk about this particular film, *Inside Out*, which is a major reference for me in how it delivers information.

I do not want to dwell on the quality of the animation, or even on Pixar’s memorable storytelling. I want to focus on the fact that this plot is extremely complex to understand, both for a child and even for an adult. But the film gives us its first five minutes to explain clearly how what we are about to see works. It shows us what a memory is and how it is stored. It shows us how emotions live alongside those memories. It lays out the ground rules and gives us the coordinates to understand the story organically.

It is probably the best animated instructional film I have seen. It is an excellent example, a very intelligent way to disguise and camouflage information design inside the context of an animated narrative.

## Johnny Kelly and Chipotle

Anchor: 433 · U1 · L02 · 08:50–09:41

Within the field of motion design (captions: *Moton Design*), one of my current references is Johnny Kelly, a British director and designer. What I admire most is his talent for telling stories with mixed techniques, where the analog and the digital coexist in service of the story.

One of his best-known projects is this animated short that he made a few years ago for the Chipotle brand. Through stop motion (captions: *top motion*) presented in a time-lapse format, with delicate art direction and a soundtrack that is a cover of a well-known Coldplay song, the director tells us a moving story that invites us to question our relationship with what we eat.

## Chris Curtis and bold screen images

Anchor: 433 · U1 · L02 · 09:41–10:26

Chris Curtis (captions: *Chris Covrish*), also British, is a director at Edwin. What appeals to me most is how daringly he presents images on screen. His work organically combines real images with saturated-color graphics in spaces that seem to have no limits. That is what I admire most about him. I feel that he has no fear when he faces the screen. There is a confidence in the way he presents his images that gives his audiovisual narrative a unique character.

It seems that the British are the people who influence me most in my professional life. I had not realized that until now, because my next reference is Studio AKA.

## Studio AKA and Oliver Jeffers

Anchor: 433 · U1 · L02 · 10:26–11:28

Studio AKA is an animation studio whose strength is telling memorable stories, regardless of the technique used. Each project is different from the next. The design and animation styles differ in every piece, but each is carefully made and distinct.

They are the studio behind an animated version of the last reference I want to present: Oliver Jeffers. I discovered this children’s storybook author after I had children. In each of his stories, this artist has introduced me to astonishing characters who live naturally alongside the absurd, humor, nostalgia, and even sadness in some of the books. He is truly a versatile artist and a wonderful storyteller. He also has a side as a great painter; he has wonderful oil paintings. I invite you to look at his work beyond the children’s world (captions: *Maya del mundo infantil*). What he does is truly remarkable.
