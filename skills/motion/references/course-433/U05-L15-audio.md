## Treat sound as part of the story from the start

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L15 · 00:00–01:29

This lesson is about sound, a stage people often leave until the end of the process, even though it is important to consider from the beginning. So far we have only touched on audio indirectly. It is common to think of audio as the final stage of an audiovisual piece: once the piece is finished, add music and sound effects, and that is it. But that is not how it should be. We should treat audio as another narrative tool, one that starts to play an important role while we are working at this stage.

For the final course exercise, we have a voice-over (*voiceover*) that we have been using as a script. This voice will speak to the viewer, so we need to consider several factors that will be decisive in delivering the message appropriately. Will the voice be male or female? We should be clear about its intonation and pauses. We can test all of this in the animatic, since, as I said earlier, we are constantly prototyping.

Music and sound effects (*sonorización*), or foley, should also be sketched at this stage for the same reason: they are narrative tools, not just accompaniment. Music can set the overall tone—comedy, melancholy, suspense, and so on—and help create an atmosphere.

## Hear how music changes the same forest

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L15 · 01:29–03:25

To demonstrate, we will do a simple exercise. Starting with this image of a forest, we will add a layer of music. Let’s see how the forest feels with part of the soundtrack from the video game *Ori and the Blind Forest*.

The forest quickly fills with a kind of magic. It feels a little like *The Lord of the Rings*, a little like *Robin Hood*. There is a sense of adventure and some fantasy. Now let’s see what happens if we radically replace the audio with part of the soundtrack from *The Fog* by John Carpenter. The forest loses the degree of fantasy and magic that the earlier soundtrack gave it. Immediately it feels completely eerie and mysterious, as if something bad is going to happen.

## Use music to set the rhythm of animation

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L15 · 03:25–04:22

It is true that music can be added once the edit is finished. But music can also give us a base for the animation’s rhythm. It can provide a beat that determines the duration of shots and let us make animated choreography in time with the audio. Music videos are the clearest example of music as a rhythmic base for editing animation, so it is useful to start testing how we will use it at this stage.

Next, I show a good example of animation moving to a musical beat. The clip is credited to Arctic Monkeys for “Do I Wanna Know?” and directed by David Wilson. While it plays, the captions pick up a few fragments: “Thank you!”, a garbled sung phrase transcribed as “Rezal all my cleaned ruby / Sign,” “Cos I always do!”, and several more “Thank you!” lines, including “Thank you for watching the video!”

## Plan sound effects as a narrative tool

Anchor: 433 · U5 · L15 · 04:22–05:09

Sound effects, also known as foley, are named in tribute to Jack Foley, who created the technique. They basically mean adding sounds over the animation. They can be realistic sounds or effects that strengthen actions on screen. Once again, this is something people may leave to the final stage—the last link in the animation production chain—but it is an important narrative tool that helps strengthen what we want to tell.

That is why it is important to consider now what kinds of sounds we want to use and when. The animatic may already include sound notes or rough sound mock-ups to guide later work and give instructions to the person who will handle the audio.
