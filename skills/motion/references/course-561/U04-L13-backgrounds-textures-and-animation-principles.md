## Building star and grain effects

Anchor: 561 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–02:47

We will animate backgrounds and textures and create simple repetitions to add to our final video. I will show you a couple of effects that I am going to add to my infographic.

In After Effects, create a new document and a precomposition (*precomposición*) at the same size as the one you are working on. We will start with the stars. Create a white solid (*sólido*) by right-clicking and choosing New Solid. Search for “star”; the built-in effect appears under Simulation, and those will be our stars. I will make many small stars. By default, as you can see, they animate very quickly, so the idea is to lower their speed almost to the minimum so the effect stays subtle. You can also duplicate the effect, make the particles larger, expand them a little, and perhaps slow this set down too. Then we have two sets, one with small stars and one with larger ones, which gives the design more depth.

Another effect I will add is grain (*grano*). Create a new black solid, choose OK, and search for Add Grain under Noise & Grain. After adding it, the effect shows a preview of how it will look in the end. Switch the view so it shows the whole result. If we press Play, we can see it animate. This effect is very heavy, so I generally add it at the end of the whole animation. As we saw in the texturing section, it applies over all the dark areas in the design. To apply it over white, select this layer, duplicate it with Control+D, find Invert among the effects and apply it. Now the grain will cover the white areas, or the light colors, in the design. We will do this at the end, once everything is animated, and I will show you how to apply it then.

## Six principles of animation

Anchor: 561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54

Let us look at a few tricks to add to your animations. In this video we will look at six principles of animation. There are twelve in all, but these are the most important for the kind of animation we are making today. The principles come from the years when Disney made drawings frame by frame; they helped make its animations feel more real and distinctive. If you watch the animations and read the names, you can see what each one is about. These details add a lot to an animation, but they are not something to use all the time. It depends on the animation you are making. If we are dealing with a serious subject, we cannot apply stretch and squash (*estirar y encoger*), because it gives the animation a cartoon tone.

Stretch and squash is about something moving or deforming. The white square is boring: it simply goes up and down, and nothing happens. The principle applied to the orange square gives the element more mass and weight. Anticipation (*anticipación*) is the action of gathering momentum. The orange square takes a small step backward before it leaves; compared with the white square, you can see what a difference that makes. Secondary action (*acción secundaria*) means adding another element to the animation, which helps give the main animation more prominence.

Timing (*timing*) is about the mass of an element. When we push something heavy, it is hard at first; once it picks up speed, it becomes easier. The opposite happens with the white square. This is true in most of the examples: compared with the ones that use the animation principles, the plain examples look boring, without dynamism or life. The next principle is what the captions call complementary and supplementary action (*acción complementaria y suplementaria*): it is the action of deforming the element. Think of the element as gelatin: as it leaves, the top part takes a little longer, then overshoots when it catches up. It is a little bounce, like gelatin.

Finally, accelerating and decelerating (*acelerar y desacelerar*) is one of the most important principles, because these are the animation graphs we will use later in After Effects. We very rarely use animations that go from one side to the other with no speed or acceleration, as the white rectangle does. That is simply boring; there is no point animating something like that. Adding speed and dynamism helps much more. You can research the twelve principles, but the others are more suited to cartoons. Keep this animation in mind when you animate your infographics. It takes a little more time, but if you want to stand out, you have to sit down and do it, because it adds a lot to the video. Now we will add fine details to our animation.
