## Set up an inspiration search

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L12 · 00:00–00:34

To create a series of visually powerful animations, we’ll look at a workflow used in professional settings. We’ll make a Pinterest board, analyze it, and then go into Illustrator to begin an aesthetic search. I’m using Illustrator because it is a good tool for the geometric, cool designs we’ll animate. Let’s get to work in Illustrator.

## Search Pinterest for visual references

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L12 · 00:34–01:48

Here we’ll look at the design process so that the final piece and its individual parts look good. Pinterest is where we’ll make the inspiration panel, or mood board (*panel de inspiración*). I’ll recreate the process for anyone who has never used it; if you already know it, you can skip this part.

Go to Pinterest and type words related to the kind of design or style you want, just as you would when searching Google. I’ll enter “Geometrics Design” and see what comes up. Some of the results are a little monochrome. The trick is to change the search: I’ll remove “Design” and enter “Geometries,” then add “Bauhaus.” Bauhaus is a design school known for using geometric shapes, so I expect to find what I’m looking for.

When I click one image, the related images belong to that same visual world. That is how I find references: I begin with a search, and once I get close to what I want, I keep clicking related images to explore that visual direction further.

## Compare organic and synthetic forms

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L12 · 05:17–05:54

The next opposition is between organic and synthetic elements. On the left, the finish is organic and more natural: the edges are irregular, and the fill has a textured, imperfect look. On the right, the design is synthetic. Everything is sharply defined and the circle is almost perfect. It looks more computer-generated, colder, and farther from nature, but it is also more orderly. We might decide here that we want something simple and synthetic; that would give us two attributes for our design.

## Compare order and disorder

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L12 · 05:54–06:39

The next duality is order against disorder. This example is symmetrical: the two sides align around the center, and many elements repeat exactly on both sides. Everything is organized and closely aligned. That amount of order may or may not work for us. The opposite is disorder, which can be more expressive and wilder. It can make the same composition look as if it is moving, creating a visual sense of movement. One option feels more boring and the other more expressive; the choice depends on the kind of design and feeling we want. By analyzing the images, we now have three attributes to consider.

## Compare monochrome and polychrome color

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L12 · 06:39–07:29

The list of dualities could be endless; we can find or make as many as we like. I’ll make color the final example: monochrome or polychrome. One option can use many colors; the example here does not use many, but we could use as many as we want. The other is only black and white, so it is monochrome. We can go in either direction. I’ll leave this document available to you.

You can also design from scratch. If you are a natural designer, you can start with a concept and build your visual system from there. For those of us who are less naturally inclined to design, working with references is useful, as long as we analyze them and find our own direction, using this system of dualities or another one. This helps reduce the time it takes to produce designs. And time is money, friends; you know that.

## Choose attributes to anchor the design

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L12 · 07:29–08:33

Now I’ll write down the visual attributes I want for my animation library. Between geometry and organic form, I choose geometric shapes. Between monochrome and polychrome, I choose color. I’ll make a palette of three or four colors, five at most. I also need to choose between a simple, minimal direction and a complex one. I will not settle this completely because the first exercises will be simple and the last ones more complex. As an overall aesthetic, though, I’ll say that the compositions should lean toward complexity.

You can make your attribute map (*mapa de atributos*) as extensive as you like, but at least three or four attributes can give you a clear direction. This will be the anchor (*ancla*) for your design.

## Save a separate color palette file

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L12 · 11:50–12:41

The last thing is to make a separate Illustrator document and put our colors in it. First, we need to check an important setting in File: make sure the color mode (*modo de color*) is RGB. We set it that way, paste in our colors, and speed things up for a moment. Then we save the Illustrator file. This is our palette file. Later we will import it into After Effects and use the eyedropper (*cuentagotas*) to pick colors from the palette for our designs. This visual design library will inform the compositions, colors, and overall look and feel of the animations.
