## Choose a character to guide the movement

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L11 · 00:00–01:21

There are many ways to approach this kind of work. We’ll choose a method that is both effective and fun: a character whose body language (*expresión corporal*) and personality will always guide how we animate each exercise. It could be Audrey Hepburn, Jim Carrey, your brother-in-law, or your dog. Choose whoever inspires you most. In my case, I’ll choose someone—but you’ll see that in the video.


The idea is to base a coherent animation style on body language. Whose body language? That of a character you choose because they inspire you or give you a way to copy how they move. Pick someone whose manner is distinctive to you.

Here we see Cary Grant moving. He is one of my idols. John Wayne is the quintessential American cowboy, about to punch a cow. We also have the famous ASIMO robot. It is very old-school now; today’s robots do much more, but at the time it was remarkable. Audrey Hepburn shows the elegance she was known for, and Jim Carrey has extraordinary expressiveness.

The first step is to define how each character moves through attributes. Cary Grant, for me, is elegant and simple, so I would try to carry those qualities into every animation and exercise. John Wayne is direct and dry, with authority, decisiveness, and leadership; you can see that he hits whoever gets in his way.

The robot is not a person, but it is a character. It is effective and, above all, artificial; its movement could hardly have less flow. Audrey Hepburn is elegant and sophisticated. Jim Carrey, specifically in The Mask, is always histrionic, and for that character the attributes are histrionic, striking, extravagant, and surprising. Here he is acting crazy.

## Five position-animation examples

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L11 · 02:53–04:31

Next we’ll look at a short position-animation (*animación de posición*) example for each character. First, the great Cary Grant (captions: *saqueo saqueo gran*). The curves suggest elegance and softness as he moves from one place to another almost as if levitating. He stops very gently, which should give us the elegance and simplicity we described.

John Wayne arrives and hits you. He does not need to stop especially smoothly: he goes from one place to another, and there he is. The robot’s animation is dry and artificial. It has no easing (*suavizado*), meaning no smooth acceleration or deceleration. It moves from one place to another mechanically, but very efficiently.

For Audrey Hepburn, I introduce a slight curve to bring out her elegance and sophistication. Instead of moving in a straight line, she follows a small arc, which emphasizes her elegance. With Jim Carrey, his histrionics, madness, and strong expressiveness call for a very large, exaggerated bounce (*rebote*) as he travels from one side to the other. The animation feels surprising and draws attention, which is exactly what it should do.

## Select your own character

Anchor: 503 · U3 · L11 · 04:31–06:02

That is the idea: choose a character to guide you and serve as a reference for all your animations, so they share a style. I chose well-known characters to make the examples universal and easy to understand. Your character can come from a film or be someone you know, such as your brother-in-law or your dog. It is up to you. The important thing is to choose someone with distinctive attributes that can guide all your animations.

Now for the character I chose: drum roll—Dirty Harry (captions: *Redoblet Amor* and *Don't Clean His Wood*). He is the quintessential cocky tough guy, a variation on John Wayne’s tough-guy type. I chose him because, besides being very tough, he has an extra quality: cockiness. Most tough guys are cocky, but this one is the cockiest of them all. For me, he also has an authoritarian and efficient side. This police officer broke all the rules, but in the end he solved the cases.

The character is Clint Eastwood, chewing gum, and he will guide my whole movement system. We have now chosen our character and analyzed the attributes that will let us animate everything consistently. In the next lesson we’ll do a little design before we start animating.
