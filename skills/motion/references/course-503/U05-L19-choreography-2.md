## Preparing the shapes and their masks

Anchor: 503 · U5 · L19 · 01:50–05:10

I am in After Effects, with the scene roughly laid out. I have not finished setting it up because I want you to see a couple of things. There is a circle, which I will hide for a moment, and two rectangles. To make the rectangles, I put the anchor point (*punto de anclaje*) at one end and rotate the layer. Here I have rotated it 45 degrees, duplicated it, and used the layer properties to adjust it. If I add 180 degrees, it faces the other way. I undo that with Command or Control+Z. That is a simple way to make the rectangles.

Now I will make masks (*máscaras*). The circle here is made of two colours. This is the small step up in our After Effects work: we want to split the circle in two, with one rectangle colouring one side and the other rectangle colouring the other side. To do that, I duplicate a rectangle, keep the same name, and place the copy above it so that it is visible. I apply an effect so it shows only in the area defined by another layer.

If the Effects and Presets window is not visible, open it from Window > Effects and Presets. In the search field, type “matte” and choose the Channel effect called “Set Matte” (*mate*). Apply it to the rectangle. When a layer has an effect selected, its controls normally appear in the upper-left panel, in a tab beside the Project panel. The important control here is “Take Matte From Layer”: we need to tell it which layer will act as the mask.

Let’s look at the design again. The two semicircles use different colours so that they contrast with the rectangles behind them. The duplicated rectangle is grey at the moment, so I change its colour to yellow in the colour palette. Then, in “Take Matte From Layer,” I choose the circle. The circle now acts as a mask for the rectangle, giving us a semicircle. If we moved the circle, which is acting as the mask, we could make some very nice animations with it. To recap, we have two rectangles and a circle. I duplicated one rectangle, applied Set Matte and told it to take its matte from the circle. I will do the same with the other rectangle.

I have done it. I hope it was as easy for you as it was for me. If you get lost or something does not work, go back in the video and try it again in After Effects. I recommend not continuing until you understand each part and can make it work. Now we are ready to animate the scene.

## Rotating the composition with a null object (*objeto nulo*)

Anchor: 503 · U5 · L19 · 05:10–07:40

I will create a null object, as we saw in the lesson on layers. I have a small habit: I like to put its anchor point in the centre. With the layer selected, I double-click here while holding Control, and it places the anchor point in the middle. I find the Align panel and centre it horizontally and vertically, make it larger, and can change the null object’s layer colour so it contrasts with the background and is easier to work with.

First I rename it “Animation Rotation.” It is useful to give layers descriptive names, so that I or a colleague continuing the project can tell what each layer does. I select the layers I want the null object to control, drag the pick whip, and parent or link them to this property. I press W to test that it works. It does, so I can start animating.

I press R to show the null object’s Rotation and set a keyframe (*fotograma clave*). I test a negative rotation, perhaps 90 degrees. I want the composition to stay very geometric and neatly aligned at the corners. That could work. Now I adjust the curves. Remember your character; I know I keep repeating it, but otherwise we will make any old animation, which is not what we want. I use the pen tool to add another keyframe and slow the ending a little. I pull the handle and preview it. Perhaps the start should be faster. I give the beginning a little more reluctance, then preview again. I like it. To speed up the whole animation, I hold Alt and move the keyframes closer together.

## Improvising the movement and shaping a loop

Anchor: 503 · U5 · L19 · 07:40–10:45

After making the plan, this is where we can start improvising. Work in whichever way you like. Since this is a project for enjoyment, I am going to improvise and see what options I have. For a commercial project, of course, you should follow the plan presented to the client more or less exactly. Otherwise you will look like a wild artist, which will not make a good impression.

I will start moving the elements. The circle could disappear or settle at an interesting point in the composition. We can try things, undo with Command or Control+Z, and go back. I turn snapping back on. We could also play with the rectangles that create the hemispheres. That could make an interesting image; I think I will use it. I set a Position keyframe for this rectangle. When the whole composition finishes rotating, the rectangle will move to this position.

As always, I will shape the curves, keeping in mind that we need the “Clint Eastwood” character (captions: *Clean Eastwood*): reluctance at the start, then a strong arrival. Be careful to select X and Y together by clicking and dragging. I give it a little more reluctance at the beginning and preview what I am doing. I set the preview range with B and N so we can watch the whole movement: it rotates, then the shape settles here.

I add a keyframe for the next part and move it along. Look at the curve: if it crosses below the value, it will bounce. I exaggerate it so you can see how ugly that bounce is. We do not want it to go below the value. I adjust it, give the preview area more room so the animation can settle, and watch again. The curve still needs a small correction. Now it is right: it rotates and drops.

Next we need a way to finish the cycle and make a loop. I will not make a perfect loop here. I want the composition to jump a little as it rotates and slows down, so the ending is not exactly like the beginning and has more interest. You can make a perfect loop or do whatever you like. You know how this works now: set keyframes and adjust the curves carefully. I will move ahead and show you the result.

### Key points (derived)

- A storyboard can guide the animation while still leaving room to improvise when the work is exploratory.
- Set Matte uses one layer as a mask for another; a circle can reveal a rectangle only inside its shape.
- A null object can control several linked layers together, and descriptive layer names make the setup easier to follow.
- Shape the timing to fit the animation’s character. A loop can be smooth and exact or use a deliberate jump between forms.
