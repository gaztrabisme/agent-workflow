## Cut on movement for a fluid transition

Anchor: 503 · U6 · L22 · 01:26–02:49

503 · U06 · L22 · 01:26–02:49

The first idea is to edit, preferably, on movement. I have this animation we made earlier of the swipe, or drag. I'll make a cut when the swipe moves upward and look for another animation with a similar movement so the two will connect well. Here I'll make a cut and take this choreography we made together. I remember a moment when the square moves upward, so I'll make another cut here and see how it fits. The direction isn't exactly the same, but one movement connects to the next. Let's preview it. It looks interesting. It may not be the best combination from everything we have here, but you understand the idea: cut on movement so the cut works better and the edit flows more smoothly.

After Effects doesn't have scissors for cutting, but it has a useful keyboard shortcut: Control or Command+Shift+D. It makes a cut and divides the layer in two, the equivalent of scissors in editing programs such as Final Cut or Adobe Premiere.

The second tip is to know your material—the animations you've made—and choose only the best for the mini-reel: the strongest or most interesting work. ## Remap animation time

Anchor: 503 · U6 · L22 · 03:19–05:05

503 · U06 · L22 · 03:19–05:05

The third tip is to edit the timing of your animations. That may sound a little strange, so let's look at it practically; open After Effects and follow along step by step. Take the position-with-stretch animation. The movement simply goes to one side, rising diagonally, and that's it. In the mini-reel [Spanish captions: “mini rig”], though, I worked on the animation's internal movement so it rises and then falls. That creates an interesting loop, so the animation never stops.

To do it, right-click the layer, open the menu, and apply Time Remapping (*remapeo de tiempo*) to the layer. It creates a keyframe (*fotograma clave*) at the beginning of the composition and another at the end. These keyframes represent the start and end of the animation. If I separate them, I slow the animation down; if I bring them together, I speed it up.

I'll identify where the animation is in the composition, from this point to this point, make a keyframe here and delete the last keyframe. Then I'll copy the keyframe from the beginning and paste it farther along. When we preview it, the animation moves in ping-pong: it loops forward and then backward. This is an interesting way to work with the animation's internal timing.

## Make a four-tile mosaic with snapping

503 · U06 · L22 · 06:22–07:46

The next tip is to use snapping (*ajuste magnético*) and mathematical operations to assemble the compositions. Let's make something like this mosaic. I have an empty composition in the Instagram format, 1080 by 1080. I'll drag in the composition with the animation. For example, if we wanted to make a four-tile mosaic [Spanish captions: “4×4”], we'd need four compositions at 50%. I'll set the scale to 50%. First, I'll place the anchor point (*punto de anclaje*) at the upper left; with snapping on, it goes right there. If I set Position to 0, 0, it will line up exactly with the edge of the composition. You can also use Align, which is very useful. If I duplicate this composition and move it with snapping on, it will snap into place. I'll repeat that a couple more times with Command+D, selecting and duplicating the layer, until I have a mosaic.

## Build a nine-tile grid and offset its timing

Anchor: 503 · U6 · L22 · 09:13–11:10

503 · U06 · L22 · 09:13–11:10

Let's make a mosaic of nine to see the mathematical operations. Open Scale by pressing S. To fit three across and three down, divide the scale by 3: type /3 and press Enter. The result is 33.3. With snapping on, duplicate with Command or Control+D and place each composition at the corners of the others. After Effects assumes that the part of the layer you're dragging from is the part you want to align with another element. To align the bottom, start from the bottom; if you start from the top, it won't snap to the part you want. Then duplicate again.

You can also desynchronize the different compositions or animations. Add a delay (*retraso*) between the different animations to make the composition dynamic. Remember to use the math operation to divide the scale for three elements across and down, or divide by two for two elements; you can also simply set each to 50%. Keep snapping on to place the compositions precisely in the final frame.

## Match animation to the music

Anchor: 503 · U6 · L22 · 12:42–14:53

503 · U06 · L22 · 12:42–14:53

As we have briefly discussed adding audio to the mini-reel, let's look at a couple of tips for working with audio in After Effects. Here I have an audio file, a track I imported into the Project window just like any other item—a video, an Illustrator file or anything else. This one is an MP3. I'll drag it into the timeline.

The first thing to remember is that we want to synchronize the sound with the image. Press the spacebar to preview and look for moments where the animations can fit. You can listen to the track. Another way is to select the audio layer and press L twice to see its waveform (*forma de onda*). The waveform helps me see the beats—the percussion hits. It's very useful for lining up animation moments. While editing, you can try to match the beats with the cuts.

Another useful step is to place markers at points in the track that you've heard and want to use for a cut, to synchronize with an animation moment or something else. You can add markers from the side, or use the Shift+1 shortcut; Shift+2, Shift+3 and so on place markers at the current point in the timeline. Double-click a marker to add a description of the event or what you want to do at that point. It's simple: import the track, try to understand it by listening many times, and find the moments when you want your animation or edit to fit. Use markers to make clear what happens at each point. It then takes taste and patience to synchronize the sound and image.

## Set the reel's running time

Anchor: 503 · U6 · L22 · 14:53–16:23

503 · U06 · L22 · 14:53–16:23

The last thing about the final mini-reel is its duration. There isn't a rule. I may still adjust it a little. The reel you see here, which I've already edited, is about 20–21 seconds long. I didn't set a target; I assembled it until it felt right or until the material I had could sustain it.

When publishing on Instagram, we're talking about 2019, the maximum is 60 seconds. Since it will work in a loop, I think a duration between 20 and 40 seconds is interesting. If you want to take it to 60 seconds and the piece can hold that length, great; it's up to each of you. If you're watching this video in the future, check Instagram's specifications or search Google for the maximum video duration, because it may have changed from 60 seconds to two minutes, for example. Before deciding the length or looking for limits, search for the maximum duration allowed for an Instagram video post. My recommendation is 20 to 40 seconds.
