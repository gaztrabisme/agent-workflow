# Course 561 — Creation of animated infographics

## Method steps

1. **Choose a subject and collect source material.** Gather a small set of topic facts and visual references; the teacher uses Wikipedia for facts and Google Images or existing infographics for examples and prompts. A couple of facts can be enough; select what will become scenes and keep the eventual sequence to roughly five to ten panels. [561 · U2 · L04 · 01:25–02:33] [561 · U2 · L04 · 02:33–03:28]
2. **Sketch the story in storyboard panels.** Draw small frames, decide what each frame says and how it enters or transitions, and jot animation reminders beside the content. The teacher's example moves from a title to PayPal, Tesla, and SpaceX panels. [561 · U2 · L05 · 00:02–00:45] [561 · U2 · L05 · 00:45–01:41] [561 · U2 · L05 · 01:41–02:33] [561 · U2 · L05 · 02:33–03:50] [561 · U2 · L05 · 03:50–04:43]
3. **Curate and distribute the facts in rough scene sketches.** Decide what to keep, place facts into blocks, and work out the opening, titles, explanations, information panels, and sequence. Use sketches to test both content and layout; remove, rearrange, or add elements as needed. [561 · U2 · L06 · 00:02–00:44] [561 · U2 · L06 · 03:07–05:55] [561 · U2 · L06 · 05:55–08:50] [561 · U2 · L06 · 08:50–10:45]
4. **Make information readable and set a visual direction.** Keep a clear title for each subject, leave room for reading, place recurring information in roughly consistent locations, and use subject-related motifs and simple forms. [561 · U2 · L06 · 00:44–01:49] [561 · U2 · L06 · 01:49–03:07] [561 · U2 · L06 · 08:50–10:45]
5. **Design the plates.** Develop the storyboard into a small set of finished scenes, using a consistent system for colors, type, sizes, and strokes. Arrange facts with grids and suitable graphics such as bars, dials, or sliders; try variations and refine without rushing. [561 · U3 · L07 · 00:02–01:42] [561 · U3 · L07 · 01:43–02:44] [561 · U3 · L07 · 02:45–06:02] [561 · U3 · L07 · 06:03–07:30]
6. **Prepare and texture selected artwork.** Keep editable vector design in Illustrator, bring selected elements into Photoshop for texture, and separate layers so later animation can target individual elements. Texture only the elements that benefit from emphasis. [561 · U3 · L08 · 00:02–01:03] [561 · U3 · L09 · 00:02–00:40] [561 · U3 · L09 · 00:41–01:54]
7. **Import and organize animation assets.** Bring scene artwork into After Effects as compositions with layer sizes retained; split combined artwork into separate layers where independent motion is needed, then align and name layers. [561 · U4 · L10 · 00:02–02:13] [561 · U4 · L10 · 02:13–03:25] [561 · U4 · L10 · 03:25–04:59]
8. **Set the overall timeline and reading time.** Arrange precompositions in sequence, mark scene endpoints, and establish simple timing before adding detail. In the example, titles get about two or three seconds and dense information panels about ten seconds; not all information appears simultaneously. [561 · U4 · L11 · 00:02–01:56]
9. **Block simple motion and transitions.** Animate titles and elements with position, scale, opacity, and rotation; use easing and stagger appearances so viewers can follow the information. Preview scene changes and adjust reading time and flow. [561 · U4 · L11 · 01:56–04:43] [561 · U4 · L11 · 04:43–05:56] [561 · U4 · L11 · 05:56–07:20] [561 · U4 · L12 · 00:02–02:23] [561 · U4 · L12 · 02:23–04:59] [561 · U4 · L12 · 04:59–07:10]
10. **Add scene-specific detail and animation principles.** Work scene by scene; use simple, restrained motion for dense information, and apply principles such as anticipation, timing, secondary action, follow-through, and easing when they suit the subject. Add and tune detail while repeatedly playing back the work. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54] [561 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–03:06] [561 · U4 · L14 · 03:06–06:58] [561 · U4 · L15 · 00:00–02:19] [561 · U4 · L15 · 02:19–05:55] [561 · U4 · L16 · 02:30–05:53] [561 · U4 · L16 · 05:53–09:08] [561 · U4 · L16 · 09:08–14:30] [561 · U4 · L17 · 00:00–02:13] [561 · U4 · L17 · 02:13–05:38] [561 · U4 · L17 · 05:38–09:00]
11. **Review, render, and revise.** Check keyframes, transitions, and the rendered result; if the project is too heavy to play smoothly, render a preview and adjust after review. Render a high-quality master, compress a sharing copy, and create a short GIF when useful for social publishing. ## Principles and claims

- An infographic is described as a long strip of information combining data and graphics around one topic. [561 · U2 · L04 · 00:02–00:44]
- A couple of facts can be enough to build scenes; the teacher advises about five to ten panels rather than making the video tedious. [561 · U2 · L04 · 01:25–02:33] [561 · U2 · L04 · 02:33–03:28]
- A storyboard can include both the content of each frame and reminders about how it will animate. [561 · U2 · L05 · 00:02–00:45]
- Keep a clear title for each subject and leave room for information to be read; recurring facts should stay in roughly the same places. [561 · U2 · L06 · 00:44–01:49] [561 · U2 · L06 · 08:50–10:45]
- For legibility, use simple typography and avoid script fonts; simple icons, circles, and lines are sufficient when illustration skills are limited. [561 · U2 · L06 · 01:49–03:07]
- Visual consistency comes from limiting colors, using one typeface if possible (up to three), assigning sizes and weights by information role, and keeping line strokes consistent. [561 · U3 · L07 · 01:43–02:44]
- Grids help divide and center information; bars, small dials, and sliders can both show information and provide animation opportunities. [561 · U3 · L07 · 02:45–06:02]
- Texture is selective: not every object needs texture, and a general background texture or a texture layer over the whole design can be enough. [561 · U3 · L09 · 00:02–00:40] [561 · U3 · L09 · 02:49–04:01]
- Extra layers add detail but also add animation work; the teacher says that the extra detail can be worthwhile. [561 · U3 · L09 · 02:49–04:01]
- Animation is an extra detail that decorates an information graphic; too much movement can distract viewers from reading. [561 · U4 · L11 · 00:02–01:56] [561 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–03:06]
- Dense information should appear in a sequence rather than all at once, giving viewers time to read each item. [561 · U2 · L06 · 10:45–12:04] [561 · U4 · L12 · 02:23–04:59]
- Reusing a motion system across elements or scenes makes the animation feel more integrated and less wildly varied. [561 · U4 · L12 · 07:10–10:17] [561 · U4 · L15 · 00:00–02:19]
- Many infographic animations can be decorative rather than meaningful; the teacher suggests experimenting with simple or random movement and seeing what looks good. [561 · U4 · L12 · 07:10–10:17]
- The teacher names six principles for this kind of work: stretch and squash, anticipation, secondary action, timing, complementary/supplementary (follow-through and overlapping) action, and acceleration/deceleration. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- Stretch and squash deforms a moving element and can make it feel as if it has more mass and weight. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- Anticipation is a small preparatory move in the opposite direction before the main movement. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- Secondary action adds another element that gives prominence to the main action. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- Timing conveys mass: a heavy object is harder to push at first and moves more easily once it has gained speed. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- Follow-through/overlapping action can make an element behave like gelatin: one part lags, catches up, and overshoots with a small bounce. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- Acceleration/deceleration gives movement speed changes; the teacher says constant-speed travel from one side to another is boring. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- These principles are not for constant use; stretch and squash can give a serious subject a cartoon tone. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- Constant-speed movement is described as boring; acceleration and deceleration add speed and dynamism. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- With many on-screen elements, simple effects are enough. [561 · U4 · L17 · 05:38–09:00]
- Naming layers as work proceeds avoids a chaotic project and makes items easier to find. [561 · U4 · L10 · 03:25–04:59] [561 · U4 · L15 · 02:19–05:55] [561 · U4 · L17 · 02:13–05:38]
- The six-principle figure pairs plain white shapes with red examples and labels the six principles; the red square visibly stretches in the stretch/squash example. (frame) [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]

## Checks the teacher uses

- **Reading pace:** play dense scenes and try to read their information; tune dwell time. From a rendered artifact, measure each panel's visible duration and the interval between successive text reveals; deciding whether the text is actually comfortable to read needs judgement by eye. [561 · U4 · L11 · 00:02–01:56] [561 · U4 · L15 · 00:00–02:19]
- **Sequential appearance:** check that items do not all animate at once and that they appear in the intended order. From a rendered artifact, measure the first visible frame/time for each item and their order. [561 · U4 · L12 · 02:23–04:59] [561 · U4 · L15 · 02:19–05:55]
- **Scene endpoint and cutoff:** place a marker at the scene end, animate a little beyond it when needed, and check that motion is not cut off. From a rendered artifact, measure the final motion frame against the scene transition or composition end. [561 · U4 · L11 · 00:02–01:56] [561 · U4 · L14 · 06:58–08:25] [561 · U4 · L15 · 05:55–08:46]
- **Easing and motion quality:** inspect keyframes and speed graphs, adjust acceleration/deceleration, then play the animation. Graph shape and endpoint speed are measurable in the timeline; whether the movement feels smooth or pleasant needs judgement by eye. [561 · U4 · L11 · 01:56–04:43] [561 · U4 · L12 · 07:10–10:17] [561 · U4 · L16 · 05:53–09:08]
- **Motion restraint:** preview the information-heavy scene to see whether movement distracts from reading. There is no numerical threshold given; this needs judgement by eye. [561 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–03:06]
- **Path relation:** keep the comet tail from getting ahead of the asteroid and adjust it step by step. From a rendered artifact, compare the tail endpoint and asteroid position across frames; final plausibility needs judgement by eye. [561 · U4 · L14 · 03:06–06:58]
- **Visual principle comparisons:** the teacher contrasts plain and principle-based examples, calling the plain movement boring and the treated movement more dynamic or lifelike. The comparison requires judgement by eye. [561 · U4 · L13 · 02:47–05:54]
- **Export size and quality:** preview the GIF and try color/transparency settings; the demonstrated short GIF goes from about 9 MB to under 1 MB at roughly 600 px wide. File size, dimensions, frame count, and playback duration are measurable from the rendered artifact; whether colors break or quality looks acceptable needs judgement by eye. [561 · U4 · L18 · 03:15–05:46]
- **Preview under load:** if After Effects is too heavy to play at real speed, render and review the output, then return to adjust if needed. Rendered duration and dropped/missing frames can be measured; whether timing and transitions look right needs judgement by eye. [561 · U4 · L15 · 08:46–11:45] [561 · U4 · L17 · 09:01–10:18]

## Tool-specific steps

### Illustrator

- Design with vector artwork so it can be scaled without pixelation; use it as the source for the plates. [561 · U3 · L08 · 00:02–01:03]
- Release combined artwork to layers, save it, and import it into After Effects when separate animation targets are needed. [561 · U4 · L10 · 02:13–03:25]

### Photoshop

- Create a document at the Illustrator design’s dimensions; paste the whole composition as a dim guide, then paste selected elements as separate layers, position them against the guide, and name the layers. [561 · U3 · L08 · 01:04–01:48] [561 · U3 · L08 · 01:48–02:10]
- Add texture to selected layers with a clipped brush layer, reduced-opacity brushwork, sampled color, and layer modes; use Add Noise with Monochromatic selected and test layer modes, duplicating/inverting a noise layer to texture light areas too. [561 · U3 · L09 · 00:41–01:54] [561 · U3 · L09 · 01:55–02:48]
- Import a selected video range as video frames to layers; use Save for Web, shorten the clip, reduce dimensions/colors as needed, preview the result, then save the GIF. [561 · U4 · L18 · 03:15–05:46]

### After Effects

- Import scenes as compositions with layer sizes retained; make a main composition, set duration, and name scenes and layers. [561 · U4 · L10 · 00:02–02:13] [561 · U4 · L10 · 03:25–04:59]
- Set scene timing and markers, create position/scale/opacity/rotation keyframes, use Easy Ease and edit the speed graph, and stagger layer starts. [561 · U4 · L11 · 00:02–01:56] [561 · U4 · L11 · 01:56–04:43] [561 · U4 · L12 · 02:23–04:59]
- Convert Illustrator vectors to shape layers to animate stroke Offset or Trim Paths; use anchor-point placement and unlinked scale dimensions for directional reveals. [561 · U4 · L11 · 04:43–05:56] [561 · U4 · L16 · 02:30–05:53]
- Use nulls and parenting for groups; use masks/mattes when a stroke or glow needs to follow or reveal selected artwork. [561 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–03:06] [561 · U4 · L16 · 09:08–14:30]
- Add star and grain effects on solids; reduce star speed for subtle motion and apply grain late because it is heavy. [561 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–02:47]
- Add to Render Queue for a high-quality QuickTime/MOV master; use Media Encoder for compressed formats or HandBrake to resize/reduce file size. [561 · U4 · L18 · 00:00–01:24] [561 · U4 · L18 · 01:24–03:15]
