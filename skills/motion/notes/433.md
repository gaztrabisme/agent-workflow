# Course 433 — Storytelling for Animation and Motion Design

Working notes from the course reading copy. Anchors point to lesson ranges; `frame` marks a point read from an inspected figure rather than stated in the lesson prose. No lessons are listed as excluded or captions-only in `references/course-433/BLOCKED.md`.

## Method steps

1. **Plan before production.** Picture the whole project and its working rules. Identify the sender and receiver; the message duration and the time available to make it; and both the reaction wanted from the viewer and the maker’s own objective. (433 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:03; 433 · U2 · L04 · 01:03–01:56; 433 · U2 · L04 · 01:56–02:46; 433 · U2 · L04 · 02:46–04:12)
2. **Set the input.** Start from the brief, script, or voice-over: what will be told. Distinguish that content from the output question of how it will be told. (433 · U2 · L04 · 04:12–04:42; 433 · U3 · L05 · 00:50–01:41)
3. **Define the premise ingredients.** Decide the form (figurative or abstract) and function (for example, explanatory, denunciatory, promotional, or informative) that suit the input and communicator. Their combination provides a premise. (433 · U3 · L06 · 00:49–01:37)
4. **Break down the voice-over and brainstorm.** Read it carefully, highlight concepts and images, and put down loose ideas, possible story milestones, and visual possibilities. Sticky notes are one method; notebook or app are alternatives. (433 · U3 · L06 · 03:08–03:50; 433 · U3 · L06 · 03:50–04:58)
5. **Curate and choose an idea path.** Remove ideas that add little, combine similar ideas, group related notes, compare possible routes, and choose one by instinct or personal interest; the teacher also suggests drafting more than one premise before choosing. (433 · U3 · L06 · 04:58–05:19; 433 · U3 · L06 · 05:19–06:48)
6. **Arrange the selected ideas and state the premise.** Put ideas in a possible sequence, then write a premise that is clear about context and subject while leaving visual possibilities open. Novelty is desirable without making the premise convoluted. (433 · U3 · L06 · 06:48–07:51; 433 · U3 · L06 · 07:51–08:34)
7. **Choose and clarify tone.** Choose the story’s narrative personality; combine tone with function to open possibilities, and make the intended tone clear enough that viewers do not misread it. (433 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–00:41; 433 · U3 · L07 · 00:41–02:08; 433 · U3 · L07 · 02:08–02:55; 433 · U3 · L07 · 02:55–03:42)
8. **Choose a structure that serves the message.** Map the events and their order, identify the start, end, conflict, search, solution and turning points, and decide whether a chronological three-act or a non-linear form fits. A circular return is one option. Keep the receiver’s understanding of the message as the goal. (433 · U3 · L08 · 00:08–01:08; 433 · U3 · L08 · 01:08–02:54; 433 · U3 · L08 · 02:54–03:31; 433 · U3 · L08 · 03:31–05:03; 433 · U3 · L08 · 05:03–05:43; 433 · U3 · L08 · 05:43–06:50)
9. **Prototype the storyboard.** Use quick, discardable sketches or other easy-to-rearrange panels to test the story. Follow the voice-over order as a working sequence; this need not be the story’s chronological order. Introduce the world, protagonist and conflict, and keep earlier panels visible beside new ones so the whole sequence stays in view. (433 · U4 · L09 · 00:00–00:48; 433 · U4 · L13 · 01:17–04:05)
10. **Develop the visual storytelling tools in the prototype.** Choose framing for the information needed at each moment; give characters roles and define their visual distinction, personality and behavior rules; preserve continuity; add transitions only when they help; and use analogies that clarify the story for its audience. Keep testing alternatives and remove panels or ideas that do not serve the story. (433 · U4 · L09 · 04:19–05:52; 433 · U4 · L10 · 00:49–01:45; 433 · U4 · L10 · 01:45–02:28; 433 · U4 · L10 · 02:28–02:57; 433 · U4 · L11 · 02:21–02:42; 433 · U4 · L11 · 02:42–03:52; 433 · U4 · L12 · 00:00–01:14; 433 · U4 · L12 · 01:14–01:56; 433 · U4 · L12 · 01:56–02:57; 433 · U4 · L12 · 02:57–03:58; 433 · U4 · L13 · 06:34–08:01; 433 · U4 · L13 · 08:01–09:42; 433 · U4 · L13 · 09:42–11:46)
11. **Review the whole storyboard and hand off only when the story works.** Check the sequence from beginning to end, continue prototyping, and use only the detail needed to explain the story. Once the prototype works, it can be redrawn more cleanly and passed to an animatic. The visual production technique is a later decision. (433 · U4 · L09 · 03:45–04:19; 433 · U4 · L13 · 11:46–14:33)
12. **Estimate time, then build an animatic.** Imagine the storyboard in motion and estimate narration time; edit, merge, shorten or expand scenes as needed. Distribute ordered panels on a timeline, set and revise durations, and rough in camera movement and transitions. Replay, revise repeatedly, and render a version when the story feels like it works. (433 · U5 · L14 · 00:30–01:32; 433 · U5 · L14 · 01:32–01:50; 433 · U5 · L14 · 01:50–02:16; 433 · U5 · L14 · 02:16–02:57; 433 · U5 · L14 · 02:57–03:26; 433 · U5 · L14 · 03:26–03:51; 433 · U5 · L16 · 00:00–01:31; 433 · U5 · L16 · 01:31–02:42; 433 · U5 · L16 · 02:42–03:32; 433 · U5 · L16 · 03:32–04:40; 433 · U5 · L16 · 04:40–05:29; 433 · U5 · L16 · 07:06–08:12)
13. **Prototype sound with the animatic.** Decide voice qualities, intonation and pauses; test music for tone and atmosphere, consider it as a timing base, and sketch sound effects or foley. Use sound notes or rough mock-ups to guide later work. (433 · U5 · L15 · 00:00–01:29; 433 · U5 · L15 · 03:25–04:22; 433 · U5 · L15 · 04:22–05:09; 433 · U5 · L16 · 05:29–07:06)

## Principles and claims

- Influences can come from outside design, from recent work or childhood, and shape a maker’s vision consciously or unconsciously. (433 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–01:52)
- Storytelling joins rational and instinctive response, creates an emotional connection, and helps facts and messages be told, remembered, understood and shared. (433 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:14; 433 · U2 · L03 · 01:14–02:19; 433 · U2 · L03 · 02:19–03:40)
- Storytelling also operates in everyday interactions and design contexts such as presentations, instructions, layouts, interfaces and ATMs. (433 · U2 · L03 · 01:14–02:19)
- Animation and design offer a distinctive set of tools for telling stories; the course applies them to motion design. (433 · U2 · L03 · 03:40–04:43)
- Planning is an overall scan of the project that establishes where the work will happen and what rules it will use. (433 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:03)
- The planning pillars pair sender with receiver, message duration with available production time, and desired viewer response with the maker’s own objective. (433 · U2 · L04 · 01:03–01:56; 433 · U2 · L04 · 01:56–02:46; 433 · U2 · L04 · 02:46–04:12)
- The story X-ray distinguishes input (what is told) from output (how it is told); base idea/premise, tone/personality and structure/order are its three links. (433 · U3 · L05 · 00:50–01:41; 433 · U3 · L05 · 01:41–02:24)
- A premise joins form and function; it does not need to specify animation technique or graphic style. It should be clear enough to understand the subject and open enough to leave options. (433 · U3 · L06 · 00:49–01:37; 433 · U3 · L06 · 02:14–03:08; 433 · U3 · L06 · 07:51–08:34)
- Brainstorming welcomes passing thoughts and vague ideas; ideas can be prototyped, added, removed or replaced before selection. (433 · U3 · L06 · 00:00–00:49; 433 · U3 · L06 · 03:08–03:50)
- The more novel a premise, the more appealing its result may be; novelty does not require convolution. (433 · U3 · L06 · 07:51–08:34)
- Tone gives a story personality and identity, bonds sender and audience, and can be combined with function in many ways. The intended tone needs to read clearly to viewers. (433 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–00:41; 433 · U3 · L07 · 00:41–02:08; 433 · U3 · L07 · 02:55–03:42)
- Structure orders narrated events and their temporal importance; it supports a story while allowing varied turns, rises and falls between its start and end. (433 · U3 · L08 · 00:08–01:08)
- In the described three-act structure, Act 1 introduces characters, world rules and conflict; Act 2 searches for a solution through increasing difficulty toward a second climax; Act 3 finds the solution and closes the cycle. (433 · U3 · L08 · 01:08–02:54)
- Non-linear storytelling can break chronological order and remain structured; the teacher names circular, puzzle and bead-necklace forms. A three-act structure also applies to animated infographics and motion-graphics videos. (433 · U3 · L08 · 02:54–03:31; 433 · U3 · L08 · 03:31–05:03; 433 · U3 · L08 · 01:08–02:54)
- Structural play stays subordinate to the story’s ultimate goal: the receiver understanding the message. (433 · U3 · L08 · 05:43–06:50)
- Storyboarding is a rapid-prototyping tool for drawing, framing and sequencing quick ideas to see whether the story works. The drawing itself need not be polished. (433 · U4 · L09 · 00:00–00:48; 433 · U4 · L09 · 02:02–02:58)
- Storyboards are modular prototypes; panels can move until late in the process, and the intended output format should inform the frame. (433 · U4 · L09 · 02:58–03:45)
- Framing gives viewers information at the needed moment and guides where to look; close-ups convey emotion, extreme close-ups heighten drama, detail shots emphasize an element, and long shots provide context. (433 · U4 · L09 · 04:19–05:52)
- A character can be a person, shape, typography or other actor with a defined narrative role. Characters share the story’s visual DNA, but their narrative and visual development can differ by role. (433 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–00:49; 433 · U4 · L10 · 00:49–01:45)
- Character construction uses visual distinction, personality and behavior rules in service of the story; sometimes a character comes first and a story is built around it. (433 · U4 · L10 · 01:45–02:28)
- Continuity is the natural flow of on-screen information across scenes. A transition is a narrative tool with a reason and metaphor, not a flourish detached from the story. (433 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–01:18; 433 · U4 · L11 · 01:18–02:21)
- Animation can use the image itself as the transition’s material; shared geometry or color, movement speed and direction, and camera movement can connect scenes. (433 · U4 · L11 · 02:21–02:42; 433 · U4 · L11 · 02:42–03:52)
- Visual analogies clarify hard-to-grasp material by comparing different things with shared attributes. They work best when familiar to the specific audience and can make the receiver a participant in the information. (433 · U4 · L12 · 00:00–01:14; 433 · U4 · L12 · 02:57–03:58)
- An animatic adds time to the previously ambiguous storyboard and is the final link for validating whether the story works. It can be rough or detailed to suit project complexity and production needs. (433 · U5 · L14 · 00:00–00:30; 433 · U5 · L14 · 01:50–02:16)
- Sound is a narrative tool to consider from the beginning: voice performance, music and foley can shape delivery, tone, atmosphere and rhythm. (433 · U5 · L15 · 00:00–01:29; 433 · U5 · L15 · 03:25–04:22; 433 · U5 · L15 · 04:22–05:09)
- Music can provide a beat for shot durations and animated choreography; foley can be realistic or can strengthen an on-screen action. (433 · U5 · L15 · 03:25–04:22; 433 · U5 · L15 · 04:22–05:09)
- A working story may take different visual forms, including 3D, vector, traditional animation or live action. The story can be developed before choosing that final form. (433 · U4 · L13 · 11:46–14:33; 433 · U5 · L16 · 09:26–10:20)

## Checks the teacher uses

- **Planning objective:** compare the finished narrative’s real success with the intended viewer response. This could be assessed from the rendered piece and viewer response, but the course gives no metric or procedure. (433 · U2 · L04 · 02:46–04:12)
- **Premise:** judge whether it is clear about context and characters while still leaving visual possibilities open; judge novelty without convolution. These are qualitative judgments, not rendered-artifact measurements. (433 · U3 · L06 · 07:51–08:34)
- **Tone:** check whether the viewer receives the intended tone without misunderstanding; this needs audience judgment/listening, not a numeric measurement. (433 · U3 · L07 · 02:55–03:42)
- **Structure:** check whether the story’s events hold together and the receiver can understand the message. Message comprehension can be examined with viewers; the teacher supplies no scoring measure. (433 · U3 · L08 · 00:08–01:08; 433 · U3 · L08 · 05:43–06:50)
- **Storyboard:** ask whether it is engaging, understandable, coherent, complete, free of unnecessary material, too obvious or too convoluted; review from a distance and test it with other people. These are mainly judgments by eye and audience response; no numeric thresholds are given. (433 · U4 · L09 · 03:45–04:19)
- **Storyboard detail:** use only enough detail to explain the story; this is a case-by-case judgment. (433 · U4 · L09 · 03:45–04:19)
- **Character:** check whether the character is recognizable, has a defined role, and behaves according to its established personality and rules; judgment by eye against the story. (433 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–00:49; 433 · U4 · L10 · 00:49–01:45; 433 · U4 · L10 · 01:45–02:28)
- **Transition:** inspect whether the two scenes share a usable visual element, whether motion speed/direction or camera movement connects them, and whether the transition is necessary and strengthens continuity. Shared colors/shapes and movement can be inspected in the rendered sequence; narrative justification remains judgment by eye. (433 · U4 · L11 · 02:21–02:42; 433 · U4 · L11 · 02:42–03:52)
- **Analogy:** check whether the comparison makes the concept familiar and graspable for the specific audience; requires judgment and ideally a viewer response, with no stated metric. (433 · U4 · L12 · 00:00–01:14; 433 · U4 · L12 · 02:57–03:58)
- **Timing:** compare intended duration with scene timings in the animatic. Total runtime and per-scene durations can be measured from the rendered artifact or timeline; the desired rhythm and whether each moment has enough time require viewing judgment. No pass/fail durations are prescribed. (433 · U5 · L14 · 00:30–01:32; 433 · U5 · L14 · 01:32–01:50; 433 · U5 · L16 · 03:32–04:40; 433 · U5 · L16 · 04:40–05:29)
- **Animatic revisions:** replay versions, change timings, and be self-critical; clarity, reading and cleanliness matter. Scene duration and runtime are measurable; whether the story feels right is judgment by eye and ear. (433 · U5 · L16 · 05:29–07:06; 433 · U5 · L16 · 07:06–08:12)
- **Audio:** listen for how voice, music and effects change the message, tone, atmosphere and rhythm. Beat-to-shot timing can be measured in the rendered sequence; the sound’s emotional effect is judgment by ear. (433 · U5 · L15 · 00:00–01:29; 433 · U5 · L15 · 03:25–04:22; 433 · U5 · L15 · 04:22–05:09)

## Examples

- **Terry Gilliam, *The Adventures of Baron Munchausen*:** fantastic vignettes, extravagant characters and the striking winged figure of Death are cited as formative visual and narrative influences. (433 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–01:52)
- **Charles and Ray Eames, *Powers of Ten*:** a scale journey pulls away from a Chicago picnic, then returns and travels inward to a proton, using animation to communicate scale. (433 · U1 · L02 · 01:52–03:52)
- ** *Rick and Morty*, “Mad Max” episode:** the post-apocalyptic homage carries an analogy for Morty and Summer facing their parents’ separation. (433 · U1 · L02 · 03:52–05:25)
- **Edgar Wright, *Hot Fuzz*:** a quick montage of short scenes conveys the protagonist’s transfer and attitude, with timing, humor and sound. (433 · U1 · L02 · 05:25–06:26)
- **Edgar Wright, *Shaun of the Dead*:** a pub conversation describes brief vignettes that foreshadow later events without showing them. (433 · U1 · L02 · 06:26–07:32)
- **Pixar, *Inside Out*:** the opening explains how memories and emotions work, embedding information inside an animated narrative. (433 · U1 · L02 · 07:32–08:50)
- **Johnny Kelly’s Chipotle short:** stop motion and time-lapse with mixed analog/digital techniques and a Coldplay cover tell a story that questions food relationships. (433 · U1 · L02 · 08:50–09:41)
- **Chris Curtis’s work:** real footage and saturated graphics are combined boldly to create a distinctive audiovisual presentation. (433 · U1 · L02 · 09:41–10:26)
- **Studio AKA and Oliver Jeffers:** varied techniques and styles carry memorable characters, absurdity, humor, nostalgia and sadness. (433 · U1 · L02 · 10:26–11:28)
- **Amparo y Justicia campaign:** a delicate subject and a bill proposal are framed as a story about secondary victimization and possible recovery through video-recorded interviews. (433 · U2 · L03 · 04:43–06:24; 433 · U2 · L03 · 06:24–07:46)

- ** *The Wizard of Oz*:** a circular structure returns Dorothy to Kansas; the Kansas sections are black and white and Oz is in color. (433 · U3 · L08 · 03:31–05:03)
- ** *Memento* and *Irreversible*:** puzzle structures reorder events and reveal clues or resolve doubts as the film proceeds. (433 · U3 · L08 · 03:31–05:03)

- ** *2001: A Space Odyssey*:** a thrown bone cuts to a space station, compressing a large passage of time in a transition. (433 · U4 · L11 · 01:18–02:21)

## Tool-specific steps

- **After Effects (the teacher’s choice for this project):** order and number storyboard images; open an editing project, import the images and voice-over, and create a 1920×1080, 24 fps composition. The teacher says Premiere, Final Cut or any editor with a timeline can also be used. (433 · U5 · L16 · 01:31–02:42)
- Place images against the voice-over using rough estimated timings; begin with an arbitrary common base duration, then adjust each scene. Rough in zooms, pans or other camera moves and review playback in real time. (433 · U5 · L16 · 02:42–03:32; 433 · U5 · L16 · 03:32–04:40)
- Edit voice-over spacing as needed; add scene names, durations and useful production notes, including sound or music ideas; iterate and render a movie version. (433 · U5 · L16 · 04:40–05:29; 433 · U5 · L16 · 05:29–07:06; 433 · U5 · L16 · 07:06–08:12)
