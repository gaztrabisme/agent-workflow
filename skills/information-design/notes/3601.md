## Method steps

1. **Choose a subject and frame an investigative question.** A project may start from an experience used to make a personal dataset or from an available dataset; for the course project, the teacher recommends an existing dataset. Ask a question the data could answer. (3601 · U3 · L06 · 00:00–01:02; 3601 · U3 · L06 · 01:02–02:01; 3601 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:50)
2. **Find and assess the source.** Search with a specific question, open results rather than judging by their titles, compare secondary summaries with the primary source, and read the report and methodology to understand definitions, treatment, scope, and gaps. (3601 · U3 · L06 · 02:01–03:09; 3601 · U3 · L06 · 03:09–04:40; 3601 · U3 · L06 · 04:40–06:16)
3. **Write the brief before deciding the “what” and “how.”** State who the reader is, why the data is being visualised, and where it will appear. The audience’s knowledge, the purpose, and the channel affect tone and the amount of detail or time a reader has. (3601 · U3 · L06 · 06:16–09:22)
4. **Prepare a trustworthy working dataset.** Inspect the workbook and fields; remove irrelevant material; preserve the source data; correct or format values; use the source methodology to interpret categories; choose useful dimensions; record missing values and any replacement years; keep the analysis organized. (3601 · U3 · L07 · 01:16–02:24; 3601 · U3 · L07 · 03:34–05:38; 3601 · U3 · L07 · 05:38–07:19; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 14:14–15:14)
5. **Interrogate the data with five questions.** Ask who, what, when, where, and how much. Decide which measure and dimensions answer the original question; distinguish overlapping categories, compare years, account for missing values and geographic groupings, and calculate absolute or relative change when useful. (3601 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–00:47; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 00:47–02:24; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 02:24–04:35; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 04:35–07:26; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 07:26–10:06; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 10:06–14:14)
6. **Find the insight and select what the story should emphasize.** Visualize or compare the data to see trends, outliers, differences, and gaps. Choose which layer carries the main story and which details can remain secondary; the clean-water example adds income to regional comparison and gives missing coverage a place in the story. (3601 · U3 · L08 · 10:06–14:14; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 13:37–15:13; 3601 · U2 · L05 · 07:50–10:45)
7. **Build a visual language from the subject and the data.** Gather references from the subject and familiar imagery, consider the variables and possible chart forms, and connect a metaphor or visual vocabulary to the data. Treat inspiration as a way to serve function and story, not as a style exercise by itself. (3601 · U2 · L05 · 05:11–07:50; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 00:09–03:18)
8. **Sketch candidate encodings and layouts.** Try different chart marks, scales, groupings, labels, and arrangements on paper. Use the variable and the precision needed to choose among position, length, area, shape, and other visual attributes. Keep sketches rough and use them to expose what the design and the data preparation still need. (3601 · U3 · L09 · 03:18–07:00; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 07:00–09:50; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 09:50–13:37; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 13:37–15:13; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 15:13–17:37; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 17:37–18:45)
9. **Prototype and validate the sketch against actual data.** Select a promising idea, try the tool or chart framework that fits it, bind the intended fields, and plot the data. Check whether scales, ordering, grouping, labels, and visual form work; return to the sketch or try another tool when the result does not. (3601 · U3 · L09 · 17:37–18:45; 3601 · U3 · L10 · 00:09–01:52; 3601 · U3 · L10 · 01:52–04:30; 3601 · U3 · L10 · 04:30–05:52; 3601 · U3 · L10 · 05:52–07:27; 3601 · U3 · L10 · 07:27–08:56; 3601 · U3 · L10 · 08:56–10:52; 3601 · U3 · L10 · 10:52–14:05)
10. **Develop a coherent display for its reading context.** Arrange the main and supporting views around the story; establish the layout, visual grammar, scales, text hierarchy, and reusable styles; keep important content clear of a print fold; and make sure the different charts and map work together. (3601 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–01:43; 3601 · U4 · L11 · 01:43–03:56; 3601 · U4 · L11 · 03:56–05:58; 3601 · U4 · L12 · 02:10–03:18; 3601 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–00:54)
11. **Spell out the story and the encoding.** Draft and verify the copy, then add titles, context, direct labels, axes, scales, keys, group headings, and annotations. Check that the key explains the encoding and that each view’s labels describe what it actually shows. (3601 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–02:46; 3601 · U4 · L14 · 02:46–06:08; 3601 · U4 · L14 · 06:08–07:47; 3601 · U4 · L14 · 07:47–09:24; 3601 · U4 · L14 · 09:24–12:46; 3601 · U4 · L14 · 12:46–15:43)
12. **Evaluate, revise, and prepare the work for delivery.** Review a print at the scale and format readers will see, note refinements, and test whether people understand it without an explanation. Revise any unclear marks, keys, labels, hierarchy, or map treatment; then export for the intended channel and consider whether another medium or collaboration better carries the story. (3601 · U4 · L15 · 05:34–06:58; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 00:09–01:30; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 08:00–09:10; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 09:10–10:00; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 10:00–10:33; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 10:33–11:19; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 11:19–12:47; 3601 · U5 · L18 · 00:09–00:41; 3601 · U5 · L18 · 00:41–01:46; 3601 · U5 · L18 · 01:46–02:52; 3601 · U5 · L18 · 02:52–03:30; 3601 · U5 · L18 · 03:30–04:28)

## Principles and claims

- Data describes aspects of reality and experience through attributes; qualitative attributes can be nominal or ordinal, and quantitative ones continuous or discrete. (3601 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–02:43)
- L.A.T.C.H. names five ways to organize information: Location, Alphabet, Time, Category, and Hierarchy. (3601 · U2 · L03 · 02:43–04:30)
- Raw data does not speak for itself; visual communication can make trends and differences quicker to see than a table. (3601 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–02:34)
- The teacher calls attributes detected automatically by visual perception “pre-attentive” and uses visual variables to encode numbers as visible forms. (3601 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–02:34)
- The visual-attribute board groups length, width, curvature, shape/pattern, added mark, and blur as form; orientation, proximity, enclosure, and motion as spatial; and hue, saturation, lightness, and transparency as colour. (frame) (3601 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–02:34)
- Visual literacy includes reading, writing, and creating images; a sign’s meaning can depend on context, as with red in a traffic-light system. (3601 · U2 · L04 · 02:34–04:51)
- A visual metaphor can make large or abstract quantities tangible and connect an encoding with a human story. (3601 · U2 · L04 · 04:51–10:08; 3601 · U2 · L05 · 05:11–07:50)
- The teacher describes a successful infographic as a form that fits the data’s function and gives its hidden story emotional as well as analytical force. (3601 · U2 · L05 · 10:45–12:49)
- Statistics are a footprint of experience: they can help people understand reality but cannot capture all its complexity. (3601 · U2 · L05 · 10:45–12:49)
- Data visual storytelling can begin a conversation and invite reflection or empathy without claiming that every person represented had the same experience. (3601 · U2 · L04 · 04:51–10:08)
- A percentage can help compare populations of different sizes; if categories partition a whole, check that they add to 100%, and account for overlapping definitions before summing them. (3601 · U3 · L08 · 00:47–02:24)
- Missing data is part of the evidence and may itself belong in the story; the teacher records substitute years and shows where country data is unavailable. (3601 · U3 · L08 · 07:26–10:06; 3601 · U4 · L12 · 11:13–12:43)
- A slope chart can show start-to-end change; small multiples can reduce label congestion, provided the comparison axes stay consistent. (3601 · U3 · L09 · 03:18–07:00)
- Length is easier to compare accurately than area; the teacher prefers length for small changes on a finite percentage scale and allows area when values span a broad range and precision matters less. (3601 · U3 · L09 · 07:00–09:50)
- Reduce marks or layers when they obscure the intended story; for the book infographic, the teacher drops an additional data layer to avoid overwhelming readers. (3601 · U3 · L09 · 15:13–17:37)
- Keep chart scales comparable when the displays are meant to be compared; the teacher fixes each income chart to the same 0–100 range. (3601 · U4 · L12 · 06:48–09:26)
- A key, scale, and direct labels should explain the marks and the values they represent. (3601 · U4 · L14 · 09:24–12:46; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 00:09–01:30; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 01:30–02:24; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 02:24–03:13)
- The final water-access chart places the past and present values in a glass metaphor and distinguishes no change, increase, and decrease; the labels and legend make those meanings explicit. (frame) (3601 · U5 · L17 · 02:24–03:13)
- Inspiration comes from subject matter, lived experience, and many fields; the teacher distinguishes this from collecting attractive visual styles without a story or function. (3601 · U1 · L02 · 05:17–07:01; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 00:09–03:18)

## Checks the teacher uses

- Open and compare source pages; read the primary report and methodology to verify what was measured, how it was treated, and where coverage is missing. **Rendered artifact:** cannot be established from the graphic alone; requires source and method review. (3601 · U3 · L06 · 02:01–03:09; 3601 · U3 · L06 · 03:09–04:40; 3601 · U3 · L06 · 04:40–06:16)
- Compare copied table headings and values with the original, inspect formulas and rounding, and preserve a source copy before editing. **Rendered artifact:** not sufficient; check the workbook against its source data. (3601 · U3 · L07 · 03:34–05:38)
- Check whether categories that should partition a whole total 100%; diagnose overlap in the definitions before charting them. **Rendered artifact:** arithmetic is measurable from the data table; the reason for an incorrect sum requires definition review. (3601 · U3 · L08 · 00:47–02:24)
- Filter and investigate missing values, record the closest year used, and consider whether missingness affects which countries belong in the comparison. **Rendered artifact:** missing marks or a no-data map can be inspected; correctness needs the source dataset and year notes. (3601 · U3 · L08 · 04:35–07:26; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 07:26–10:06; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 06:18–07:18)
- Plot the sketch and inspect whether the scale spreads values usefully, whether outliers remain visible, and whether marks overlap. **Rendered artifact:** axis bounds, plotted positions, and overlaps can be measured against the source values; deciding whether a scale clarifies the pattern remains visual judgment. (3601 · U2 · L05 · 03:24–05:11; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 17:37–18:45)
- Compare repeated charts on the same minimum and maximum; keep the relevant endpoints and region breaks aligned. **Rendered artifact:** scale bounds and mark positions can be measured; whether the result reads clearly still needs judgment. (3601 · U4 · L12 · 06:48–09:26; 3601 · U4 · L12 · 09:26–11:13)
- Check the graphic at its intended reading size by printing it and going through each element for possible refinement. **Rendered artifact:** print inspection is a visual judgment by the designer, not a numeric test. (3601 · U4 · L15 · 05:34–06:58)
- Ask other people to interpret the infographic without a voice-over; listen for whether they understand its point and the marks. **Rendered artifact:** cannot be concluded from the image alone; it requires an audience test. (3601 · U4 · L15 · 05:34–06:58; 3601 · U4 · L14 · 12:46–15:43)
- Use the test findings to diagnose concrete failures: numbers without context, an unexplained key, a key that does not match the display, unclear bars, weak type hierarchy, or a map that appears disconnected. **Rendered artifact:** presence, labels, axis coverage, and layout can be inspected; hierarchy and coherence require judgment by eye, and comprehension requires readers. (3601 · U5 · L17 · 00:09–01:30; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 01:30–02:24; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 02:24–03:13; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 03:13–03:46)
- Check that labels avoid the centre fold and are consistently oriented, and that lines do not extend beyond their actual data endpoints. **Rendered artifact:** label positions, angles, and mark endpoints can be measured; the teacher’s stated review is visual. (3601 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–01:43; 3601 · U4 · L13 · 00:54–03:50)
- If a colour treatment or map element is uncertain, iterate alternatives and test them with people. **Rendered artifact:** palette values and variant presence can be measured; whether the encoding is clear and fits the story needs visual and reader judgment. (`U04-unit.md`, Practice exercise 4.3; 3601 · U4 · L13 · 05:52–08:48)

## Examples

- **Personal maps:** the teacher collected more than 500 hand-drawn world maps; she reads them as different personal perspectives and stories carried in a map. (3601 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–01:33)
- **Bruno Munari, _Drawing a Tree_:** breaking a familiar tree into many forms and components shows how curiosity and structure can inform visual exploration. (3601 · U1 · L02 · 01:33–03:03)
- **Otto and Marie Neurath’s Isotype charts:** repeated pictograms communicate unemployment, weaving production, and other statistics through a visual language. (3601 · U1 · L02 · 03:03–03:49; 3601 · U2 · L04 · 04:51–10:08)
- **William Playfair’s charts:** historical line, bar, pie, and area forms are cited as conventions that make quantitative relationships visually readable. (3601 · U2 · L04 · 02:34–04:51)
- **_The Shining_ colour analysis:** the teacher maps the film’s colour from beginning to end; the brighter moments point to dramatic changes in Jack. (3601 · U1 · L02 · 03:49–05:17)
- **_A Rough Guide to Conflict_:** 133 wars and more than 95 million deaths are encoded as poppies on a time and duration display; flower size represents deaths, position represents time and duration, and colour represents region. (3601 · U2 · L05 · 01:50–03:24; 3601 · U2 · L05 · 03:24–05:11; 3601 · U2 · L05 · 07:50–10:45)
- **_Scientific American_ food-waste infographic:** height and colour, a waste-pile image, and cow equivalents turn supply-chain waste and household meat waste into tangible quantities. (3601 · U2 · L04 · 04:51–10:08)
- **_MeToomentum_:** a dandelion shape connects 200,000 tweets from the first six months of #MeToo with vulnerability, strength, endurance, and hope. (3601 · U2 · L04 · 04:51–10:08; 3601 · U5 · L18 · 02:52–03:30)
- **_London Under the Microscope_:** a virus-shaped view of London layers mobility, cases, deaths, and vaccines; the project also sonifies the pandemic timeline. (3601 · U2 · L04 · 04:51–10:08; 3601 · U5 · L18 · 00:41–01:46)
- **Clean-water access infographic, _A Thirsty World_:** a tap, glasses, country comparisons, income groups, and a no-data map connect the 2000–2020 access measure with everyday water imagery and global coverage gaps. (3601 · U3 · L06 · 01:02–02:01; 3601 · U4 · L12 · 02:10–03:18; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 00:09–01:30; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 01:30–02:24; 3601 · U5 · L17 · 02:24–03:13)
- **_Sentinel_ sea-level projection:** a theatre projection uses tactile imagery to show sea-level change. (3601 · U5 · L17 · 10:33–11:19)
- **David Bowie’s “Space Oddity” deconstruction:** a musicologist collaborator helps expose the song’s layers and celebrate its craft. (3601 · U5 · L18 · 01:46–02:52)
