# Course 4217 — Creative Data Visualization for Narrative Designs

## Method steps

1. **Frame the communication problem.** Before choosing visual forms, identify the data’s characteristics, the readers and their needs, the use context, and the medium; decide whether the work should support quick comparison, exploration, discovery, or engagement. [4217 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:08; 4217 · U2 · L04 · 02:25–03:22; 4217 · U2 · L04 · 04:45–05:21]
2. **Choose a subject and reliable source.** Start from a trusted source and a topic the designer is curious or passionate about; the project also communicates what the designer wants to talk about. [4217 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–01:39]
3. **Collect context and relevant datasets.** Explore related charts, tables, source notes, and available years; select datasets that add useful context, and choose a regional classification when grouping countries. [4217 · U3 · L08 · 01:39–03:39; 4217 · U3 · L08 · 03:39–05:28; 4217 · U3 · L08 · 05:28–06:50; 4217 · U3 · L08 · 06:50–08:44]
4. **Set and state a defensible scope.** Choose a selection rule that can be explained to readers (the example is the 40 countries with the highest renewable-energy share in 2020); retain a working copy of the source and make the criterion explicit in the visualization’s text. [4217 · U2 · L05 · 03:40–04:39; 4217 · U3 · L09 · 03:21–04:02]
5. **Prepare a clean, joined dataset.** Keep clear column headings directly above data, filter to the chosen year and entities, sort as needed, preserve intermediate tabs, join tables on matching country names, check match counts, investigate unmatched rows, and remove unused fields. [4217 · U3 · L09 · 03:21–04:02; 4217 · U3 · L09 · 04:02–06:06; 4217 · U3 · L09 · 06:06–09:18; 4217 · U3 · L10 · 05:09–09:24; 4217 · U3 · L10 · 09:24–12:48; 4217 · U3 · L10 · 12:48–15:31; 4217 · U3 · L10 · 15:31–18:56]
6. **Move from variables to a rough story structure.** List the variables, decide the order readers will encounter them, and sketch the macrostructure before polishing it. The course example starts with regions and population for context, then shows the renewable-energy measures; it uses circle area for population, line length for renewable share, and an organic mark for per-capita energy. [4217 · U3 · L11 · 00:08–01:14; 4217 · U3 · L11 · 01:14–02:26; 4217 · U3 · L11 · 02:26–02:54; 4217 · U3 · L11 · 02:54–03:47]
7. **Choose visual channels and chart forms for the task.** Treat position, size, shape, opacity, color, orientation, texture, line thickness, and multiple elements as visual variables; use the project’s purpose and the information’s precision needs to decide which variables and forms carry each value. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:32; 4217 · U2 · L03 · 02:18–04:02; 4217 · U4 · L12 · 03:29–05:30]
8. **Decide whether a visual metaphor serves the content.** Do not force one. Study the subject and the data for forms that can deepen their relationship; a metaphor may emerge from the dataset, or deliberately challenge a familiar metaphor. [4217 · U2 · L07 · 00:00–00:45; 4217 · U2 · L07 · 00:45–01:54; 4217 · U2 · L07 · 01:54–02:25; 4217 · U2 · L07 · 03:56–05:25]
9. **Build the visualization in layers and refine it.** Use a generated chart as a structural starting point when helpful, then add and refine marks, labels, grid, and supporting information. Keep refinements in service of the story and test small adjustments in the composition. [4217 · U4 · L12 · 11:14–12:44; 4217 · U4 · L15 · 00:11–03:08; 4217 · U4 · L15 · 09:31–12:04; 4217 · U4 · L16 · 02:17–08:01; 4217 · U4 · L16 · 10:37–15:22]
10. **Write the title, explanation, and legend for the reader.** Describe the selection, include the year and units, and explain encodings in the order readers encounter them. Keep sentences and words simple; use examples when a size or mark needs further explanation. [4217 · U4 · L17 · 00:09–01:31; 4217 · U4 · L17 · 01:31–05:16; 4217 · U4 · L17 · 05:16–10:59; 4217 · U4 · L17 · 10:59–12:15; 4217 · U4 · L18 · 02:30–05:55]
11. **Credit sources and check the work with readers.** Declare sources, inspect the whole composition at different zoom levels and on a phone, and ask people outside the field whether it is clear. [4217 · U4 · L18 · 08:45–10:39]
12. **Adapt and export for the intended use.** Choose an output format and artboard appropriate to the context; when a mobile version is needed, rearrange the layout, enlarge text, and test the exported image on a phone. [4217 · U4 · L19 · 00:00–02:15; 4217 · U4 · L19 · 02:15–05:45; 4217 · U4 · L19 · 08:45–10:45]

## Principles and claims

- Information design visually represents content so people can explore and understand it; a data visualization can enable analysis, exploration, and discovery. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:32]
- Visual variables can be treated as letters and combined into visual words; combinations can be simple or experimental according to what the work needs. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:32]
- For verbal comparison of numerical values, the course’s cited ranking is position, length, area, angle, then intensity. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 02:18–04:02]
- The channel-ranking figure describes its ordering as typical precision for stating ratios between two values. (frame) [4217 · U2 · L03 · 02:18–04:02]
- Consider color-vision impairments; the teacher advises against a green–red scale and suggests checking palette accessibility with Color Oracle. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 04:02–05:37]
- Color-coded categories can be given an additional indicator: the endemic-species example pairs group colors with initials. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 04:02–05:37]
- Including zero on a quantitative axis is recommended for showing differences accurately; a non-zero base can emphasize differences, and viewers may overestimate them even when the base is marked. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 04:02–05:37]
- The paired bar-chart figure shows the same values appearing more different when the vertical axis begins at 40 than when it begins at zero. (frame) [4217 · U2 · L03 · 04:02–05:37]
- A chart form depends on the needs of the project; a more immediate chart is not automatically the right choice when the goal is engagement or exploration. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 01:32–02:18; 4217 · U2 · L04 · 04:45–05:21]
- Readers, context, medium, and intended actions affect design decisions; consider how much time readers will need. [4217 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:08; 4217 · U2 · L06 · 04:48–05:16]
- Printed work can use layered information to support exploration at different depths when interactivity is unavailable. [4217 · U2 · L04 · 03:22–04:45]
- Visual and written words are both part of a visual narrative; words can teach readers how to read a graphic and explain the designer’s point of view. [4217 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–00:57; 4217 · U2 · L05 · 02:25–03:40]
- Data and visualizations are not completely neutral: selection criteria and human intervention matter, so explain the choices, including selection criteria, in short text. [4217 · U2 · L05 · 03:40–04:39]
- Numbers can have lives behind them; organic forms can help keep that human dimension in view. [4217 · U2 · L05 · 00:57–01:48; 4217 · U2 · L06 · 03:43–04:48]
- Multiple infographics can work together to give readers a deeper understanding of a subject. [4217 · U2 · L05 · 01:48–02:25]
- The teacher’s migration project first reveals routes as lines, then lets readers discover the map and supporting data; the sequence was chosen to invite exploration. [4217 · U2 · L06 · 01:08–02:00; 4217 · U2 · L06 · 02:54–03:43]
- A rational visual approach does not necessarily lack empathy. [4217 · U2 · L06 · 02:00–02:54]
- Visual metaphors should not be forced; forms can be drawn from a subject, from patterns in the data, or chosen to challenge a common metaphor. [4217 · U2 · L07 · 00:00–00:45; 4217 · U2 · L07 · 01:54–02:25; 4217 · U2 · L07 · 03:56–05:25]
- In the “Key workers” example, tree regions, branches, and dots represent geographic regions, sectors and levels, and individual stories. (frame) [4217 · U2 · L07 · 02:25–03:56]
- RAWGraphs is one possible way to establish the graphic’s skeleton; explore tools, then choose the parts that answer the project’s specific need. [4217 · U4 · L12 · 00:00–03:29; 4217 · U4 · L12 · 11:14–12:44]
- Where color does not carry essential information, the teacher is comfortable beginning in black and white and adding color later; if color carries information, she works on it near the beginning. [4217 · U4 · L15 · 09:31–12:04; 4217 · U4 · L16 · 08:01–10:37]
- The course’s final legend assigns circle area to population, line length to renewable-energy share, a dot to top-20 fossil-fuel countries, and leaves to per-capita renewable consumption. (frame) [4217 · U4 · L17 · 05:16–10:59; 4217 · U4 · L17 · 10:59–12:15]
- In the final example, the title and explanation precede a legend and regional chart; the layout makes the intended order visible. (frame) [4217 · U4 · L17 · 01:31–05:16; 4217 · U4 · L17 · 05:16–10:59]

## Checks the teacher uses

- **Check the chart channel against the comparison task.** Position is presented as more precise than length, area, angle, and intensity for verbally stating ratios. Rendered check: identify which mark dimension carries each value and whether comparisons rely on position, length, area, angle, or intensity. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 02:18–04:02]
- **Check palette accessibility.** The teacher recommends Color Oracle and extra category indicators. Rendered check: inspect the graphic under the simulator’s vision modes and check that categories remain distinguishable through another indicator; this uses visual judgement. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 04:02–05:37]
- **Check axis baselines.** The teacher compares a zero-based and non-zero bar chart and cautions about distorted perceived differences. Rendered check: read the axis minimum and compare it with the data’s zero baseline; the tick value can be read, while the risk of exaggeration is a perceptual judgement. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 04:02–05:37]
- **Check the data selection and merges.** The spreadsheet’s count confirms 40 countries; the merge tool reports 40 of 40 rows, then 39 of 40 for population, leading to a manual check for Venezuela. This is measurable from the table/tool output, not from the finished graphic alone. [4217 · U3 · L09 · 09:18–09:44; 4217 · U3 · L10 · 05:09–09:24; 4217 · U3 · L10 · 15:31–18:56]
- **Check the target number of encoded paths.** Document Info reports path counts while the teacher creates and trims leaf marks. Measurable in the vector document: count paths against the intended line count. [4217 · U4 · L14 · 06:15–09:33; 4217 · U4 · L14 · 09:33–12:12; 4217 · U4 · L14 · 12:12–15:33]
- **Check line-count cues and value-to-shape matching.** Every tenth line is made thicker to help readers estimate; compare the mark count and its thick-line intervals with the corresponding table value. The count is measurable in the vector document; whether the cue helps is judgement by eye. [4217 · U4 · L14 · 06:15–09:33]
- **Check order and identity against the data.** The teacher compares spreadsheet rows with the country marks, verifies country order, and searches the table when unsure. Measurable from the source table and graphic together; not recoverable from the rendered graphic alone if labels are unclear. [4217 · U4 · L14 · 12:12–15:33; 4217 · U4 · L16 · 18:16–20:57]
- **Check overlaps and spacing.** The teacher reduces overlaps, checks alignment, and adjusts spacing between marks, text, and curves. Rendered measurements could include mark intersections, label-to-mark gaps, and variation in repeated spacing; the teacher also judges the composition by eye. [4217 · U4 · L15 · 00:11–03:08; 4217 · U4 · L15 · 03:08–09:31; 4217 · U4 · L16 · 02:17–08:01]
- **Check the reading hierarchy.** Identify the main elements and use hierarchy to guide reading; the teacher checks axes and text for collisions and centers the composition. Judgement by eye, with overlaps and spacing also measurable on the artifact. [4217 · U4 · L16 · 02:17–08:01]
- **Check explanation and legend completeness.** Verify that selection rule, year, variables, units, and mark meanings are stated in reader order; compare those statements with the marks shown. A text/encoding checklist is measurable from the rendered artifact; clarity still needs judgement. [4217 · U4 · L17 · 01:31–05:16; 4217 · U4 · L17 · 05:16–10:59]
- **Check examples for quantitative keys.** The teacher explains circle-area references and leaf references at 40, 20, 10, and 5 MWh. Rendered measurement: compare reference marks’ displayed sizes/counts with their stated values; whether readers can interpret them needs judgement. [4217 · U4 · L18 · 00:00–02:30; 4217 · U4 · L18 · 02:30–05:55]
- **Check source attribution.** The teacher adds source credits and says sources should always be declared. Measurable as presence of the cited source text in the artifact; accuracy requires comparison with the source record. [4217 · U4 · L18 · 08:45–10:39]
- **Check at different scales and on a phone.** The teacher zooms in and out, checks the work on a smartphone, and asks non-experts whether it is clear. Measure rendered text size and visible collisions if desired; readability and clarity require judgement by eye and reader feedback. [4217 · U4 · L18 · 08:45–10:39; 4217 · U4 · L19 · 08:45–10:45]
- **Check mobile legibility after export.** Open the exported PNG on a phone and return to the design if anything is too small. The rendered artifact can be checked for text size and clipping; actual legibility is a judgement made on the device. [4217 · U4 · L19 · 08:45–10:45]

## Examples

- **“Ongoing actions taken by countries to address air pollution” (UN Environment Programme):** a comparatively immediate horizontal-bar graphic categorizes countries’ actions. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 01:32–02:18]
- **“Space Junk” (BBC Science Focus):** organizes space debris by average distance from Earth and object type, combining positional marks and small charts. [4217 · U2 · L03 · 01:32–02:18]
- **COVID-19 Community Vulnerability Index (Surgo Foundation):** minimal, precise views support pattern finding across states and counties; interaction filters and reveals detail. [4217 · U2 · L04 · 02:25–03:22]
- **Marx and Engels correspondence:** colored lines show correspondence over 40 years, while circle size and counts add works and letter totals; print layering supports slower exploration. [4217 · U2 · L04 · 03:22–04:45]
- **“Patterns of Violence for Brazil’s Land Defenders” and “A Landscape of Loss”:** leaf forms encode average proportions of environmental-defender deaths and lines encode average counts; a companion map uses peaks for deforestation by state and callouts for reported events. (frame) [4217 · U2 · L05 · 00:57–01:48]
- **“The Stories Behind a Line”:** six asylum seekers’ journeys become individual routes, with travel days, transport, distance, personal memories, and later-revealed map context. [4217 · U2 · L06 · 00:00–01:08; 4217 · U2 · L06 · 01:08–02:00; 4217 · U2 · L06 · 02:00–02:54]
- **“The Great Wave”:** excess deaths in Italian provinces are represented with one line per person and one leaf per 100 people, grouped by province and ordered by percentage increase. [4217 · U2 · L06 · 03:43–04:48]
- **“Where is Piero?”:** city locations associated with Piero della Francesca’s works are connected with curved bands inspired by painted drapery. (frame) [4217 · U2 · L07 · 00:45–01:54]
- **“Key workers” (ODI):** trees represent regions; branches represent sectors and levels; dots represent stories or initiatives. [4217 · U2 · L07 · 02:25–03:56]
- **“Hearts and minds” (ODI):** layered mountain forms challenge the usual arrows-and-waves imagery for migration and show public narratives and attitudes alongside migration figures. [4217 · U2 · L07 · 03:56–05:25]
- **Renewable-energy course project:** a top-40-country comparison combines region, population, renewable-energy share, per-capita renewable consumption, and top-20 fossil-fuel status. [4217 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–01:39; 4217 · U3 · L11 · 01:14–02:26; 4217 · U4 · L17 · 01:31–05:16]
