# Course 2495 — Data Visualization and Information Design: Create a Visual Model

Working notes for later skill design. Anchors point to the lesson section carrying the source material. “Frame” marks a detail read from a course figure rather than stated in the lesson prose.

## Method steps

1. **Start with communication and a reader.** Begin from a topic that interests the designer, then consider whether it could interest a potential reader; keep the reader in view from topic choice through visual form. (2495 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:29)
2. **Find a source and assess its reliability.** Check a source’s history, associated organizations, and how and by whom it is cited; choose an interesting subject from its coverage. (2495 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–01:14; 2495 · U3 · L07 · 01:14–02:08)
3. **Explore the source to locate data and a story.** Browse by topic or keyword, inspect available maps, charts, and tables, then select a central dataset and any useful secondary layer. (2495 · U3 · L07 · 02:08–03:19; 2495 · U3 · L07 · 03:19–04:30; 2495 · U3 · L07 · 04:30–05:38)
4. **Inspect and prepare the data.** Preserve the downloaded source, make a working copy, remove unneeded rows or columns, clarify headings, and use filters and sorting to understand values and narrow the dataset. (2495 · U3 · L08 · 01:11–02:27; 2495 · U3 · L08 · 02:27–05:01)
5. **Set explicit selection criteria for a static graphic.** Decide which year, ranking, population threshold, countries, and time span to show; explain the selection to readers because a static piece cannot show every value in a large dataset. (2495 · U3 · L08 · 02:27–05:01; 2495 · U3 · L08 · 05:01–08:58; 2495 · U3 · L08 · 08:58–12:22; 2495 · U2 · L05 · 04:17–06:14)
6. **Add context and record provenance details.** Join related data when needed; record units, years, and source exceptions alongside the values so they can be explained later. (2495 · U3 · L08 · 05:01–08:58; 2495 · U3 · L08 · 12:22–15:43; 2495 · U3 · L08 · 15:43–18:34)
7. **Abstract the dataset into variables and assign visual roles.** Write down the variables, distinguish central information from contextual information, then decide which variables will locate, size, shape, or otherwise encode each measure. (2495 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:08; 2495 · U3 · L09 · 01:08–02:33; 2495 · U3 · L09 · 02:33–03:35)
8. **Sketch the overall model before polishing.** Try possible structures against the data, such as a scatter plot for context and a repeated form for a time series; revise the idea when testing reveals problems. (2495 · U4 · L10 · 01:51–02:44; 2495 · U4 · L10 · 05:08–06:30)
9. **Research visual inspiration after studying the data.** Collect appealing shapes, textures, and structures from data visualization and other fields, then adapt useful qualities to the dataset and intended reader. (2495 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–01:51; 2495 · U4 · L10 · 02:44–04:08)
10. **Build a structural chart and map its variables.** Use a chart form that carries the main structure; when contextual values distort the layout, consider showing their order on the axes while labeling the actual values separately. (2495 · U4 · L11 · 01:31–02:46; 2495 · U4 · L11 · 02:46–04:21)
11. **Develop the custom encoding against the actual values.** Set a stable reference range, build the repeated marks from the data, and make one visual unit correspond to one data unit such as one year. (2495 · U4 · L12 · 04:22–05:31; 2495 · U4 · L12 · 05:31–07:47; 2495 · U4 · L12 · 07:47–09:35; 2495 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–03:01)
12. **Place the marks and add visual guides.** Align each mark with its data position, create guides that clarify ordering, and trim guide lines so they do not add unnecessary visual noise. (2495 · U4 · L13 · 06:55–10:10; 2495 · U4 · L13 · 13:27–15:44)
13. **Write the legend and explanatory text as part of the visualization.** Explain the encodings, units, time span, selection rules, sources, and important exceptions; use examples and short, direct wording. (2495 · U4 · L15 · 00:00–01:26; 2495 · U4 · L15 · 08:18–10:30; 2495 · U4 · L15 · 10:30–13:22)
14. **Add further information with an explicit rule.** The teacher uses additional visual variables to show comparisons or insights and adds their meaning to the legend. (2495 · U4 · L16 · 00:00–01:55; 2495 · U4 · L16 · 01:55–03:34; 2495 · U4 · L16 · 03:34–05:56; 2495 · U4 · L16 · 05:56–07:42)
15. **Step away, review, and test with readers.** Return with fresh eyes, check close detail and the overall composition, view at intended sizes, and ask non-expert readers whether they understand the work and its guides. (2495 · U4 · L16 · 10:00–11:15; 2495 · U4 · L17 · 00:00–01:15)
16. **Correct, refine, and prepare final outputs.** Fix errors and layout problems, refine the visual details, then export the formats needed for delivery and sharing. (2495 · U4 · L16 · 03:34–05:56; 2495 · U4 · L17 · 03:11–04:23; 2495 · U4 · L17 · 04:23–06:49; 2495 · U4 · L17 · 06:49–07:40; 2495 · U4 · L17 · 07:40–09:11)

## Principles and claims

- Information design gives structure to unstructured information so people can understand complex reality; a visualization communicates information and stories through visual form. (2495 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:31)
- The designer’s visual alphabet is made from variables the eye can distinguish: position, orientation, shape, size, line thickness, texture, opacity, color, and multiple elements. (2495 · U2 · L03 · 01:31–03:02)
- Visual variables can be used singly or combined; examples combine position with size and color, shape with repeated elements and thickness, and orientation with thickness and opacity. (2495 · U2 · L03 · 01:31–03:02) (frame)
- Chart form follows the data and content: the course contrasts length in histograms, position in scatter plots, and position plus size in bubble charts; radial arrangements are also possible when the data or content calls for them. (2495 · U2 · L03 · 01:31–03:02)
- Data visualizations can range from simple infographics to complex pieces that support several depths of analysis; reading speed and complexity depend on needs, readers, medium, and usage context. (2495 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–00:59; 2495 · U2 · L04 · 00:59–01:44)
- Communication is the main goal from topic choice through visual representation; designers should imagine a potential reader’s interests while beginning from a personal point of view. (2495 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:29)
- Aesthetics can help engage readers and invite them into the content; visual appeal is not described as an end in itself. (2495 · U1 · L02 · 03:00–03:53; 2495 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:29)
- Usage context affects the design: the teacher treats the weekend print supplement *La Lettura* as a setting for slow reading and designs its visualizations like visual equivalents of long articles. (2495 · U2 · L04 · 01:44–03:32)
- Human selection is inevitable, especially in static work; explain what was selected and why so readers can understand the designer’s starting point. (2495 · U2 · L05 · 02:55–04:17)
- A legend and words help readers understand a visual alphabet; the teacher rejects the assumption that an infographic should need no words and says readers cannot be presumed to know how to read graphics. (2495 · U4 · L15 · 00:00–01:26)
- Work in layers so readers can access different levels of information; the population-density example first encodes density with dots, then adds total population with circle area and geographic area with symbols. (2495 · U2 · L05 · 04:17–06:14)
- Treat contextual data differently from the central story when appropriate: population and GDP position the countries, while per-capita emissions carry the main time-series information. (2495 · U3 · L09 · 01:08–02:33; 2495 · U3 · L09 · 02:33–03:35)
- Keep actual measures and displayed order distinct: in the CO₂ example, population and GDP become ordinal positions to make the plot more readable, while actual values are added as labels. (2495 · U4 · L11 · 01:31–02:46; 2495 · U4 · L11 · 02:46–04:21; 2495 · U4 · L14 · 03:01–05:47; 2495 · U4 · L14 · 05:47–08:17)
- A first visual idea is provisional; the teacher tests it with data and changes it when needed rather than becoming attached to it. (2495 · U4 · L10 · 05:08–06:30)
- Inspiration can come from outside data visualization; shapes and textures that attract the designer can inform a visual alphabet and its style. (2495 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–01:51; 2495 · U4 · L10 · 02:44–04:08)
- Clear and consistent rules, small legends, and an index of the visual symbols can make a data-heavy book more accessible to its young audience. (2495 · U2 · L06 · 03:01–04:41)
- State collaboration expectations and capabilities, share honest feedback, and seek collaborators with different competencies and perspectives. (2495 · U2 · L06 · 06:43–07:45; 2495 · U2 · L06 · 07:45–08:31)
- Use color carefully as a strong visual variable; the teacher warns that too many colors can make a visualization difficult to read. (2495 · U4 · L16 · 00:00–01:55)
- The course’s final CO₂ figure visibly pairs a written explanation and encoding guide with the plotted countries and values. (2495 · U4 · L17 · 06:49–07:40) (frame)

## Checks the teacher uses

- **Source reliability:** inspect the source’s history, organizations, and external citations or references. Mode: source review outside the rendered artifact. (2495 · U3 · L07 · 01:14–02:08)
- **Reader interest and connection:** ask whether the topic interests the designer and might interest a potential reader; consider whether visual elements engage the reader. Mode: judgement, not a rendered-artifact metric. (2495 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:29)
- **Dataset structure:** check for stray rows above headings, remove unused columns, clarify column names, and keep headings visible while exploring. Mode: inspect the source/workbook, not the rendered artifact. (2495 · U3 · L08 · 01:11–02:27)
- **Scope and selection:** check the latest year, ranking, population threshold, country count, and time window against the intended story; explain those choices. Mode: compare against the data and selection criteria, not visual appearance alone. (2495 · U3 · L08 · 02:27–05:01; 2495 · U3 · L08 · 05:01–08:58; 2495 · U3 · L08 · 08:58–12:22; 2495 · U4 · L15 · 10:30–13:22)
- **Source values and units:** verify values and units in the source, note exceptions such as Turkmenistan’s 2018 GDP value, and confirm units before putting them in the legend. Mode: source-data check. (2495 · U3 · L08 · 12:22–15:43; 2495 · U4 · L15 · 08:18–10:30)
- **Chart structure:** notice when the United States makes the actual-value scatterplot hard to read; test an alternative ordering for contextual axes. Mode: inspect the plot by eye; point separation or overlap can also be measured on a rendered artifact (Derived D1). (2495 · U4 · L11 · 01:31–02:46; 2495 · U4 · L11 · 02:46–04:21)
- **Encoding accuracy:** fix a common minimum and maximum for the radial reference graph, compare each curve to the data grid, and keep corresponding years aligned. Mode: compare the rendered/vector marks with source values; curve lengths and reference positions are measurable, but the course supplies no general tolerance (Derived D1). (2495 · U4 · L12 · 04:22–05:31; 2495 · U4 · L12 · 05:31–07:47; 2495 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–03:01)
- **Guide and label clarity:** inspect line weight, spacing, alignment, overlaps, label exceptions, and whether guide lines stop at their circles. Mode: judgement by eye plus measurable line widths, gaps, and bounding-box collisions in a rendered artifact (Derived D1). (2495 · U4 · L13 · 03:01–05:50; 2495 · U4 · L13 · 06:55–10:10; 2495 · U4 · L14 · 01:00–03:01)
- **Text and legend coverage:** correct typos; explain each visual rule, time period, units, source, and selection; use sample shapes and values. Mode: text presence and units can be checked in the artifact, while whether the explanation is clear needs reader judgement (Derived D1). (2495 · U4 · L15 · 01:26–04:03; 2495 · U4 · L15 · 04:03–08:18; 2495 · U4 · L15 · 08:18–10:30; 2495 · U4 · L15 · 10:30–13:22)
- **Encoding distinctions:** limit colors and explain what they mean; distinguish annual emissions values and the peak-year dot in the legend. Mode: count colors and inspect contrast in the rendered artifact (Derived D1); judge whether marks are distinguishable and meaningful by eye or with readers. (2495 · U4 · L16 · 00:00–01:55; 2495 · U4 · L16 · 05:56–07:42)
- **Data correctness:** check peak years against the data again; the teacher finds and corrects a wrong year for Australia. Mode: compare displayed labels/marks with the data table, not a visual-only check. (2495 · U4 · L16 · 03:34–05:56)
- **External-reader comprehension:** send work in progress with legend and explanatory text to non-expert readers and listen to their feedback. Mode: judgement by readers, not a mechanical artifact measure. (2495 · U2 · L05 · 01:29–02:55; 2495 · U4 · L16 · 10:00–11:15)
- **Fresh-eye and output-size review:** pause, inspect at close and overview zooms, print at intended size, and view on a smartphone to find composition issues. Mode: judgement by eye in the target viewing conditions; visible text size and collisions could be measured from a render (Derived D1). (2495 · U4 · L13 · 03:01–05:50; 2495 · U4 · L17 · 00:00–01:15)

## Examples

- *Crime in Northern Italy: A Visual Exploration* extracts names, crime terms, their proximity, frequency, and relevance from anti-mafia reports across nine cities and eleven years; its rules map type to color/shape, frequency to size, and relevance to height or distance from center. (2495 · U2 · L03 · 03:02–04:23; 2495 · U2 · L03 · 04:23–05:24)
- The crime example’s radial view places crime terms around a city and labels size as frequency and distance from center as relevance. (2495 · U2 · L03 · 04:23–05:24) (frame)
- *From Vincent to Theo* visualizes Van Gogh’s letters with dates, places, and themes, in collaboration with ItaliaNLP Lab. (2495 · U2 · L06 · 01:10–02:01)
- The *La Lettura* population-density example selects the top 40 countries over ten million population, uses one dot per person for density, then adds circle area for population and symbols for region. (2495 · U2 · L05 · 04:17–06:14)
- The population-density figure presents the countries as a grid of dot-filled circles with a dot-count key. (2495 · U2 · L05 · 04:17–06:14) (frame)
- The *La Lettura* weekend-reading context is illustrated with readers’ photographs of the supplement beside breakfast, coffee, and a book. (2495 · U2 · L04 · 01:44–03:32)
- *The Menstrual Cycle* for *Scientific American* combines a circular cycle diagram, explanatory text, and a key for its marks. (2495 · U2 · L06 · 02:01–03:01) (frame)
- *Planet Earth* combines infographics and illustrations for children; examples encode snowflake size and speed, mollusc data, and giant-squid measurements. (2495 · U2 · L06 · 03:01–04:41)
- *Geopolitical Atlas of Water* combines cartography and infographics, with journalists/researchers, a cartographer, and the designer contributing distinct expertise. (2495 · U2 · L06 · 04:41–05:28)
- *The Shape of Dreams* represents Google dream searches with clusters of petal-like shapes; *Key Workers* and *The Stories Behind the Line* are examples of developer collaboration. (2495 · U2 · L06 · 05:28–06:43)
- For visual inspiration, the teacher discusses the branching “diagramism” image, curled line illustration by Tania Alvarez, “All that is gone,” and red coral prints. (2495 · U4 · L10 · 02:44–04:08)
- The worked CO₂ project uses 20 countries, 20 years of per-capita values, and 2019 population, GDP, and annual emissions; the finished design combines ordinal axes, radial curves, circles, labels, a legend, and explanatory text. (2495 · U3 · L08 · 08:58–12:22; 2495 · U3 · L08 · 12:22–15:43; 2495 · U3 · L08 · 15:43–18:34; 2495 · U4 · L17 · 06:49–07:40) (frame)

(2495 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–01:31; 2495 · U4 · L11 · 04:21–06:11)

- **Adobe Illustrator — build the radial curves:** draw a curve with the Pencil tool; rotate and duplicate it; use Transform Again; use the Radar Graph tool with a fixed 0–40 range and add zero values to create a semicircle; paste the data and make the repeated yearly forms for each country. - **Adobe Illustrator — align and refine marks:** use a circular reference grid, adjust curves individually, strengthen selected year lines, distribute and shorten guide lines, recolor selected artwork, and transform the size of each country shape while keeping horizontal and vertical scales equal. - **Adobe Illustrator — typography and labels:** choose a font family with regular, bold, and italic styles; set repeatable label spacing; import GDP/population values, flatten transparency to separate text objects, distribute labels, and state axis ordering, years, and units.
