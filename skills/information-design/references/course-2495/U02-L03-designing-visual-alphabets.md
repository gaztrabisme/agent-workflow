## Data visualization and the information it shapes

Anchor: 2495 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:31

2495 · U02 · L03 · 00:00–01:31

In this lesson, I introduce data visualization and explain how visual alphabets can communicate information and stories. Oxford Languages defines data visualization as a chart, diagram, picture, and so on, created as a visual representation of information.

We are surrounded by data and information. I want to quote Alberto Cairo, a journalist and designer who is the Knight Chair in Visual Journalism at the School of Communication in Miami (captions: *night chair*). He writes: “Unstructured information means reality, the world out there in all its glorious complexity. Every phenomenon that can be perceived or measured can be described as information. Information design aims at ordering this unstructured information. Giving data a structured shape helps us understand the complex reality behind it.” The captions render “this unstructured information” as “disinstructured information.”

As data visualization designers, our goal is to communicate stories and information by giving them a visual form that helps people comprehend them and gain knowledge. I think of my projects as the visual equivalents of articles or stories. Designing a visualization is similar to writing: I write with visual alphabets and, in some cases, experiment with new visual letters.

## Visual variables and chart forms

Anchor: 2495 · U2 · L03 · 01:31–03:02

2495 · U02 · L03 · 01:31–03:02

To define a visual alphabet, we use visual variables: elements that our eyes can see, perceive, and distinguish in order to organize information. Examples include position in space, orientation, shape, size, line thickness, texture, and multiple elements. We can work with variables one at a time or combine them. Choosing and combining them is an essential step in the design process, and I do it often.

Data Viz Project is a useful catalog of possible visual representations and graphics, and it is listed in the course’s additional resources section. Histograms use the length of bars or lines to show numbers (captions: *Instagrams*). If the data or content calls for it, histograms can also be arranged radially. A scatter plot uses position: points are arranged along horizontal and vertical axes. Adding size as another variable makes a bubble chart. We can also combine size and position differently for a timeline (captions: *sides*). I suggest exploring Data Viz Project to see the range of possible solutions.

## From an annual report to a visual model

Anchor: 2495 · U2 · L03 · 03:02–04:23

2495 · U02 · L03 · 03:02–04:23

Next, I want to experiment, as we will do together. I extracted text data from the annual report of the Direzione Nazionale Antimafia, an Italian anti-mafia organization. We will return to this project later to discuss the role of collaboration. I focused on paragraphs about the situation in northern and central Italy. From them, I extracted all the personal names, all terms referring to crimes, and how close crime terms and personal names appeared to one another in the text. I had this information for nine cities and eleven years. For each word, I tracked its frequency in the document and its relevance within the paragraph.

I designed a website that would let people explore these data. It has three sections: people, crimes, and proximity. Color and shape represent the type of word: for example, personal names are red circles and terms are blue squares (captions: *world*). Size represents frequency; height, or distance from the center, represents relevance (captions: *hate*). Defining this legend is an essential step in the design process, and we will work on it during our practical activity.

## Explaining the rules across the project

Anchor: 2495 · U2 · L03 · 04:23–05:24

2495 · U02 · L03 · 04:23–05:24

The legend for the people section groups personal names into cities. It explains that circle size represents frequency and that the element’s height represents its relevance in the document. For each view, I added further information layers. For example, a curved line connects names that appear in multiple cities.

I did the same for the proximity section, grouping terms that occurred next to one another in a paragraph and explaining the same rules I had defined earlier. In the crimes section, each radius represents a crime. Again, the size of an element represents frequency, and its distance from the center represents relevance.
