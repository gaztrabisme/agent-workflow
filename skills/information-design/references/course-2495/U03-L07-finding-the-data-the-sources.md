## The three phases of a data visualization project

Anchor: 2495 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–01:14

This lesson begins with the first phase of a data visualization project: finding a reliable source, exploring it, and selecting data and a story to tell. My usual design process has three phases: choosing sources and a topic, analyzing data, and defining a specific visual model. We will work through all three phases together, starting with the sources.

I often propose the topics for my visualizations, so I keep looking for new stories and datasets. I also have a list of excellent, reliable sources for finding data; it is in the additional resources. For this project I will use Our World in Data (captions: “our word in data”), which I consider an excellent and reliable source. I chose CO₂ emissions because I love how data visualization can help us explore complex, urgent issues.

## Check a source’s reliability

Anchor: 2495 · U3 · L07 · 01:14–02:08

A useful way to assess a source is to look at its history, the organizations associated with it, and especially how and by whom it is cited and referenced. On Our World in Data, I open About and then Audience and Coverage to see how the work is used. The coverage page includes references from the New York Times, the Financial Times, academic papers, books, videos, and conferences. Seeing this range of use makes me comfortable relying on the source.

## Choose a subject that interests you

Anchor: 2495 · U3 · L07 · 02:08–03:19

I like that the website lets me browse datasets by topic. There are many options, so I suggest starting with your own interests and inclinations; working on a subject you care about is a good way to enter this field. I am especially interested in urgent and current issues, so I open Energy and Environment. There are many possible subjects there, and I choose CO₂ emissions.

The site presents charts and datasets in context, with commentary. As I scroll, I find the topic of changing per-capita CO₂ emissions, which I want to explore. The topic menu and a chart with accompanying explanation show how the source links data to questions and stories.

## Explore the per-capita dataset in several forms

Anchor: 2495 · U3 · L07 · 03:19–04:30

Topics are one way to find data; searching by keyword is another. I enter “per capita CO₂ emissions” in the search bar and find several related pages. The site lets me inspect the data in different forms. First, it is plotted on a map, which is a useful way to begin exploring the content. I can also view a chart to see how values change over time, or open a table to see the complete dataset.

When I find the data I want to work with, I download it as a CSV file (captions: “CSB”). I will explain the format in the next lesson. After downloading, I know that per-capita emissions will be my main level of information and the central story I want to visualize.

## Add a second emissions dataset

Anchor: 2495 · U3 · L07 · 04:30–05:38

The per-capita data will be the main information in my visualization, but I also want to build in different layers of analysis about CO₂ emissions. I search for annual CO₂ emissions as well. The source provides this dataset as a map, a timeline chart, and a table. I download it too. The two datasets will let me tell the main per-capita story while adding another layer about total annual emissions. In the next lesson, we will work with both datasets in Google Sheets.
