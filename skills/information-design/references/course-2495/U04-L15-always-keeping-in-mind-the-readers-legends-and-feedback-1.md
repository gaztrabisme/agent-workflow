## Explain why readers need words and a clear legend

Anchor: 2495 · U4 · L15 · 00:00–01:26

I want to build a bridge between the visualization and its readers. For me, designing a visualization is like writing: I write with visual alphabets and sometimes experiment with new visual letters. Readers need tools to understand those alphabets, so a clear legend is essential. It combines visual elements with words.

One misconception is that a good infographic should not need words to be understood. I think that is the wrong approach. Nobody is born data literate. At school, we learn to read and write, but we are not taught how to read graphics. I cannot assume that a reader knows how to read even a simple infographic. That matters even more in my work, because I make complex visualizations with several levels of information.

I take one of my shapes and explain what each visual element represents. We can do that together.

## Explain the curved lines, years, and example values

Anchor: 2495 · U4 · L15 · 01:26–04:03

I start with the curved shapes on the legend layer. I copy one shape, change its color, and write a simple sentence that explains it: the curved lines represent per-capita CO2 emissions from 2000 to 2019. I want the wording to be direct and clear.

The next rule is that each line represents one year. I add text to explain this. I also show a sample country [captions: “Peace is a country”] and fictional data so the reader can see what the numbers mean. I draw two arrows, one to the value for per-capita emissions in 2000 and another to the value for 2019. I will refine the spacing later; for now I am writing down the rules.

I make the years we highlighted earlier italic to show what “each line represents one year” means in more detail.

## Show how line length represents per-capita emissions

Anchor: 2495 · U4 · L15 · 04:03–08:18

The next rule explains what the length of the lines represents: per-capita CO2 emissions, measured in tonnes. I use the United States as a reference because its shape is in the graphic and was resized. I want the reference grid to be precise. When resizing elements, I recommend recording the changes so it is possible to recreate the legend and remember the edits.

I use only part of the shape and show values at 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25. I resize three curves for each value—5, 10, 15, and so on—so the steps are easier for the reader to understand [captions: “three chords”]. I notice one value is missing and add it. I also note the final year, 2019 [captions: “2029 years”]. Then I remove the extra lines, redraw the circles to make a clearer scale, cut away the parts I do not need, and put the values beside their curves. These sample values give readers a visual guide for reading the shapes.

## Explain the circle and its area

Anchor: 2495 · U4 · L15 · 08:18–10:30

The circle is another visual element that needs an explanation. It represents total annual CO2 emissions in 2019, and the area of the circle represents the emissions in tonnes. If I am unsure about a unit, I return to the source spreadsheet and verify the measurement. Checking the units is essential.

For this circle, I do not use a grid to explain every possible size. I will write the actual annual-emissions value beside it, as I did for the 2019 per-capita value. I prepare an example with a random number and explain that the number gives the total annual CO2 emissions [captions: “the envelope CO2 emissions”]. This is another way to help readers understand the shapes and their values.

## Set the legend hierarchy and write the explanatory text

Anchor: 2495 · U4 · L15 · 10:30–13:22

With the main legend elements in place, I organize them by hierarchy. The main rules go in bold. The lines that explain how to read the shapes use the same level of emphasis, while the more detailed instructions for reading values go in italics. These visual rules guide readers through the legend.

I now write text that explains the visualization and the choices behind it. The title is “CO2 emissions per country.” I combine words with visual elements and keep the sentences short, simple, and clear. I encourage readers not to be afraid to use text; it helps them understand what we are working on. First, I say what the graphic shows: per-capita CO2 emissions and total CO2 emissions for 20 countries. I explain how I selected them: they are the 20 countries with the highest per-capita emissions in 2019 among states with populations greater than 5 million people.

I also explain that the per-capita data for each country covers 2000 to 2019 and that the countries are ordered along the horizontal axis by population and the vertical axis by GDP. I add the data source so readers can check it. I align these elements and add separators to divide the legend into clear sections.

Finally, I begin changing the circles' color. I select one circle, then choose Select > Same > Fill Color to select all the circles with that fill. I want a neutral tone, lighter than the color used for the curved shapes. I can refine it later and save this as a new version.
