## Keep readers in view from topic choice onward

Anchor: 2495 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:29

2495 · U02 · L05 · 00:00–01:29

I am going to talk about an essential point: the relationship with our readers. Our main goal is communication, from choosing a topic through to its visual representation. My usual design process has three phases: selecting sources, analyzing data, and defining a unique, specific visual model. We will work through these three phases together.

I also usually propose the topics to visualize, which I enjoy. As data visualization designers, we are people talking to other people. During research and design, I find it useful to begin from a personal point of view and put myself in a potential reader’s shoes. First I ask what would interest me as a reader, then whether the topic could interest someone else too. I will not know for sure, but this is a good way to begin. If you like a topic, feel passionate or curious about it, look for data and see whether you can design a project around it. I will suggest reliable, official sources in the next lessons.

I use the same exercise when I work on the visualization’s aesthetics. I look for visual elements that affect me and consider what effect they might have on readers, so I can design an engaging and pleasing composition. I do not focus on aesthetics only because I like pretty things, though I do. I think visually appealing visualizations can help me create the engaging connection I want with readers and catch their attention.

## Step outside the work and ask for feedback

Anchor: 2495 · U2 · L05 · 01:29–02:55

2495 · U02 · L05 · 01:29–02:55

I want readers to read my pieces, because communication is our goal. Temporarily detaching from a project while it is still in progress is useful. Starting from ourselves can be a good way to approach a project, but at a certain point we have to step outside ourselves and keep our readers in mind.

I send work in progress to my parents or friends and ask for feedback. I need to understand whether my visualizations are clear enough. This feedback stage is important, and I will return to it in our practical activity.

## Be intellectually honest about choices

Anchor: 2495 · U2 · L05 · 02:55–04:17

2495 · U02 · L05 · 02:55–04:17

Another important part of the relationship with readers is being intellectually honest and declaring our starting point of view. Most of my visualizations are exploratory: I am not trying to prove one specific point, but to provide an overview that lets users explore and understand a situation.

Even so, I always have to choose which data to select and visualize, especially in static pieces where I have to deal with physical constraints (captions: *PCs*). Human intervention is inevitable. What matters is declaring that we make choices, and explaining what we select and why, so we can be clear and honest with readers. As data visualization and information designers, this is crucial. I want to show another practical example: a La Lettura project analyzing population density. I used a World Bank source, which provides population-density data for every country; I will return to this source in later lessons.

## Make and explain a population-density visualization

Anchor: 2495 · U2 · L05 · 04:17–06:14

2495 · U02 · L05 · 04:17–06:14

Because La Lettura is a printed supplement, I had to make choices about what to show. I first selected countries with populations over ten million and stated that in the legend. Then I selected the top 40 countries by population density.

I wanted a straightforward visual model to communicate density clearly. I designed one circle for each country. The number of dots inside the circle represents the population data: one dot is one person. I usually work in layers, giving readers different depths of analysis. The first layer here is the number of dots, which shows density. I then added another layer: the circle’s area represents the country’s total population. After that I worked on the overall look and feel, because aesthetic choices affect the engaging connection I am trying to create. Finally, I added symbols for the different geographic areas.

The final piece combines the visualization with a legend and explanatory text. The text explains both what is happening on the page and the choices behind the visualization. Combining the graphic, its legend, and short explanatory text is important: we need to be honest and clear with readers.
