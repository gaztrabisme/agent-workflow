## Collecting visual inspiration

Anchor: 2495 · U4 · L10 · 00:00–01:51

In this lesson, I’m going to show how I look for visual inspiration, especially in worlds outside data visualization. This can help us design our own visual alphabet and find a visual style. The inspiration phase is very important to me. I look for visual inspiration constantly, not only when I start a new project but basically every day. I pay attention to visual elements and notice which shapes and textures attract my eye, because I like to work with them while thinking about the impact they could have on readers.

I usually work on this phase after I have studied the data. Having the content structure in mind helps me find elements I can use in my visualization. I use Pinterest a lot, with many images grouped into boards. This, for instance, is the board called “Visual” (the captions read “the bold vision”), where I collect images that attract my eye. There are not many purely data visualization works in my boards. I want to keep up with what my colleagues are doing, but I also like to draw inspiration from images outside the data visualization world. This helped me find my style, although the research started as a fairly instinctive process. Over the years, I have learned which elements appeal to me, how to find them, and how to replicate them. This process is always in progress.

## Choosing a structure for the data

Anchor: 2495 · U4 · L10 · 01:51–02:44

For this data, I need a shape that can convey information over time, and I need to repeat it for 20 countries on a scatter plot. I am considering small multiples: many small graphs grouped together in one visualization. Small multiples are a useful way to display different variables from a dataset in a compact form and compare them. I think this is the structure I will use, but I want the shapes and elements to feel organic and expressive. Now that I have this representation in mind, I can look for visual inspiration starting from it.

## Mapping the visual variables

Anchor: 2495 · U4 · L10 · 05:08–06:30

The length of the lines will represent the per-capita emissions data. Testing ideas in Illustrator is a common part of my process: I may have an idea, then discover that I need to change it when I test the actual data. That is perfectly fine. It is important not to get too attached to our first ideas and to be able to change our minds.

I will draw the structure again. Population goes on the horizontal axis and GDP on the vertical axis, and the countries will be arranged along those axes. I have decided to use the different curved lines to show the main data, per-capita emissions. Finally, I will add another level of information, total annual emissions, represented by the size of a circle.

These sketches are very ugly, but they help me understand which elements I have and what I can do with the visual variables. The next step is to draw them with a set of tools. I start with the main structure, the scatter plot. In the next lesson, I will show how to make it using an excellent free online tool, RAWGraphs.
