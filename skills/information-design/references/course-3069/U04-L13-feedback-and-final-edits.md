## Getting useful feedback

Anchor: 3069 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–01:30

When you create a visualization, ask someone else to look at it. In data art and data visualization, it is important that another person understands the insight and what the visualization is showing. The person does not need to be a professional: you can show the project to friends or family, perhaps your son or daughter. These may be the people who are most honest about what you have created.

Feedback also helps you check for mistakes in the text or introduction, and whether the viewer understands the introduction and what you want to show. With this information, you can edit the project and make it better. Illustrator has tricks to help check readability. Once your edits are finished, you might add your own logo to make the project more personal and put it out in public. After receiving feedback from one or two friends, return to your computer and finish the edits.

## Showing and adjusting the draft

Anchor: 3069 · U4 · L13 · 01:30–02:38

Showing your work to someone else is a helpful and important part of the process, but it can be scary; it is scary for me, too. With a data visualization, the audience needs to understand what it shows. In this case, the title is clear, but do they understand the explanation, the information, and the data you visualized? Without using too many words, show it to a relative, a friend, or anyone who can give you feedback.

You can also print a version to check whether the contrast is right, the elements are the right size, or anything has accidentally been repositioned. With the feedback, return to the project and add things or make necessary edits. Perhaps you are not satisfied with the contrast of some text. Or the person giving feedback does not understand what you are saying and suggests rephrasing it or making it bigger.

## Checking color vision and contrast

Anchor: 3069 · U4 · L13 · 02:38–03:42

Another thing to keep in mind is checking the project for people who are color-blind. Illustrator has a built-in proof setup that you can choose. It shows two types. Click one to see whether color-blind people can see the work correctly: the contrast needs to be high enough, and they need to be able to decipher what you have drawn. There is another view, too. In that view, the shapes are different enough for people to decode, and the contrast between the elements is high enough. Illustrator has this built-in tool, but you can also search online for other tools or apps that do this.

## Proofreading the poster

Anchor: 3069 · U4 · L13 · 03:42–04:16

Next, check that you have not made spelling mistakes. Since I copied and pasted all the text and titles, this should not be a big problem, though perhaps you typed something by accident. Check that everything is placed correctly, and go through the poster chapter by chapter.

## Adding a creator credit and presenting the finished work

Anchor: 3069 · U4 · L13 · 04:16–05:06

The last thing I think is missing is some text about me, the creator of the project. You can choose whether to add this, but it is what I always do. I added it at the lower right of the project. You do not want it to be too obvious, but you do want to show that you created the project.

Now the project is finished, and we can move to the next exciting part: presenting it to your audience. After the final edits, the next lesson will cover sharing your work online or in print.
