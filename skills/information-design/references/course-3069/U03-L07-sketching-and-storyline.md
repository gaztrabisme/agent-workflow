## From the book to first character marks

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–03:36

3069 · U03 · L07 · 00:00–03:36
Begin by sketching the project's storyline. Start with the book, the characters you have identified, and the data you collected. Since you have read the book, you can also return to information you found online about the book and its story. Write down the characters you think matter. You can treat each character as a whole person or look for a feature that could stand for them.

Dorothy is the little girl with ties in her hair, and she wears a checked blouse; either detail could become a mark. Aunt Em is grey, and there is not much information about her, so a dot might be enough. The Scarecrow is important to the story: he wears a hat and is filled with hay, so you might draw his structure. Toto is a small dog. The sketch does not need to be figurative; a wiggly pattern can suggest his fur. Continue this way for the characters you find in the book.

## Combine the characters through a shared visual language

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L07 · 03:36–05:02

3069 · U03 · L07 · 03:36–05:02
As you sketch the elements, start deciding how to combine them so they work well together. I like circles in a visualization, so I will use circles here too. Dorothy, Toto, and the Scarecrow can each have a circle with a different pattern. Choose the shapes or elements that suit your own visualization. I want to focus on the characters and also consider how their marks can work together in the poster layout. The poster will be landscape, so I am already thinking about what goes where.

## Set out the chapter structure

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L07 · 05:02–06:55

3069 · U03 · L07 · 05:02–06:55
The data shows 18 characters and 24 chapters. To begin the landscape layout, draw 24 bubbles for the chapters. The chapters do not all contain the same number of characters, so their bubbles should not all be the same size. Some can be smaller and some larger to show the differences. The chapters also have different word counts, which can be included as another measure. The first chapter has 373 words, or something around that number. I like moving to the computer at this stage because it lets me think aloud about colour and other choices. The central question is what the visualization should show: here, I want to show how many characters appear in each chapter. The main idea is taking shape; locations might bring more life to the layout, but I will return to that later.

## Look for a style and return to inspiration when stuck

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L07 · 07:43–10:08

3069 · U03 · L07 · 07:43–10:08
To decide on a style, I look online, often on Pinterest or Instagram, for artists, photography, or other references. Data visualization is inspiring on its own, but I also look around for colours and shapes and search for images related to the subject. Ask what comes to mind when you think of a wizard or a little girl, and what colours you associate with the book. Emeralds come up often, so look up what an emerald actually looks like. Search for *The Wizard of Oz* and collect images that inspire you, but find a style of your own.

The process moves back and forth between designing and looking for inspiration. Getting stuck is part of it; one way I get moving again is to revisit images I have collected on Pinterest. They can be about architecture, photography, design, or the subject itself. Search your own collections or look up a subject, then choose colours, shapes, and materials that feel related and that you prefer. It is your project, so you decide what to do with them. Pinterest is one option; Instagram or another method works too. With the sketches and storyline in place, the next lesson turns to method and colours.
