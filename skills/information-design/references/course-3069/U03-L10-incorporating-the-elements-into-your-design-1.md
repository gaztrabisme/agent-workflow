## Build and edit a reusable character symbol

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L10 · 01:14–03:41

Illustrator's Symbols panel is useful when an element will appear many times. If Toto is black now but I later decide he should be brown, I can edit one symbol definition instead of changing every copy in the layout. To make a symbol, select all its parts and drag them into the Symbols panel. I name this one “Ciao, ciao” so I can find it easily among similar symbols. Once it appears in the panel, I can drag instances onto the canvas and copy and paste as many as I need.

There are four instances here. To change their color together, I double-click one and accept the warning that I am editing the symbol definition. In the Layers panel, I can see its components. I change the black background with the Color panel, then change the hair to a white-yellow. When I double-click the canvas to leave symbol editing, every instance has the new appearance. I do not need to repeat the change for each character.

## Count each character within each chapter

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L10 · 03:41–06:15

The symbols represent the characters from the book, including Toto, Aunt Emma, Uncle Henry, and the others. I check the book data to count how many times each character appears in each chapter, then make rows of symbols for the chapters. I counted 16 Dorothys in chapter one, so I drag a Dorothy symbol onto the row and copy it until there are 16. I repeat this for every chapter until the book's data is represented.

It is useful to save screen captures (captions: *screen prints*) of these intermediate stages; they show the process and can also be used in a video about the work. Once all the chapters and characters are on the artboard, I can see how they might fit together. The chapter circle represents the chapter's length in words, and the symbols inside it represent the characters mentioned in that chapter—the chapter's character count (captions: *caravans*). I have filled all the chapter circles with the characters mentioned in each one. I could use rectangles or another shape instead of circles; that choice is up to me and what feels appropriate to the story. The project's overall feeling should connect to the story itself.

## Add chapter numbers, titles, and a typographic hierarchy

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L10 · 06:15–09:11

With the chapter and character layers in place, I can add text or other data. There is still space to use for illustrations, a project title, and other information. I also need to guide readers through the chapters; otherwise, they would see these Petri dishes without knowing the order or what they represent.

I added the chapter number. This example is chapter four, marked by a black circle with the number inside. I put it at the bottom, but I can decide later where it looks best. I also added the chapter title. In other projects I would usually set a title outside the circle, but here I wanted it to fit the circle idea, so I placed it on the circle.

I try different fonts before choosing one, and may change them as I work. I look for fonts that suit the subject because they help create the project's atmosphere. The overall font is a fairly classic one that, in my opinion, fits *The Wizard of Oz*. Readers can choose what they prefer. For the chapter number I chose a very different font from the titles, annotations, legend, and other text. This helps the layers read distinctly, gives emphasis to the most important parts, and guides the audience through the visualization.

## Put chapter text on a path and keep it readable

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L10 · 09:11–12:46

To put a chapter title on a circle, I make another circle with no fill and no stroke. I copy the straight text, select the circle, and use the type-on-a-path tool. Illustrator offers several ways to fit the text. I try skew, but it looks strange; I use the rainbow option, center the text on the circle, and flip it. Then I adjust the spacing until the text sits in the middle. I can also make the circle smaller to fit the chapter layout.

In the Layers panel, I made one layer for the black text and another copy in the background color. Moving the background-colored copy over the chapter title works like a mask. I repeat this process for all the chapters.

Some chapter titles are too long to read comfortably around a circle. I split those into two parts rather than make readers turn their heads or read upside down or sideways. I copy the text into a wider circle and move it until it looks natural. Some titles fit in one sentence and some are split in two.

## Let the layout change as new information is added

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L10 · 14:18–16:11

Now that this data is in place, I can add more layers. I am not satisfied yet because the page still looks a bit empty. I could add information about the characters: not only how often they are mentioned in each chapter, but how often each character appears in the whole book. There is space for that, although the layout will probably change as I work. I can add information between chapters or somewhere else, then rearrange the elements as needed.

This is the final arrangement I chose, but during the process it moved around a lot, and at one point I had legend elements on the right. The work goes back and forth: what I add next may lead me to move things or remove something and rearrange everything. Next I will bring in the other data we found in the book, highlighted and recorded in the Excel sheets: the total number of appearances for each character.

## Scale character symbols to their total mentions

Anchor: 3069 · U3 · L10 · 16:11–18:44

I bring the graph made from the character data onto the artboard. It is a grouped layer, so I ungroup it. I release the clipping masks and remove the parts I will not need, repeating the selection and release steps until only the useful information remains.

I keep a reference circle marked 400. That is not actual data; it gives me a known size to compare against while scaling, so I remember what the circle represents and avoid mistakes. I add labels so I know which character goes with each circle (captions: *Orange down*). Then I can compare the circle sizes: they represent how many times each character is mentioned in the book.
