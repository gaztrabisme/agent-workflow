## Start with a question and understand the data

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L05 · 01:50–03:24

The infographic began with that question. We researched the subject and found a dataset listing wars in the 20th and 21st centuries, along with a comprehensive study of battle deaths. The data included each conflict, its start and end years, continent, number of deaths, and duration. Looking across the numbers, what stood out was the human sacrifice: 95 million deaths in just over a century. That was the figure I carried into the next stages. I wanted to show when the wars happened, but the people and the number who died carried the weight of the story for me.

## Encode the numbers and validate the chart

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L05 · 03:24–05:11

I used size to encode the number of people who died: each bubble represents a conflict, and its size represents the number of lives lost. The bubbles are placed along the x-axis by time and along the y-axis by duration. After working out the encoding system, I moved into Excel to chart and compare the data.

![3601 · U2 · L05 · 03:54](frames/U02L05-F0354.00.jpg)

On screen: “Charting the data and validating the sketch” / “conflicts duration” / “timeline” [rest omitted]  
Shows: A bubble chart plots conflict duration against a timeline; the bubbles vary in size.

The wars span the whole century, and wars in the second half generally lasted longer, as the bubbles rise. One conflict is an outlier in position: it had lasted more than six decades when I made the graphic, and now it has lasted more than seven. The conflicts are positioned at the time they ended, because that is when we count the human sacrifice. By bubble size, the Great War and the Second World War stand out.

To validate the sketch, I checked whether the scales worked. I changed the vertical duration axis from linear to logarithmic. A linear scale goes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. A logarithmic scale helps when the values span a broad range and cluster at the smaller end; it opens the scale by multiplying by a factor such as 2 or 10. Here we used a factor of 2, giving 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. This spreads the values out: at a duration of 15 years, there is less overlap and the war patterns are clearer. But the scale loses an important outlier, Israel and Palestine.

Shows: Two versions of the bubble chart compare the linear duration scale with a logarithmic scale.

## Find a visual language for the data

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L05 · 05:11–07:50

The next question was how to show that each bubble represents people who died in war. I looked for references and built a moodboard to guide the visual language, or visual lexicon, for the scatterplot. I first searched for war infographics and data visualisations. This was partly to avoid repeating what others had already done and partly to understand which visual signs had been used. We want to communicate immediately, and knowing earlier approaches can inform the encodings. I usually look for inspiration in the data itself, which could be about science, nature, art, or popular culture.

I returned to my sketchbook to explore this richer visual lexicon. The stem was a new element: it indicates the conflict, the flower's starting point on the timeline, and the moment the war began. Moving it horizontally and vertically shows duration and the passage of time along both axes. The stem also helps overcome a limitation of the logarithmic scale. I prototyped the new visual system and arrived at a field of commemoration. Bringing lines into the scatterplot focuses attention on the second half of the century, an important pattern in the data.

## Turn the visual system into a story

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L05 · 07:50–10:45

The finished image challenges the idea that 20th-century wars are mainly the two world wars: it shows war as a constant across the last century. It also suggests that the planet is becoming more peaceful. If regional distribution were the priority, I might use another colour encoding. The different reds for Africa, Asia, or Europe do not stand out; they blend together. I was willing to sacrifice immediate regional recognition because region is a background story and I wanted readers to connect with the poppy metaphor. Giving the flowers different hues would weaken the link between red, violence, and blood.

## Design, empathy, and the limits of data

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L05 · 10:45–12:49

The aim of data design is to give numbers a voice and create empathy, engaging audiences on both rational and emotional levels. Design can help us see through the eye and the brain, feel the implications of data, and recognise the human stories behind a visualisation.

![3601 · U2 · L05 · 10:58](frames/U02L05-F1058.00.jpg)

On screen: “empathy” / “seeing with the eyes, with the mind, and with the heart”  
Shows: Museum visitors look at a wall display of the poppy project beneath a statement about empathy.

A successful infographic finds a form that matches the function of the data, gives form to stories hidden in the numbers, and communicates those stories emotionally. Design can reach both our analytical and emotional brains, making us feel and sense the implications of the data. That is the power of design: transforming statistics into stories that matter.

As we look for a dataset and try to give it a voice, we must remember what the data stands for: the people behind the numbers and the stories represented. We must also ask how close the numbers come to the truth of reality. Statistics are only a footprint of experience. They can bring us closer to understanding the world, but they cannot fully capture its complexity. René Magritte's painting reminds us: this is not a pipe, but a representation of a pipe. A data visualisation can bring us closer to understanding the pipe, but cannot show it in all its dimensions.

![3601 · U2 · L05 · 12:30](frames/U02L05-F1230.00.jpg)

On screen: “Ceci n'est pas une pipe.” [This is not a pipe.] / “The Treachery of Images, 1929” / “René Magritte”  
Shows: Magritte's painting depicts a pipe above the sentence “Ceci n'est pas une pipe.”

That concludes this introduction to data and design and to design's potential to give data form and reveal hidden stories. In the next lesson, we will start looking for a dataset.

### Key points (derived)

- Begin with a question, understand the dataset, then build and validate an encoding system.
- A visual metaphor can connect patterns in the data to a human story, while design choices also require trade-offs.
- Data visualisations represent experience; they can support understanding and empathy without capturing the whole of reality.
