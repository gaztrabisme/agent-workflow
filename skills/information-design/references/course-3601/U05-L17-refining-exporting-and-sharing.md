## Prepare the infographic for publication

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 00:09–01:30

Now that the infographic is complete, I want to show how to export it for publishing and think about ways to share it with a wider audience. That could mean social media, or perhaps a different format that brings in interactivity.

After reviewing the work in progress on paper and testing the infographic with three people, I decided to refine a few elements. The numbers did not make clear what they represented, and the key did not explain itself; both needed to work harder. There was also a disconnect between the key and legend and the way the information was actually shown. Direct labels were working well, so I thought the global figure should be brought inside the glass too.

The map felt as if it were floating, and its color and style clashed with the rest. It needed to look like a separate insight while still belonging to the same visualization, the same world. The two other visualizations show progress, but this map looks only at 2020, the latest data point. Its main purpose is to show that we do not have data for many nations.

## Add contrast and a subtle dimensional effect

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 01:30–02:24

I added a background color, which made it possible to call out the white space: the remaining gaps between each country’s value and 100%. To create a subtle embossed effect, I joined the outer and inner axes and turned them into a shape. It is easy to do and remains editable.

I gave the shape a gray Multiply effect and an inner glow. To apply it, draw a shape, then choose Effect, Stylize, Inner Glow. Set Multiply to 14. The effect makes it feel as if there is a hole in the page.

## Clarify the labels, legend, and bars

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 02:24–03:13

I added annotations to clarify how the percentage relates to each country name and to show that this view is only for 2020. I also made the color encoding for the numbers more detailed. Before, I used only blue and red to show a positive or negative change. The key also identified no change, so I labeled some countries in gray and used gray for numerical values where there was no change.

I also spelled out what the bars mean. User testing showed this was missing: people could be confused about why 2020 appeared there.

## Give the typography a clearer hierarchy

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 03:13–03:46

I used Playfair, another Google font, for the subheaders and main title, and Lato for the different styles of labeling.

## Keep the color meanings clear across the views

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 03:46–05:07

The map’s color system fit much better with the direction of the rest of the infographic, but the colors have different meanings in the two views. On the map, light blue represents the level of the population that had access to water in the past, and the gradient moves from where we were to where we got to. In the glasses, the color steps represent the percentage of the population. Boxing the map out makes clear that it is a different view; in the glasses, I wanted to keep the same meaning throughout.

When I printed the design, the glasses felt too heavy with the large, dark gradient. The gradient there marks only the increase between 2000 and 2020, not the total access to water at either point. I added a light gradient over the shape to emphasize just the change from where we were to where we got to. It also helps separate the three income groups into layers.

## Organize the file and embed its assets

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 05:07–06:18

When I am happy with the design and it is ready to deliver, I clear out unnecessary layers and make sure there are no hidden layers that are not needed. I also make sure the assets are embedded. An asset is embedded when it has no link icon; another asset in this file is still linked. To embed it, click the icon and choose Embed from the menu in the Layers panel or the toolbox. I do the same for the other glass.

For a book or magazine, publishers will usually ask for all the copy on a separate layer. They will probably place the work inside a larger InDesign file, where they can handle the copy separately. Group the copy on one layer. Here, I can move the visual elements down with the rest of the dendrogram.

## Add grain and explain the missing-data note

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 06:18–07:18

I also added a couple of touches to the illustration. The blue gradient now has a grain effect, which brings it closer to the Photoshop file and makes it feel like part of the same family. Select the shape and apply the effect; you can edit it afterward. It is subtle: the intensity is 10 and the contrast is 35.

The last note is the caption that explains the entries with missing data for 2000. In the lesson about knowing your data, I filled gaps for countries that did not have data for 2000 but did have data for 2020, using the earliest available years. I put an asterisk beside those countries and added a clarification of what the asterisk means.

## Save the source files and export an image

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 07:18–08:00

Once the design is ready, save it. I usually save an Illustrator file, an EPS file, which is a vector file (captions: *not a vector file*) that can be opened in other vector-editing tools, and a PDF. Then choose Export As. You can export the entire artboard as a PNG or JPEG, at high resolution. CMYK is for print and RGB is for screen, so choose RGB (captions: *vOS*) for a screen image.

## Show the process and picture the work in context

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 08:00–09:10

To reach a broader audience, think of the journey as being as important as the destination. When presenting the work, do not focus only on the finished artwork. Show the steps that got you there: the behind-the-scenes work, sketches, and anything that demonstrates your process and problem-solving.

It is also useful to show what the project could look like in context. This infographic was designed for a book, so I could find a mockup of an open book. I could also show it on a screen, such as an iPad or laptop. Instead of simply placing the artwork as it is into different formats, I could ask what would happen if it became interactive.

## Build a short story for a microsite

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 09:10–10:00

An interactive display is an easy place to start brainstorming. The infographic could become a microsite with a splash screen that introduces the story, followed by an animation through the global statistics. In the last two decades, the share of the global population with access to safe drinking water rose from 62% in 2000 to 74% in 2020. Even so, one in four people worldwide still do not have access. That could work as a short animated introduction.

As people scroll, the next part could explore the relationship between income and access to safe drinking water through the glass metaphor. There could be two glasses side by side that fill up, one for each year, as people move through the four income levels. Or there could be one glass animated from one year to the other.

## Explore other media and ways to bring the metaphor to life

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 10:33–11:19

The “what if” question can apply to the medium as well as the format. After exploring a vector-based infographic, I could ask what it would look like if it were hand-illustrated or expressed another way.

That brings to mind Sentinel, a projected data visualization used in a theater to talk about climate change. We used rich, tactile, tangible visuals to show sea-level change. I can imagine bringing even the simple glass metaphor to life with photography, animation, or a video of a glass being filled.

## Connect the work with people and organizations

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L17 · 11:19–12:47

Ultimately, the aim is to connect the work with other people and help them see it. For the water infographic, I could launch it around March 22, World Water Day, when there is already a lot of attention on the subject. I could tag those organizations or contact them directly. We never know what might happen: an organization might use the assets and the work to amplify the story and extend its reach. It is worth trying. Use the internet and social media to connect with people.

These ideas apply to commercial work as well as self-initiated projects. After delivering a project, think about how it could generate new business: what if it could be brought to life in a different format or connected with another organization? That can open up more opportunities. I hope these ideas make you excited to explore other formats. In the next lesson, we will talk about the power of collaboration.
