## Refining the dendrogram strokes

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L13 · 00:54–03:50

The first step is to make change over time more prominent: the reader should be able to compare 2000 with 2020. I give the first stroke the palest colour in the palette. This makes the countries’ high coverage easier to see against the gap in the other half of the visualisation. I select objects with the same fill colour, then delete the dots, both blue and orange, because they become distorted when wrapped around the circle. Then I give the progress stroke a subtle gradient, which suggests change. In Illustrator, I can apply a gradient to the stroke and use Option while changing the endpoints with swatches so the gradient always comes from the centre.

I colour the connector lines with the same pale colour and test different line-width profiles. A line can start and end small, or start small and become larger, like a water drop. I also try two bulges to suggest the two decades, but the shape looks odd on short segments. A rounded profile is another option, but it extends the visible line past the segment’s actual endpoint: the centre of the line and the visible edge no longer match. I revert to the straight profile because it shows the differences more accurately. I give the orange segments the same profile and apply a gradient using the swatches. The gradient does not run from the centre when I miss the required option, so I reselect those strokes and set it correctly.

## Choosing a colour scale for missing coverage

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L13 · 05:52–08:48

The map originally uses a diverging colour scale: it runs between two ends of a spectrum and interpolates several hues. A diverging scale can show values that move in opposite directions, such as high and low values; it is effective for showing where coverage is low. But I do not want another diverging scheme, because the dendrogram already uses one. I want to signal reduced access with red, using a sequential scale that keeps the same hue and changes its lightness.

I have tested a few options. To change a colour quickly, select it, open the swatches and replace it with the colour you want. The darkest blue in the swatches is the 2020 colour in both the dendrogram and area chart, so keeping it consistent gives the design a shared language. The map does not need another lightness scale to show population percentage, because the bar lengths already encode those percentages; the map can introduce a separate encoding system. This version works, but it still clashes somewhat with the other elements.

One option is to colour the 0–25% bars with a blue tint. That could produce too many differences among the bars, while the shortest bars—the ones that need the most contrast to be visible—would be the lightest. I do not think that will work. Another option is a black-and-white scale. It avoids confusion about what the blues mean in this different encoding system and could make the blank areas stand out. We would not lose the data: even without a heat map, the same information is plotted in the dendrogram above. These are options to consider; as the visual display fills in, a solution may become clear. I also turn the global-trend lines white so that they are easier to see.

## Developing the tap illustrations

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L13 · 08:48–10:32

I have already explored some illustration ideas, as the mood boards and online references show. They helped me decide whether the taps should be large or small, the same size or different sizes, and whether they should share a shape. Assets found through Google Images or Pinterest can be more than placeholders: they can inspire the visual style.

I quickly draw vector versions in two styles. One is flat; the other uses perspective to add a sense of three dimensions and movement. It looks more like a splash of water from an open pipe than a polished tap with water flowing out. I like using a different tap in each position, one flat and one in perspective, and continue testing how they look. After sketching by hand, scanning and colouring the drawings, I place them in the visualisation. Their forms begin to merge with the other elements, so I refine them in a final pass.

## Adding hand-drawn taps and a glass

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L13 · 10:32–11:13

The final tap treatment is the one I am aiming for. You do not need a hand-drawn visualisation or any illustration in your infographic. Choose the medium that fits: collage, photography, vector illustration, or something else. I like illustration because it offers a shortcut into the story and gives the data a more human personality.

Once the illustration is settled, I make the glass too and place both elements in the layout. I export the glass as a TIFF or PSD with a transparent background. That way, when it sits on top of the rest of the artwork, the elements underneath still show through.

## Polishing labels around the fold

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L13 · 11:13–12:26

Before the next class, I will polish the typography now that the layout and positions are set. Labels must not fall in the centre fold. Some can be shortened—for instance, “USA.” I can shorten labels that contain a number as well, or break a label over two lines. I also do not like labels facing up or down, so I rotate them to read in a consistent direction. Both the numbers and the names need rotating. Move the cursor near a selection until it becomes the rotation cursor, then hold Shift to snap to 180 degrees. It takes a few minutes, but it is worth the effort. The rotation can start around the midpoint; in these examples, I use Ireland and Pakistan as the starting places.

## Removing unchanged segments

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L13 · 12:26–13:26

Finally, clean up the green segments that Data Illustrator added to indicate no change. A one-point difference can produce a green segment even though the values are effectively unchanged. Snap the point into place, then delete the remaining green segments by selecting their stroke colour. Once the elements are in place, the next step is to add the annotations and copy that readers need to understand the visualisation. In the next lesson, let's spell it out.
