## Build a visual language from the subject

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L09 · 00:09–03:18

Now that we know the data, we can shift from investigating it to explaining it and communicating its insights. A simple tool for this is sketching with pen and paper: it is inexpensive, fast, and analogue. This is one of my favorite steps because it lets me connect the data, its stories, and design, then begin to build a visual grammar for communicating the numbers.

The process is free-form. You can sketch while analysing data, or first spend some time assembling a mood board. I keep looking for references throughout the process, usually beginning with the subject itself. For this project, access to clean water makes me think about drinking, washing hands, taking a shower, and many other everyday needs (captions: “CUT!” and “War.”). These needs connect to the simple action of turning a tap on and off. As I build references, the tap becomes one image. I also look at familiar objects and actions, such as a water drop and a glass that can be half full or empty. Familiar images can give viewers a quick route into what the data says.

I also look for patterns that can translate numbers into shapes, sizes, and positions. Water can take many forms: a line, a drop, a circle, or a sphere expanding from its centre. Those references can take a mood board in several directions. When connecting the imagery to data, it helps to see the possible chart forms in front of you. The Financial Times visual vocabulary is useful because it gathers many charts on one page. To navigate them, think about the variables in the dataset: change over time, magnitude, or comparisons, as we discussed in the previous lesson.

You can start a mood board by searching for the subject in Google Images or Pinterest. I like working with newspaper so that the different explorations are visible together and I can assess what works and the decisions I have made. Design involves decisions at every step.

## Compare change over time across countries

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L09 · 03:18–07:00

For the clean-water project, an important part of the story is not only where each country is now, but where it was in the past. A common way to show change over time is to put a timeline on the horizontal axis, from the starting point to the point we reach. A line chart or area chart lets us compare the start and end and see whether the overall trend increased or decreased. This could be a good starting point for describing the global trend.

But what if we include all 138 nations? Rather than showing every year across the two decades, we can reduce the complexity and show only the start and end. That is a slope chart. The vertical scale runs from zero to 100 percent of the population with access to clean water (captions: “clear water”); the horizontal axis marks 2000 and 2020. Some countries begin high and remain high. Most of Europe had access two decades ago and still has it. Other countries begin at about 20 percent and reach 35 or 40 percent. A few unfortunately go down. Those are outliers: cases moving against the broader story of progress around the globe.

A slope chart can work well with pre-attentive processing, making it easier to notice patterns of increase and decrease. Colour could reinforce the direction of change: blue for increases and black or red for decreases. With 138 lines, though, labels could become cluttered. One way to reduce this is to break the chart into small multiples by region, with a separate chart for Europe, the Americas, Asia (captions: “Indonesia”), Africa, and other regions. Keep the axes consistent: zero to 100, with 2000 and 2020 in each small chart. The result reveals clusters. Most of Africa is quite low on the scale, unfortunately, and the lines open toward the middle; the Americas are more distributed, with South America around the middle and North America higher; Europe is mostly high, with a few outliers. Even in small multiples, the lines may still be difficult to label.

## Connect chart marks to water imagery

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L09 · 07:00–09:50

When I look at the sketch, I also think about the visual form it is starting to create. This is where I can connect the chart to metaphors in the mood board. I bring the glass reference into the chart, making a link between the data and a familiar image. The limitation remains that many lines will be close together and overlap, making individual countries hard to label. I want a system that separates the lines so they can be seen individually.

If angle and direction are not the only attributes we can use, perhaps we can use size. We could compare a circle for 2000 with a circle for 2020, or show two drops whose sizes change. The drop might even split. Another option is length. Length is one of the strongest visual attributes for connecting what we see to data because people can judge it accurately. If I draw a line twice as long as a segment, I can make it roughly double. If I draw a circle twice the size, it becomes more of a guessing game: I have to imagine how many copies of the smaller circle fit into the larger area, or how it would fit when stretched.

For two time periods and a measure that changes, I am drawn to length more than size because I want to represent small variations accurately. Area can work when the numbers span a broad range and exact precision matters less; with percentages on a finite scale, I would probably use lines (captions: “Errors”). For each country, I use two lines: one for 2000 and one for 2020, showing the percentage of the population with access. Even though the range is zero to 100, I draw the scale from the top down because water starts at the top and comes down.

## Reduce clutter and organise the country marks

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L09 · 09:50–13:37

The progress we want to show is the difference between 2000 and 2020. Rather than repeat the starting value and the full comparison on each mark, we can remove some visual noise and use a segment for each country. Position it on the vertical axis from where the country started to where it reached. Some segments are short, as for much of Europe, where there was little change. Some countries made considerable progress but remain low on the scale; others are higher. Each segment can have a country label.

The next decision is how to organise the information. The LATCH framework we discussed in the first lesson offers ways to structure it. We could arrange the lines by location or alphabetical order, and we need to decide how to cluster them. I am thinking about a dendrogram as a shape because it can show hierarchy. It could branch into Africa, Asia, Europe, and the Americas, with the countries’ lines positioned on the same zero-to-100 scale from 2000 to 2020. In this sketch, Asia would be lower, Europe would be mostly at 100 percent, and the Americas would be mixed.

There is something the visual landscape is still missing: how much of the container is filled, as in a glass that is full or half full. If we show only the progress, we do not show the filled part. We can link the progress to the top so that the sketch shows where each country was and where it got to, while revealing the white space. That unfilled space is part of the story: how far does the bar have to go before it reaches 100 percent? We can mark the countries that went down with a different colour. Their bars show the reverse direction from 2000 to 2020. This shape also makes me think the connection could come from one shared source: the one percent of water on the planet available to us.

## Turn the rough marks into a working storyboard

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L09 · 13:37–15:13

These sketches are rough. Their purpose is not beauty but the conceptual value they bring to the workflow. Once I understand the variables and how they might connect to the mood boards, I can develop a richer, more complete encoding system. The next sketch is more defined, but it is still about function rather than beauty: it shows the journey from a rough execution toward a storyboard for the design.

As I think about a tap with two worlds, another important part of the story is income—wealth (captions: “warmth”). Regional distribution is one layer, but the GDP makes the story clearer. In this sketch I divide the world into high-income countries on the left and the others on the right. The format begins to resemble a book spread because the brief is for an infographic in a book, similar to the infographic History of the World. I want a title and a short introduction, and to label every country so the viewer can see whether the trend from 2000 to 2020 was positive or negative. Within the two groups, I can still show the geographical distribution.

## Test layouts, colour, and complexity

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L09 · 15:13–17:37

In the next exploration, I divide the world into regions and put high-income countries on the left of each tab and the rest of the world on the right. This could reveal, for example, that Africa has countries only in the rest-of-world group and no high-income countries. I am not sure this approach emphasises the binary distinction enough, so I return to the previous sketch and reconsider how to group the two income groups by geography and lay them out, perhaps alphabetically.

I also test linking countries with colour. The sketch becomes a dead end: when I add colour to tag countries, I immediately lose the water reference. I want to manage the information with one colour or only a few. Two diverging colours could distinguish positive change in blue from negative change in red, pink, or orange. Different shades of blue might also distinguish countries and connect them across a mural-like layout, but a darker blue could imply a quantitative value, such as more access, when I only mean to link the continents. In another exploration I use one colour and let darkness indicate progress. I also consider adding the number of people without access around the outer circumference, since the chart currently shows only the percentage with access. For the book, however, I do not want to overwhelm the audience with complexity, so I choose a layout with just this layer of data.

## Use the sketch as a roadmap and validate it

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L09 · 17:37–18:45

As the sketches show, sketching with data is a way to unlock creative thinking and solve problems, not to make a final product. Its purpose is to give you a roadmap for the work ahead. If you do not know what you are designing, it is impossible to start the next design step. Sketching also exposes practical needs: for instance, I need gaps in the display, so I may have to add blank rows to the data sheet before plotting it.

The sketch and its data encoding need to be validated. I may have a sketch, but I cannot tell whether it works until I plot the data. Now we have a plan and an idea to execute, so let's prototype it to see whether it works. In the next lesson, we will use prototyping tools to validate our sketch.

### Key points (derived)

- Begin with the subject and the data together: familiar images can help viewers recognise the story, while chart forms encode the variables.
- Test chart structures by sketching: a slope chart or small multiples can reveal country-level change, and grouping or changing the mark can reduce clutter.
- Choose visual encodings that fit the precision required. Length is easier to compare accurately than area, especially for percentages on a 0–100 scale.
- Use rough sketches to decide hierarchy, grouping, labels, colour, and data preparation needs, then plot the data to validate the design.
