## Start with a niche and build skills through collaboration

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L18 · 00:09–00:41

In this closing lesson, I want to share why collaboration matters. Data design sits at the intersection of science and art, and working across so many different fields can feel intimidating when you are starting out. There are many things to learn. My advice is to find a niche, choose something you enjoy, and then extend your skillset by collaborating with others from the beginning.

## Combine your strengths with those of specialists

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L18 · 00:41–01:46

At first, I thought not being able to code was a limitation. After 15 years of working with data, I have understood that my strength is problem-solving: creating a visual language that is unique to the data and to the audience I am speaking to. I can extend my abilities by working with expert developers who know their craft so well and can bring my ideas to life in incredible ways.

One of my latest explorations is a sensory visualization of COVID cases in London and the impact of COVID on the city, called London Under the Microscope. It came from a great collaboration with a developer, R-Portray, and a sound engineer, Duncan Gere, who was able to sonify the data. We made the project by bringing different disciplines together. My creative vision came to life through that collaboration, and I could never have reached that kind of outcome with my own skillset alone.

## Learn the subject matter with a collaborator

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L18 · 01:46–02:52

When you work with data, you are unlikely to be an expert in every subject area because you may work across many fields. Working with experts lets you learn those subjects. That is one of the enjoyable parts of data design: you are constantly learning.

In the example the captions call “AudityVs,” a visual deconstruction of David Bowie’s “Space Oddity,” I reached out to Miriam Quick, a musicologist. I shared an initial idea: what if we used data visualization to show the craft of David Bowie? Could we reveal how masterful that craft was, reveal its many layers, and celebrate the artist through data visualization? Miriam’s response was, “Yes, let’s do this.” We explored the music and the many layers of “Space Oddity,” and I learned a great deal about music because Miriam is an expert in that field.

## Collaborate to stay motivated and get past blockers

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L18 · 02:52–03:30

Collaboration can also keep you motivated when you are doing self-initiated research. Having someone who keeps you accountable or inspires you can fuel the work.

With MeToomentum, we tried to analyze the MeToo movement and the first six months of social-media conversations. Working with the brilliant developer Lucia Konchenkova helped me get past some of the project’s blockers. We could discuss the work together, think through the implications of the data, and come up with solutions together. It gives you strength.

## Work with clients and partners from the planning stage

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L18 · 03:30–04:28

For projects that are not self-initiated, including commercial work, collaboration is important when planning, setting strategy, and thinking about the data. It helps expose gaps in what we know. It is important to work with clients or whoever we are partnering with, and to share both what we know and what we do not understand about the data.

We cannot design without a clear understanding of the material we are working with. Treat any project as a collaborative project, whether that collaboration happens during execution or at the beginning while we are trying to understand what the data means.

## Find communities and people who can help you grow

Anchor: 3601 · U5 · L18 · 04:28–05:48

As you learn to work with data, consider extending your skillset by partnering with an animator, a developer, or an expert in a particular field. Reach out.

You might also look at the Data Visualization Society. It is a volunteer-run, community-based project that organizes conferences such as Outlier and gives you a way to take part in the data-visualization community. Software such as Datawrapper or Tableau also has lively communities that provide feedback and support.

You can join workshops or hackathons too. I have participated in a couple of Data4Change workshops. They were great opportunities to grow, learn about different challenges, and collaborate with many people from different backgrounds and disciplines. You may be surprised by the magic that happens when you join forces with other people. You can plant an idea in someone else’s head (captions: *somebody’s house head*) and see it grow into an unexpected result (captions: *an expected result*).

You do not need to know everything when you start. Learning is a journey, and I feel that I am constantly learning too. Enjoy it, keep experimenting, stay hungry and curious, and be proactive about reaching out to people who may be able to help.
