## Create a dataset or use one already available

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L06 · 00:00–01:02

There are two ways to find data. You can create your own dataset from an experience that interests you, such as phrases from a movie like The Shining or a song like “Space Oddity” by David Bowie. Data is a definition of an experience, and that is how I approached these two projects. More commonly, you can find a dataset that is already available. For this project, I recommend starting with an existing dataset, while choosing a subject you care about. Because this is a self-initiated project, you can also create your own dataset if that is what interests you.

## Turn an interest into a question

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L06 · 01:02–02:01

Finding an available dataset starts with deciding what question you want to answer. For my final project, the question is: How many people have access to safe drinking water? The idea came from looking at an image. We often call Earth the blue planet and say that water covers 70% of its surface. But when you compare the total volume of water with the volume of the planet, you see that there is not that much water. Only 1% of all water is available for people to consume. That gives me a starting point for investigation; now I need data that can answer the question.

## Search precisely and assess results

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L06 · 02:01–03:09

I begin with a simple online search for “access to safe drinking water.” Be as specific as possible: even several keywords can return millions of results. Search for access to safe drinking water rather than just “water” or “safe water.” Avoid results marked as advertisements. Open a few links to understand what is behind them; you cannot assess the content from a result title alone. As you work in this field, you will also become familiar with organizations that regularly publish data. I have included a list of common places to look in the course resources.

## Compare sources and find the primary data

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L06 · 03:09–04:40

I open the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the World Health Organization (WHO), UNICEF, and Our World in Data. Our World in Data is a secondary source: it does not produce the data, but publishes analysis and detailed insight into data produced elsewhere. Its Clean Water summary is a long explanation of access to clean water. It also covers the distribution of clean water and links the subject to health and mortality (captions: “half, immortality”). Its population-share material also links the subject to GDP and poverty and lets me see change over time. The dataset I want is the share of the population with access to safely managed drinking water. The source points to washdata.org, a WHO/UNICEF collaboration called the Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP). After opening the JMP site, I check the other sources. UNICEF points to the same 2021 research, while CDC uses the same dataset but references an older 2019 release. I want to explore the primary source.

## Inspect the JMP data, report and methodology

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L06 · 04:40–06:16

In the household section, open the data and look at its level of detail. It includes drinking water, sanitation and hygiene, but I want to focus on drinking water and choose between definitions such as basic and safely managed. Download the report as well as reading third-party analysis such as Our World in Data. The report and methodology explain how the data was collected and treated. Its summary shows trends, geographic distribution, and gaps: much of the world is not covered. Once I understand the material and am ready to explore the data, I can use the household data for the world and download the dataset. For more guidance on researching data and vetting its source, see the resources.

## Define audience, purpose and channel

Anchor: 3601 · U3 · L06 · 06:16–09:22

Before opening and analyzing the data, define the brief. I start projects by writing notes in a sketchbook and answering who the audience is, what I am trying to do, and why. Without knowing who we are speaking to, we cannot find the right way to communicate. For this project, the audience is a curious, global reader of another book I am working on. The infographic will serve the book and should be accessible to a wide range of readers, not only people who already know statistics.

The next question is purpose: why visualize this dataset? Is the goal to persuade, inform, or entertain? Here, I mainly want to inform and enlighten by revealing an invisible aspect of the subject. Especially in the Western world, people may not think about how precious water is because it is available every day, whenever we want it, from a tap (captions: “WhatsApp”).

The final question is where the infographic will appear: what is the channel? If you are creating something for social media, write that down. The channel affects the tone of voice and the visual exploration. Communicating on social media differs from communicating in a book. I am making an infographic for a book, so readers will have time to explore the information and details; attention usually lasts longer than in an interactive or social-media experience. Understanding the brief is fundamental before deciding what the data says, continuing the research, and deciding how to communicate the what. I answer who, why, and where before the what and how. Write down and answer these questions; take time to understand the brief because it will inform the next steps. In the next lesson, we will open the dataset, prepare it, clean it, and begin looking for its stories.
