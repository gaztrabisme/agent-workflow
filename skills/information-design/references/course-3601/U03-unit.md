# Unit 3 · Working with Data

Anchor: 3601 · U3

Now that you know the basics, it's time to get started with the project! In this unit, we'll wear the hat of the detective. By the end, you'll have defined your brief and established a dataset to work with. You'll also learn how to uncover the insights and create an idea to execute. You can follow all these steps using the same dataset as me, but I encourage you to base your project on a dataset that inspires you.

## Tasks

- ### Research: What Subject Do You Wish to Explore? 
Practice exercise 3\.1

A helpful way to find a subject you wish to explore is to brainstorm some questions you want to discover the answer to. These questions can be about anything. Here is a useful exercise to kick start investigative research: replace the text in brackets by tapping into your curiosity.

· Which country has the highest/lowest \[GDP per capita]?

· Which part of the world is the most \[populated]?

· Which governments are \[big spenders]?

· What is the most common \[name in the world]?

· What are the \[most polluted rivers]? 

· Where are the highest/lowest \[mountains]?

· Where are people \[emigrating from and migrating to]? 

· Is \[religion] growing or disappearing?

· How did \[nuclear bombs] develop?

· How big is \[the universe]?

· How much faster are \[we travelling today]?

· Who is \[smoking] the most?

· Does \[wealth] correlate with \[health]?

From each other, you may discover even more.
- ### Define the Brief 
Practice exercise 3\.2

Start defining a top\-level brief for your project by answering the following questions:

· **PURPOSE: Why** are you visualising the data? What is the purpose of the infographic? Consider the goal and how you can achieve it. Do you want to inform, surprise, entertain, or persuade? If it's a marketing piece, you might want to attract a new audience or reach existing customers. The two may need to deliver a different focus/benefit. Always aim to be insightful and truthful to the data/information you are representing. 

· **CHANNEL: Where** is the infographic going to be published? What is the medium/channel? If you have a blank canvas and zero restrictions, consider the best channel and format in which to present your visual story. This decision will inform the way viewers interact with and read the infographic. What setting will this be used in? Is it part of a live presentation, where you'll have more control over the way information is presented? Or a printed document, where readers decide how fast and how deeply they will delve into the information?

· **AUDIENCE: Who** is your audience? Who is the user, reader, or viewer? Think about what your audience knows, what they don't know, and what you want them to understand with your visualisation. Take into consideration any context (i.e. language, culture, and time the audience will spend reading or interacting with the dataviz, etc.)

After you have answered these initial questions, the what and how will naturally follow! What is the story? What are the interesting data points, outliers, and unexpected trends in your dataset? How can I communicate those insights in an accurate, compelling, and memorable way? If you have any questions, don't hesitate to ask.

- ### Clean and Analyse the Data 
Practice exercise 3\.4

Now, it's time to apply what you learnt in the lessons 'Data Preparation' and 'Knowing your Data' about how to format data and perform basic calculations in a spreadsheet. Once you have identified a dataset and have downloaded it on your computer, perform the following tasks:

· Import the data into Google Sheets. You need to have a free Google account in order to use this software. A handy shortcut to creating a new spreadsheet is to type in your browser 'new.sheet'. 

· Duplicate the original dataset and rename it. 

· Tidy column headings so you are clear about what the data is about.

· Delete any unnecessary columns.

· Check numbers and clean the format of the values (i.e. delete thousands separators).

· Correct any entries. 

· Investigate missing data.

· Answer the key questions: Who, what, when, where, and how much? You can download the document I used in the lesson 'Knowing Your Data' for guidance by scrolling below.

Remember to engage with the forum. Ask any questions and share pictures of your progress. Then I can gain an idea of your creative process.

- ### Sketch with Data 
Practice exercise 3\.5

Now it's your turn to draw the map. Don't overthink it, just let your hand draw a few marks on paper. I encourage you to start with a marker rather than a pencil – your actions will be more committed and less defined. Soon you will see the fear of the blank canvas dissolving.

This is the time to start imagining possible solutions. But it's easy to feel overwhelmed working with data. Away from the restrictions of templates and digital tools, you are more connected to your mind and heart. You can think more freely about the forms of the data and the story it represents, and it can also help you to tap into your own imagination and find your own creative voice.

Since sketching is not meant to be the final outcome, it's important to be mindful of the time we invest. We don't look for perfect drawings (look at my sketches!). Sketching is fast, rough, and disposable. 

You may feel the desire to skip this stage (especially on a short deadline) and jump straight to the computer to work out your solutions on the screen. But you could spend ages trying to work up ideas that are not properly formed in your mind, moving things around on screen and getting nowhere. 

Even as little as 20 minutes of upfront conceptual sketching could help foster a deeper understanding of the subject matter, expose knowledge gaps, and ignite a creative spark. 

Let's go! Put pen to paper. Once we have a few ideas on paper, we can even validate how effective they are in communicating the insights by sharing them and gathering feedback.


![](https://assets1.domestika.org/course-images/000/037/691/37691-big.jpg)
- ### Explore the Tools and Prototype the Visualisation 
Practice exercise 3\.6

See, sketching wasn't that hard after all! You might even be surprised by the many ideas you've come up with! Pick 1 or 2 sketches that you feel are the strongest and validate them with a prototype. 

Being able to prototype the visual lexicon you wish to create is very important. To start with, you might want to try with a few different tools and see what each tool allows you to create. 

Often the process is a cycle. By prototyping and visualising the data, you might realise that your initial sketch needs to be reconsidered, or you might come up with a completely different idea. Be open to starting the process again and returning to the blank paper. 

Dataviz isn't magic. It requires planning, researching, designing, failing/succeeding, and showing up the next day to do it all over again! As always, don't forget to interact with the forum. I'd love to know how you're progressing.

- ### Charting Conventions and Catalogues

One of the most difficult things to do when you're starting to solve the puzzle is figuring out which chart to use. Eventually, you'll want to push the conventions and try different forms and chart types. But before that, I'd recommend developing a really good handle on the basic charts and when to use them. Even a bar chart can become an interesting and catchy visualisation.

You should always start by exploring the data. From there, you can decide which encoding system or chart will be best, rather than trying to force a dataset to fit the visual representation you want to use.

In this initial phase of exploring the dataset, testing different types of visualisations can be very useful to see patterns, links, densities, and other hidden clues that might not be visible by only reading the numbers in a table. 

There are so many ways to visualise data, how do we know which chart to pick? A good way to familiarise yourself with the many charting conventions available is by checking out the following resources.

[FT Visual Vocabulary](http://ft-interactive.github.io/visual-vocabulary/)

- ### The Sketching Process

The best tool we have to encode data is pen and paper. Many data designers like myself use sketching as their primary design tool. Sketching with data is used to understand what's in the numbers and how to organise those variables to gain meaning and communicate insights. It's also used to break the mould and create truly unique forms tailored around the stories of the data.

['Valentina D'Efilippo: os superpoderes do rascunho'](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JnV7sLzvtqY&list=PLbgjH0A1l73jVG9iCtuzAUP2VLZV4Femn&index=6)
  
Here is an informal talk I gave on the powers of sketching! During this online meet\-up, I shared the reasons and benefits of sketching through a few case studies.

[*Raw Data: Infographic Designers' Sketchbooks* \| Steven Heller and Rick Landers](https://thamesandhudson.com/raw-data-infographic-designers-sketchbooks-9780500517451)
  
A fascinating, behind\-the\-scenes collection on the creation of infographics and data visualisation, featuring preliminary sketches and early prototypes.

[Dear Data](http://www.dear-data.com/theproject)
  
A year\-long, analogue drawing project by information designers Giorgia Lupi and Stefanie Posavec. The site includes all of their postcards with a detailed write\-up about the process. It's a truly inspiring project.

['The Messy Art Of UX Sketching'](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2011/12/the-messy-art-of-ux-sketching/)
  
This article in *Smashing Magazine* presents insights and techniques on how to use sketching for designing user experiences (UX) and user interaction (UI).

['Sketching and Visual Thinking with Eva\-Lotta Lamm'](https://datastori.es/sketching-and-visual-thinking-with-eva-lotta-lamm/)
  
Eva\-Lotta is a UX designer and here she talks about the value of sketching and how it relates to data visualisation.

[*Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain* \| Betty Edwards](https://www.drawright.com/bettys-books)
  
Although the aim of sketching with data isn't beautiful execution, this book can be helpful. It's a classic based on the premise that we can all be creative and that the important thing is not the manual ability, but the way of looking. This manual is very helpful; both for people who want to draw but believe they have no talent, and for those art professionals who seek to improve their technique.
