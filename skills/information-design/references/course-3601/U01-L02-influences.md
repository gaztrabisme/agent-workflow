## The stories held in maps

Anchor: 3601 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–01:33

My work is eclectic, and its inspiration comes from many different sources. In this lesson I want to share some of the key moments and influences that brought me to where I am. I can trace my interest in information design back to maps.

As a child, I remember flicking through a book like the one shown here and spending hours imagining what the monsters and lands might look like. In my mind, I was playing out stories about those places. I have always been fascinated by how symbols and signs in hand-drawn maps can spark our imagination and take us on a storytelling journey.

That fascination led me to collect maps. In 2009, when I visited Asia for the first time, I was in Japan and saw a map of the world with Europe outside the centre. I began to question these visual representations and the way they shape our understanding of the world. I started asking local people to draw, by hand, what the world looked like in their minds. I collected hundreds and hundreds of hand-drawn maps. For me, the collection is made up of personal stories as well as information design: each map carries data about its creator, where they are from, and their experiences. They are all unique stories.

## Bruno Munari and the curious eye

Anchor: 3601 · U1 · L02 · 01:33–03:03

The next influence I want to share is Bruno Munari. While studying industrial design, I had a moment of enlightenment when I discovered his creative genius. Picasso called Munari “the new Leonardo” (captions: “Picasso named it the new Leonardo”). Munari worked across many different fields and applied creative thinking and problem-solving to architecture, urban design, and psychology. He also did a lot of work on visual perception.

*Drawing a Tree* and drawing the sun are beautiful examples of how we can look at the world through an information designer’s eyes. A simple shape such as a tree is actually quite complex. Munari breaks it into many forms and components and investigates its structure.

Munari has always inspired me because of the curious way he looks at the world. He is not confined to one specific field: he tries to connect design, the world, and an audience. His curiosity and his ability to keep reinventing himself throughout his practice and career remain inspiring. I believe that working with data requires a curious mindset. Curiosity is a key ingredient in my practice.

## Otto and Marie Neurath’s visual language

Anchor: 3601 · U1 · L02 · 03:03–03:49

The next influence is the work of Otto and Marie Neurath (captions at 03:03: “Otto Emarin Neurath”). The work of earlier giants has informed data visualisation. Otto Neurath is regarded as the father of pictograms applied to data visualisation. Long before emojis existed, he worked towards a universal system for representing numbers through iconography. The idea was inspired in part by hieroglyphics. He wanted a pictorial system that conveyed a story and offered a shortcut into it.

The Neuraths’ work is inspiring because of how they tried to communicate numbers and statistics in both a rational and an emotional way. At 03:36 the captions read “Neorath”; the reference is to the Neuraths.

## Contemporary references and first investigations

Anchor: 3601 · U1 · L02 · 03:49–05:17

During my studies I looked for contemporary references to help me navigate data design and find a path forward. About fifteen years ago, I came across an inspiring blog called Visual Complexity, curated by Manuel Lima (captions: “Manuel Limo”). There were not many books about data visualisation or many blogs at the time, and Visual Complexity was an incredible place to discover work by Jonathan Harris, Aaron Koblin, Brendan Dawes, and Jason Salavon (captions read “Aaron Copland,” “Brendan Davis,” and “Salomon”). Their work inspired me to begin investigating for myself. At first, I applied what I was looking at and tried to replicate it.

I started deconstructing *The Shining*: I looked at the programmatic language in what I was seeing, but approached it in a much more analog and smaller way. I wanted to understand how to collect data and represent it so that it made sense and communicated insights. This visualisation represents the colour scheme of the entire film, from beginning to end. The palette is quite desaturated, but two moments are much brighter than the rest. Those moments are Kubrick shouting at us that something is happening: Jack, the protagonist, is about to lose his mind.

## Finding a voice from many fields

Anchor: 3601 · U1 · L02 · 05:17–07:01

Over the years, while researching and trying to find my own voice, I realised that style itself was not what I was looking for. I want to give the data I represent a unique voice. That means drawing inspiration from many worlds: nature, biology, architecture, textiles, and popular culture (captions: “Poplar culture”). It depends on the subject and the kind of story I want to communicate.

Inspiration can come in many ways, and it is more than aesthetics. I am drawn to the beauty and intricate complexity of artists such as Ernst Haeckel (captions: “Ernst A call”). His work pictures nature and biology in full detail. I also look at examples in my latest project, which visualises the virus in London (captions: “Visualizes the Virus and London”). That work was a source of inspiration.

Inspiration is more than aesthetics, especially now, when social feeds and Pinterest boards can present aesthetics as the primary goal. It is more than look and feel: it is about function and how we can connect to a story. Inspiration comes through conversations and the places we visit. It comes more from an inner place than an outer one. We will return to inspiration during the course and look for visual references and metaphors in the subject matter we study.

## The course’s data-story process

Anchor: 3601 · U1 · L02 · 07:01–08:32

The final project will be an infographic on a subject of your choice. We will follow the whole process: find a data set, find the stories and insights in it, and create a compelling visual system to communicate both the stories and the insights.

We will begin with an introduction to what data is, why we visualise it, and an overview of the process. Then we will look at where to find a data set, download and prepare it, and search for insights. We will see how a mood board inspired by the subject matter can inform our encoding system. Encoding means transforming data into a visual image or representation. We will use sketching to make that transformation, then take the sketches into a prototype. I will use Data Illustrator, but you are welcome to explore other tools. I will develop the final design step by step, from building the layout to refining the colours and bringing images into Adobe Illustrator. When we are happy with the infographic, I will share thoughts about presenting work and collaboration.

Now you know me, my influences, and what we will work on together. I hope you are as excited as I am to get started. In the next lesson, we will begin by defining what data is.

### Key points (derived)

- Personal experiences, places, and conversations can shape visual storytelling as much as visual style does.
- Curiosity helps connect data, design, the subject, and its audience.
- A useful infographic process moves from a data set and its insights through visual references, encoding, sketching, prototyping, and refinement.
