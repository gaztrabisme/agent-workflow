## Preparing the copy and stating the story

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–02:46

The designs are nearly complete, but the text and notation still need attention. This lesson refines the copy and typography and spells out the story. A designer's job is not only to render data, but also to explain why it matters (captions: “But to talk. Wine matters.”).

In commercial work, I would usually receive the copy in a Word document. For a self-initiated project, I still prefer to draft it in a document first: I can focus on the writing, check for flaws, make sure the information is correct, estimate how much text is needed, and then paste it into Illustrator.

The title is “A thirsty world.” The introductory paragraph sets the scene and states the key insight: only 74% of the world's population had access to a safely managed water source in 2020, so one in four people did not have access to safe drinking water. The next paragraph explains that a safely managed drinking-water service must be an improved source that is accessible on premises, available when needed, and free from contamination; the data concerns households.

The first key is for the dendrograms: progress on access to safe drinking water from 2000 to 2020, measured as a percentage of national population. Its caption explains that the graphic shows the percentage of national populations with household access to safely managed drinking water and how this changed since 2000 across regions and income groups. The second key is for the area charts and describes access by income group. I also start listing the key statistics to annotate, checking the data so the figures are reported correctly. The map caption describes 2020 estimates for 138 countries and notes that data is unavailable for nearly 100 others.

I add notes and do some additional research in case there is room to annotate Nepal (captions: “NetVal”). I first think of Nepal as the country with the least access, then correct myself: the point I have found is that it had the greatest decrease between 2000 and 2020.

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L14 · 02:46–06:08

I begin with the dendrograms: copy the circle, apply Offset Path, and add anchor points where the circular line should break. Lock the other artwork, then delete the intervening pieces so separate text paths remain.

## Enclosing regional labels and adding chart headings

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L14 · 06:08–07:47

When the labels are in place, I lock them and create a background shape that contains the type. I make the shape white, then trim only the parts needed to make a clear enclosure around each label. For Asia, I add two anchor points beside the word and delete the outside segment; the remaining shape sits around the type. I do the same for Africa. Keeping the circular lines continuous looks nice, but cutting small gaps around the labels gives them more breathing room. I repeat the treatment on the other dendrogram.

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L14 · 07:47–09:24

I add a label above each chart to act as a mini-title: “WEALTHY NATIONS” for one and “THE REST OF THE WORLD” for the other. I have copied the full text into Illustrator, so I do not need to switch back and forth to the document. I set the text in the correct style, resize its box, turn on the guides to see the grid, and align the paragraph with the two columns. I avoid making the line too short. I place the next block of copy and italicize its last line because it is the point I want to emphasize.

## Creating keys for the change patterns

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L14 · 09:24–12:46

Next I add the keys. The long label for progress on access to drinking water is split across two lines. The key needs three line examples for the three cases: no change, positive change, and negative change. I copy a line segment, give the copy the same appearance, and draw another segment for the gradient. I reverse the gradient so it runs from light to dark, then duplicate the example three times.

I add the year labels. For one example, 2000 is the lightest end and 2020 the darkest. For no change, both ends are labelled 2020. For the last example, the order is reversed: 2000 is the outer point and 2020 the inner point, and I change the colour again. I do not want the 2000 label inside a box. The labels also let me check that the examples are equally spaced, so I move one down slightly. Then I copy the key for the area chart and add “by income group.”
