## Data describes reality

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–02:43

In this lesson, I'm going to introduce data and its key terminology. We often think of data as numbers in a spreadsheet, something abstract and reserved for analysts or scientists. But data represents reality: our world and our experiences. The word *data* comes from the Latin *datum*, a basic statement or definition of reality. To make the idea tangible, consider this object: a marker. It is a drawing instrument, specifically a Sharpie, and it is blue. These are qualitative attributes. Its size, weight, and number of tips are quantitative attributes.

Qualitative attributes can be ordered or unordered. Tip thickness can be arranged as fine, medium, and thick; these are ordinal attributes. A brand such as Sharpie is nominal because the labels have no natural order. Quantitative data can be continuous, such as size or weight, or discrete, meaning countable. The marker has one tip.

Writing down the descriptions gives us the beginning of a dataset. Each description is a variable. We can collect the same variables for the other things on the desk, asking each item's colour, brand, size, and other attributes, and then fill those categories with data.

## Organising information with L.A.T.C.H.

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L03 · 02:43–04:30

After collecting information, a useful way to think about organising it is the L.A.T.C.H. framework, proposed by Richard Saul Wurman, author of *Information Anxiety*. L.A.T.C.H. stands for Location, Alphabet, Time, Category, and Hierarchy. These five modes can organise a dataset of objects in different ways.

![3601 · U2 · L03 · 02:56](frames/U02L03-F0256.00.jpg)

On screen: “L.A.T.C.H.” / “Information may be infinite, however (…) the organisation of information is finite as it can only be organised by Location, Alphabet, Time, Category, or Hierarchy” / “Richard Saul Wurman” / “Information anxiety” [rest omitted]  
Shows: A L.A.T.C.H. slide quoting Richard Saul Wurman's statement about the finite ways to organise information.

First is location. The objects are already mapped out on the desk according to where they usually appear. We could change that spatial arrangement and organise them by where they were produced: geographic information, with objects placed in a landscape or space according to location.

![3601 · U2 · L03 · 03:11](frames/U02L03-F0311.00.jpg)

On screen: [rest omitted]  
Shows: The teacher points with a blue marker toward the desk as she discusses the objects' locations.

Next is alphabet. The objects can be arranged from A to Z, from books beginning with B through to smartphones. Alphabetical order is useful for a long list because it gives people an automatic system for finding the data they need. Its limitation is that it depends on the labels attached to the objects. For instance, two notebooks could be called “sketchbooks” and grouped together, or listed under “notebooks” and placed somewhere else.

## Time, category, and hierarchy

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L03 · 04:30–06:46

The next mode is time. Here the objects are laid out by how long they have been owned, from more than five years to less than one month. Time is useful because it shows change, and it is often represented as a timeline. A common convention puts the past on the left and the present on the right because we read from left to right. Timelines can also run from top to bottom: on Facebook, recent posts appear at the top and older history appears farther down as you scroll.

![3601 · U2 · L03 · 04:48](frames/U02L03-F0448.00.jpg)

On screen: “…ains why the information…” [rest omitted]  
Shows: The objects are rearranged across the desk to represent their position in time.

Category and hierarchy can be shown together. In this arrangement, colour runs along one axis, from white to black, and size runs along the other, from the largest object to the smallest. Category and hierarchy are by far the most popular way to organise data: they let us group things and rank them.

I wanted to make working with data feel approachable and explain the ideas of data, quantitative data, and qualitative data in simple terms. Now that we know what data is, the next lesson looks at why we visualise it.

### Key points (derived)

- Data describes reality through qualitative and quantitative variables.
- Qualitative data can be nominal or ordinal; quantitative data can be continuous or discrete.
- L.A.T.C.H. organises information by Location, Alphabet, Time, Category, or Hierarchy.
