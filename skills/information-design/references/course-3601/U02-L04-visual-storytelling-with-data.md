## Why visualise data?

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–02:34

Now that we know what data is, we can ask why and how we visualise it, and build foundational knowledge about the field. Raw data does not speak for itself; design gives it a voice. Visual communication can reveal stories hidden in numbers. A table is not impossible to read, but it takes time. When the same values are shown as a simple chart, we can see almost immediately whether they are rising or falling and how large the changes are.

This speed comes from visual perception. We do not see only through our eyes; we see through the brain. The brain has a fast system and a slow system. The retina sends visual input to the fast system, including the visual cortex, which can detect small differences in size, position, and contrast. It acts like a sophisticated processor scanning what we see. The information then reaches the slower analytical system, which processes it and makes sense of it.

![3601 · U2 · L04 · 01:11](frames/U02L04-F0111.00.jpg)

On screen: “Visual Perception” / “We see through the brain”  
Shows: A brain icon points to an eye icon, then to a globe icon.

Visual attributes that the cortex responds to effectively are called pre-attentive attributes because they are processed automatically. We use visual variables such as position, size, and motion to encode information for the brain. Encoding means transforming a numerical system into a visual one: writing numbers with visuals.

## Visual literacy and visual communication

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L04 · 02:34–04:51

William Playfair championed visual perception and representation. He thought tables were an ineffective way to look at numbers and wrote that statistics were dry and tedious unless the mind and imagination were engaged. He is considered the father of data visualisation. In the late 1700s, he invented common chart types including the line, bar, pie, and area charts. These forms became conventions. Later representations have multiplied, but they still rely on how the brain processes information and on the visual cortex.

Visual literacy is our innate ability to read, write, and create visual images. For example, red can suggest love, blood, passion, fire, something bad, heat, or a negative value. In a traffic-light context, however, red means stop. We have a long history of reading images: people painted caves before written language existed, and hieroglyphics developed millennia before writing. The ability is also individual. Children make sense of the world visually and can decode pictures before mastering verbal skills; they can also encode ideas with drawings and sketches.

## From encoding to visual storytelling

Anchor: 3601 · U2 · L04 · 04:51–10:08

Pre-attentive attributes are the foundation of data visualisation. We can also use images, metaphors, and colour psychology, which brings us closer to information graphics and helps us connect with the people behind the bars. In an example by Otto Neurath, silhouettes represent unemployed people queuing. They also make visual computation easier: it is quicker to count those units and compare bars than to calculate the relationship from the numbers alone.

![3601 · U2 · L04 · 05:26](frames/U02L04-F0526.00.jpg)

Shows: A numeric table and bar chart sit beside an infographic that uses repeated human silhouettes to represent unemployment.

Visual storytelling goes beyond a shortcut into a subject. It can prompt reflection and engage an audience cognitively and emotionally. In an infographic for *Scientific American*, I showed food waste at each stage of the supply chain. I combined the pre-attentive attributes of height and colour with a pile-of-waste metaphor. I wanted to show not only how much waste different strategies could reduce, but also make billions of tonnes tangible instead of leaving them as an abstract figure. I then focused on the last stage of the chain and on household meat waste to make the large numbers easier to understand.

Another example is my self-initiated project *MeToomentum*, made with developer Lucia Konchenkova. We scraped the Twitter API and retrieved 200,000 tweets from the first six months of the #MeToo movement after Alyssa Milano's first tweet. I used a dandelion as a metaphor for the pattern and visual structure of the data, to express both the fragility and strength of the voices. The dandelion—*dente di leone*, “lion's tooth” (captions: “I folded it under the lion”)—has this dual nature. In popular culture, it can symbolise endurance, growth, and hope for change. Its complexity and delicate, feminine associations helped me express my point of view and feelings. Visual communication can be personal, so communicators need to recognise their responsibility. The project let me join the conversation and share my perspective.

*London Under the Microscope* is an animation about the first year of the pandemic: each second represents a day, and the data is both visualised and sonified. The Museum of London asked me to show how the virus and pandemic reshaped the city. Charlie reimagined London's geography through the structure of the virus. That metaphor supports a layered visual and encoding system: mobility is subtle in the background membrane; cases and deaths are the main visual elements; and the vaccine appears as a halo or background story, with filaments growing from the membrane. The animation is available in the resources, with its sound.

Visual storytelling does not have to provide a conclusive answer. It can begin a conversation. We cannot visualise what happened to all nine million people living in London as if each had the same experience. Data is only a proxy for lived experience. I wanted to create space for people to remember what happened to them, think about other people's stories, and empathise with others.

### Key points (derived)

- Visual perception helps people notice changes and relationships quickly; visual literacy lets them interpret familiar images and metaphors.
- Data design can combine numerical encoding with images to make large-scale issues tangible and invite empathy or dialogue.
