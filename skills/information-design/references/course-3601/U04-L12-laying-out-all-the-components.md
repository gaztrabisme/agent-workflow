## Continuing the circular dendrogram

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L12 · 00:00–02:10

We are continuing the Illustrator layout with the data visualisation made from two circumferences, each carrying a brush. We can change a brush and replace its content. Labels now sit over the visualisation. Text beneath them helped as a placement guide, but I do not want to keep it: wrapping the text around a circle distorts it, making letters smaller, larger and more condensed than normal. Since the labels are individual elements, I can delete the outer circumference. In Outline mode, each tag can be selected separately, while the inner circumference is still one graphical circle. I need to expand it to split the graph into two halves: high-income countries on the left and the rest of the world on the right.

I have put high-income countries on the left consistently in my sketches because there are fewer of them than countries in the rest of the world. That arrangement leaves more room for the title, introduction, copy and key.

## Planning the accompanying displays

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L12 · 02:10–03:18

The secondary displays are area charts. One will compare the global trend from 2000 to 2020; the others will show the income levels, with high income and the global trend together in one display and the other three income groups in another. Instead of giving the global trend its own separate chart, I may use it as a reference in each display.

I also want the area charts to look like two glasses. A glass is an immediate visual metaphor for the amount of water people have globally. The final element will be a map, almost like a footnote. It will show the spread of the dataset and, importantly, how much of the world has no data available. The sketch shows how the two circular displays, taps, glasses and a map fit into the story.

## Splitting the data into two halves

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L12 · 03:18–05:56

I make a copy of the circumference and move it aside, naming it “the rest of the world.” I keep the original because it records the circle’s location and centre. On the duplicate, choose Object, Expand Appearance. In Outline mode, each mark can then be selected and manipulated. I move the marks that belong to high-income countries into the high-income layer rather than making another copy. Direct Selection lets me select the items and drag them into that layer. Make sure you have not selected anything by mistake. I also move the rest-of-world labels and the axis, then check that the two groups are divided cleanly.

To rotate the high-income selection, select its contents through the circle in the Layers panel. When the cursor reaches a selection corner and changes to the rotation icon, rotate the group. Drag it close to the visualisation and align it so both 0% ends start at the same point, then move the other half across. With the Rotate tool, turn the group about 45°. Rotate the other half around its centre, entering an angle of roughly 36° or 35°, then move it up. The two halves can now be aligned without redrawing their data.

## Keeping chart scales comparable

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L12 · 06:48–09:26

Before copying the chart for another group, override its axis: set the maximum to 100 and the minimum to 0. Otherwise, Illustrator chooses minimum and maximum values from each dataset, making the charts incomparable. Then return to the income ladder data and select safely managed water for the low-income group. If the graph’s data panel is closed, reopen it through Object, Graph, Data. Copy and paste the data for the lower-middle-income group, update the chart, and repeat the same process for the middle and high-income groups.

Once all the data is plotted, I make copies of the charts and save them on a separate reference layer. I may delete that layer later, but it helps me check that the work is accurate. The charts remain editable at this stage: selecting one lets me change its data and see the result. For example, changing an initial point to 80 changes the graph. When I no longer need to manipulate the data, I choose Object > Ungroup. Illustrator warns that ungrouping removes access to the graph style and data and prevents changes to the graph design.

## Turning the global trend into a reference line

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L12 · 09:26–11:13

I keep copies of the global-trend charts as references and remove their axes. Before overlaying the income-level charts, I check their alignment and align them to the bottom so they share a baseline. I delete the lower two anchor points from the global-trend charts to leave reference lines.

I centre the three income-level charts together and temporarily change their colours so the groups are visible. I then use Arrange > Send Back to change the selected chart’s position, using the keyboard because it is quicker. The overlap shows where one income level ends and the next begins. There are many axes (captions: “access”) that we do not need, so I delete them. I bring the two glass displays together by aligning them instead of moving them by hand. The global trends need to be plotted along the same y-axis, so alignment preserves accuracy. The two global trend lines then serve as guides for the high-income and rest-of-world glass displays.

## Showing the data that is missing

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L12 · 11:13–12:43

The last display is a world map. The other visualisations show the change from 2000 to 2020 and all the countries for which information is available. They do not yet show the parts of the world that are not covered by reporting. To reach universal access to clean water, we also need to evaluate whether access exists. The grey area on the map is therefore part of the story.

I made this map in Google Sheets, using the choropleth (captions: “corplat”) tab. Insert a chart and choose Map from the chart-type menu. The customisation options let you change the background colour, borders and regional data encoding.

A choropleth is a map where predefined areas—in this case, countries—are coloured or patterned according to statistical values. Lighter shades are commonly used for smaller values and darker shades for larger ones. If the background were dark, I would reverse the scheme so that the larger values had the greatest contrast.

## Getting a vector map into Illustrator

Anchor: 3601 · U4 · L12 · 12:43–14:18

I could use this map directly and download it as a PNG, but I have not settled the colour encoding or appearance. I want an SVG so I can adjust the colours and borders in Illustrator. For this example, I take a shortcut: I download the report that contains a vector version of the same map. If you use a dataset from Our World in Data, you can often download its global map too; the site supplies a vector geographic graphic where one is available. I open the report in Illustrator, double-click the graphic to access the map, select and copy its elements, and paste them into the design. If you want to create a choropleth from scratch and download an SVG, the resources include links to Datawrapper and Flourish.
