# Story and structure: find the finding before polishing

## Reader and scope

**Reader:** a designer with a checked dataset, choosing which facts and relationships will lead the graphic. **Scope:** identify the useful finding, distinguish central from supporting measures, sketch order and structure, and decide whether a metaphor helps. Broader page composition belongs with `visual-design`.

**Source lessons:** [course 3601 · U3 L08](../course-3601/U03-L08-knowing-your-data.md) and [L09](../course-3601/U03-L09-transforming-data-into-visuals.md); [course 4217 · U2 L06](../course-4217/U02-L06-the-process-of-telling-stories-with-data.md) and [U3 L11](../course-4217/U03-L11-sketching-the-macrostructure-of-the-story.md); [course 3069 · U2 L06](../course-3069/U02-L06-the-data-for-your-visualization.md), [U3 L07](../course-3069/U03-L07-sketching-and-storyline.md) and [U3 L09](../course-3069/U03-L09-workflow.md).

## Look for a question the data can answer

- Compare totals, relative sizes, changes, relationships and outliers; decide whether a single dataset or connected layers offer a useful direction. (3069 · U2 · L06 · 00:40–01:28; 3069 · U2 · L06 · 01:28–02:44; 3069 · U2 · L06 · 02:44–03:25)
- Ask who, what, when, where and how much. Compare definitions, overlapping categories, missingness, periods and geographic groups before choosing the main measure. (3601 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–00:47; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 00:47–02:24; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 02:24–04:35; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 04:35–07:26; 3601 · U3 · L08 · 07:26–10:06)
- For a narrative, list the people, places or objects to represent and the features that could become marks; use the storyline sketch to expose layout needs as well as data questions. (3069 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–03:36; 3069 · U3 · L07 · 03:36–05:02)
- Separate the main story from context. In the CO₂ example, emissions over time carry the story; population and GDP orient the reader. (2495 · U3 · L09 · 01:08–02:33; 2495 · U3 · L09 · 02:33–03:35)
- List variables and decide the order the reader meets them. The renewable-energy example introduces region and population before the energy measures. (4217 · U3 · L11 · 00:08–01:14; 4217 · U3 · L11 · 01:14–02:26; 4217 · U3 · L11 · 02:26–02:54; 4217 · U3 · L11 · 02:54–03:47)
- A view can reveal information in stages to invite exploration; the migration-story example shows routes before the reader discovers the map and supporting data. (4217 · U2 · L06 · 01:08–02:00)

## Sketch before finishing the form

Sketch candidate marks and their relationships while looking at both the question and actual data. Rough sketches are for structure and encoding; plotting the data is what validates whether a candidate works. (3601 · U3 · L09 · 00:09–03:18; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 17:37–18:45)

Try more than one structure when needed. The course examples move among start/end comparisons, region groupings, a dendrogram and a radial arrangement; they also remove a proposed layer when it overwhelms the intended story. (3601 · U3 · L09 · 09:50–13:37; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 13:37–15:13; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 15:13–17:37; 2495 · U4 · L10 · 05:08–06:30)

## Let metaphor support meaning

- A familiar object or action can help readers enter an abstract measure; in the clean-water example, taps, glasses and water levels connect access data to daily use. (3601 · U3 · L09 · 00:09–03:18; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 07:00–09:50)
- A metaphor can emerge from the topic or from a pattern in the data; the teacher also deliberately challenges familiar visual conventions. Do not force a metaphor before studying the content. (4217 · U2 · L07 · 00:00–00:45; 4217 · U2 · L07 · 01:54–02:25; 4217 · U2 · L07 · 03:56–05:25)
- Personal interest can start the project, while a potential reader's interest remains part of the communication decision. (3069 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:12; 2495 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:29)

**Rule synthesized (derived):** treat the first structure as a hypothesis. Keep a layer only when it helps answer the primary question or adds useful context; revise the structure when plotting or a reader check shows clutter or a competing message. This joins courses that test sketches against data, remove complexity and invite reader interpretation. (derived from 3601 · U3 · L09 · 15:13–17:37; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 17:37–18:45; 2495 · U4 · L10 · 05:08–06:30; 3069 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–01:30; 3069 · U4 · L13 · 01:30–02:38)

The process can loop between source, spreadsheet, sketch and design; a working order guides decisions without claiming that every project proceeds without returns to earlier stages. (3069 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:37)

## Failures

**Problem 1: accumulating layers without a reading order.** Multiple datasets may add depth, but they can compete. Name the main view and make secondary layers subordinate or remove them. (4217 · U2 · L05 · 01:48–02:25; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 15:13–17:37)

**Problem 2: metaphor drives the chart.** A metaphor may attract attention but obscure the quantity. Test it against the values and provide a direct key. (4217 · U2 · L07 · 00:00–00:45; 2495 · U4 · L15 · 00:00–01:26)

## Check before moving on

Pass T4 when one primary question and answer are visible in the sketch, central and contextual variables have distinct roles, and each extra layer has a reason. If no candidate can carry the needed data, return to analysis or chart choice.

**Practice the teachers set:** find a relationship or outlier, list the important variables, and sketch possible marks and reading order. See [course 3069, Unit 2](../course-3069/U02-unit.md), [course 4217, Unit 3](../course-4217/U03-unit.md) and [course 3601, Unit 3](../course-3601/U03-unit.md).
