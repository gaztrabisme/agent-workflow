# Brief: define the reader's question and use

## Reader and scope

**Reader:** the designer before selecting data encodings. **Scope:** establish what the reader should learn or do, who they are, and where they will meet the artifact. For a wider product journey or interface-writing task, hand off to `ux`.

**Source lessons:** [course 4217 · U2 L04](../course-4217/U02-L04-the-questions-to-start-with.md); [course 3601 · U3 L06](../course-3601/U03-L06-where-to-find-data.md); [course 2495 · U2 L04](../course-2495/U02-L04-the-broad-field-of-data-visualization-readers-and-usage-cont.md) and [L05](../course-2495/U02-L05-the-relationship-with-the-readers.md).

## Start with four questions

- What are the data's characteristics: time, place, categories, quantities or relationships? (4217 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:08)
- Who are the readers, what do they need, and should the graphic support quick comparison, exploration, discovery or engagement? (4217 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:08; 4217 · U2 · L04 · 02:25–03:22)
- What is the use context and how much time can the reader give it? The course contrasts a business meeting with a relaxed Sunday morning read. (4217 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:08; 2495 · U2 · L04 · 00:59–01:44; 2495 · U2 · L04 · 01:44–03:32)
- Will the reader spend a few seconds or explore the graphic more slowly? The course places forms across a range of reading speed and complexity according to reader needs and use context. (2495 · U2 · L04 · 00:59–01:44; 2495 · U2 · L04 · 01:44–03:32)
- Is the artifact for print or an interactive piece? The print example cannot use interactivity, so it layers information for readers to explore at different depths. (4217 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:08; 4217 · U2 · L04 · 03:22–04:45)

As a briefing aid, write one sentence for the reader's question and one for the expected answer or action. (derived: this turns the course's advice to consider a potential reader's interest and keep communication in view into an explicit prompt; 2495 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:29) Keep the reader visible through topic choice and visual form: the course begins from personal interest and checks whether a potential reader might also care. (2495 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:29)

## Set the communication goal before chart choice

| Goal | Design implication | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Compare values or find patterns quickly | For an urgent need to find patterns, use minimal, precise visualizations with interactive filtering. | (4217 · U2 · L04 · 02:25–03:22) |
| Explore several layers at different depths | For print, layer information so readers can explore at different depths; use a legend to explain the views. | (4217 · U2 · L04 · 03:22–04:45) |
| Invite engagement or discovery | The choice between simple, immediate graphics and more engaging pieces depends on project needs; immediacy need not be the main goal. | (4217 · U2 · L03 · 01:32–02:18; 4217 · U2 · L04 · 04:45–05:21) |

**Rule synthesized (derived):** make the reader's task the tie-breaker when data form and expressive intent pull in different directions. 4217 asks designers to weigh data, reader, needs, context and medium; 3601 sketches chart forms and metaphors together; 2495 keeps communication in view throughout. (derived from 4217 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:08; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 00:09–03:18; 2495 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–01:29)

## Failures

**Problem 1: choosing the form first.** A chart catalog or visual reference may suggest options, but the project question, data and reader still determine the choice. (4217 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:08; 3601 · U3 · L09 · 00:09–03:18)

**Problem 2: confusing immediacy with quality.** A bar chart may be more immediate, but immediacy is not always the main goal; engagement can be. (4217 · U2 · L04 · 04:45–05:21)

## Check before moving on

Pass T1 when reader, question, intended use, and medium are explicit. If any is missing, return to the brief instead of guessing at the chart.
