## Choose visual and written words together

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–00:57

In this lesson, I discuss the relationship between visual and written words and why it matters when designing visual data narratives. The relationship between data visualization and text is very strong for me. To design visual narratives, we have to choose both visual and written words. As in writing, the choice of visual words matters. I often work with organic, soft shapes. Oxford Languages defines organic as “relating to or derived from living matter.” Working through organic visual elements helps me make the living visible (captions: “I think through inorganic visual elements, helps me make invisible the living”).

## A graphic can make lives behind numbers visible

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L05 · 00:57–01:48

I often work with numbers that have lives behind them, and I want to keep that in mind. This visualization shows the average proportion and number of environmental defender deaths in Brazil from 2015 to 2019. The number of leaves represents the average proportion of deaths each year by state: one leaf represents 10%, and a portion of a leaf represents 1%. Curved lines show the average number of deaths in a year, with one line representing one death.

This visualization was not a standalone project; it accompanied an article written by Yesenia Funes. It was part of a longer storytelling piece for which I also created a map. The map shows deforestation rates by state in 2018, 2019, and 2020, and highlights places where events mentioned in the article took place.

## Use text to build context and declare a point of view

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L05 · 02:25–03:40

The second aspect is combining text and image. The graphics accompanied substantial reporting, and the text and graphics work together to create a story. I think combining text and graphics matters in any case. A misconception in this field, especially a few years ago, was that a good infographic should not need words to be understood. I disagree. No one is born knowing how to read even the simplest infographic, so I cannot assume that readers already know how.

Text also helps designers be intellectually honest by declaring our starting point of view. Quoting Alberto Cairo again: data visualizations are often conceived as tools that let people extract their own conclusions from data, rather than as tools for conveying messages predefined by their designers. Most of my visualizations are explorative. I am not offering one specific point; I am giving an overview of a situation.

## Make selection choices explicit

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L05 · 03:40–04:39

That does not mean my visualizations are completely neutral. I select data, even when I use criteria that are as objective as possible, and I make choices. Data are not completely neutral either (captions: “ditto. Aren't completely neutral creatures either”): they have human intervention behind them, as machines and algorithms do. I think it is important to be aware of this and to add short text to a visualization that explains even the criteria for selection.

For example, one graphic selects 40 countries with populations above 10 million and ranks them by population density. Stating that choice makes clear which countries are included and which are excluded. Now that we have seen the relationship between visual and written words, the next lesson will discuss the process of telling stories with data.
