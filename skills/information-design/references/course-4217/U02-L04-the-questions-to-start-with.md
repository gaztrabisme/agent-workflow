## Questions to answer before choosing visual forms

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L04 · 00:00–01:08

In this lesson, I discuss the questions to ask before starting a project, how needs and reasons guide design choices, and the wide range of projects we can work on. Before I start creating a visualization and choosing its visual letters, I ask a few questions. What are the data’s characteristics? Is there a timeline, geographical data, or two variables to compare? What are the needs, and who are the readers? Do I need to provide a tool that helps decision makers quickly compare complex data, or do I want to engage readers and help them discover something new? What is the usage context: a business meeting or a relaxed Sunday morning read? Am I working for print or for an interactive piece?

## Use data characteristics to find candidate charts

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L04 · 01:08–02:25

For the first question, I like a website linked in the additional resources: datavizproject.com (the captions render the name as “litabitsproject.com”). It presents a catalog of data visualizations, explains them, and gives examples of how to use them. I like that I can filter graphics by different variables, such as their function. To show change over time, for instance, I could use a line chart or a bubble timeline. The input filter narrows the examples further.

For one example, let A, B, and C stand for three movies, and let X and Y represent production costs and box office. Selecting that example table shows possible graphics for the data. A scatter plot could make each dot one movie, placed horizontally according to production cost and vertically according to box office. ## Match the design to the reader's need and context

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L04 · 02:25–03:22

Needs, readers, context, and interactivity all have a large impact on my design approach. In March 2020, I worked with the Surgo Foundation to give a visual dimension to its COVID-19 Community Vulnerability Index, a tool for investigating communities’ vulnerability to COVID-19. The goal was to help people explore a huge amount of data. I showed how different reasons for vulnerability change across states and counties. The urgent need was to find patterns and provide an overview, so the visualizations are minimal and precise even though they display a lot of information. Interactivity lets readers filter the data and look more closely at particular patterns.

## Design a printed piece for slower exploration

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L04 · 03:22–04:45

My second example is very different: a visualization of 40 years of letters exchanged by Friedrich Engels and Karl Marx. It was made for La Lettura, a weekly cultural magazine that is a weekend supplement to the Italian newspaper Corriere della Sera. Readers post social-media pictures of La Lettura to show it as part of a slow, relaxed Sunday morning. For this project I was asked to experiment visually and make appealing shapes that would engage readers. Because it is a printed magazine, I could not use interactivity. Instead, I layered information so readers could explore the topic at different depths.

I represented letters between Marx and Engels as colored lines and added the number of works each wrote independently and together. Both projects have a legend to explain how to read and use the visualizations. Legends are essential: they bridge the work and its readers. I devote a lot of time to explaining my projects visually, and we will work on this in the practical activity.

## Immediacy is one possible design goal

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L04 · 04:45–05:21

Sometimes I hear the critique that a bar chart would have been better because it is more immediate. A bar chart can be more immediate, but immediacy does not always have to be the main goal. Sometimes I want to catch people’s attention, invite them to read my thesis, and engage them. I designed this piece with those needs in mind. Now that we have answered these questions, the next lesson will consider visual and written words.
