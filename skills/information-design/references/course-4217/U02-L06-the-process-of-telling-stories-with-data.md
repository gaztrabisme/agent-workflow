## Two ways to tell stories with data

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L06 · 00:00–01:08

I want to talk about how we can tell stories using data and visual elements, while thinking about how readers will explore them. I will share the process and the reasons behind two storytelling projects that show different ways to use visualizations to create narratives.

I worked on the first project in 2016. It is called *The Stories Behind the Line*, a visual narrative of six asylum seekers’ journeys. I created it without a commission as a side project because I believe it is important to use my skills to talk about topics such as migration. I met the narrators at a welcoming centre in Vercelli, my hometown in Italy: M.B., S.S., M.D., A.L., S.W.G., and T.K. (captions: “SG&TK”).

## Reconstructing the journeys as lines

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L06 · 01:08–02:00

I wanted to communicate the narrators’ experiences by showing the data that characterized them and giving their journeys a perceivable shape. During our meetings, I asked about their travel from their homelands to Italy and reconstructed each route point by point. At every point, I asked how many days they had spent travelling and what transportation they had used. I also invited them to add any other memories they wanted included in their narratives.

The final project is the website storiesbehindaline.com (captions: “solidbeyondalign.com”), which shows the six lines formed by the routes. I designed it with designer and developer Alex Piacentini. Each story is precious, so I wanted each line to have its own space. At first, the lines appear as black paths on a white background; as people interact with the project, they can reveal the data and the map behind them.

## Choosing the sequence and visual tone

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L06 · 02:00–02:54

The website’s features are minimal and the data simple because I wanted to provide a clean, rational, simple narrative of these stories. I think immigration deserves a rational approach, and rationality does not necessarily mean a lack of empathy.

## Designing for discovery and perspective

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L06 · 02:54–03:43

The order and manner in which visual elements appear matter. I first wanted to show only the lines, then let users discover the map behind them. Many people were impressed by that discovery because they had not expected the journeys to cover such long distances.

I used clean, minimal elements to give the project a rational approach, but I was also influenced by how the narrators spoke. In our interviews, they shared painful stories calmly, quietly, and rationally. I wanted to pay homage to their approach by translating it visually. There is a quote about embracing other perspectives that I really love in *Data Feminism*: “Embracing the value of multiple perspectives also means actively and deliberately inviting other perspectives into the data analysis and storytelling process.”

## Giving the data room to be explored

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L06 · 03:43–04:48

In 2021, I worked with data journalist Davide Mancino on *The Great Wave*, a data journalism project about the COVID-19 pandemic. I contributed a visualization of excess deaths in Italian provinces in 2020 (captions: “access”). I have talked about how organic shapes can help us remember the lives behind the data. Here, each line represents one person; one leaf represents 100 people, grouped by province.

![4217 · U2 · L06 · 04:03.40](frames/U02L06-F0403.40.jpg)

The piece is very long because, dramatically, some provinces had many leaves. I wanted it to take time to explore completely, as a way of respecting the story it tells.

## Considering the reader’s time and actions

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L06 · 04:48–05:16

When designing visual narratives, I think it is important to consider how readers will use the projects, what actions they need to take, and how much time they will need. In the next lesson, we will see how to use and sometimes challenge visual metaphors.
