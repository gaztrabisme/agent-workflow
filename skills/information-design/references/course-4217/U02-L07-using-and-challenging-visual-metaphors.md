## Choosing whether to use a visual metaphor

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L07 · 00:00–00:45

I want to talk about how we can use, and sometimes challenge, visual metaphors in data visualization to create new environments that readers can immerse themselves in. I do not always use metaphors and do not want to force them, but sometimes I like to reflect the content visually.

La Lettura asked me to design a visualization about the Italian painter Piero della Francesca. I began studying his work while looking for data about him.

## Building a drapery from the locations

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L07 · 00:45–01:54

During my research, I chose to focus the piece on the geography of his work (captions: “Niamh’s work”), showing the cities that currently host Piero della Francesca’s masterpieces. After collecting the data, I began thinking about how to visualize it. A map was the straightforward representation, but I also wanted the design to visually engage with his work. I studied it again to find an element I could replicate, and I loved the shapes and shadows made by its draperies.

After plotting the cities on the page, I designed curved lines to connect them and then used that structure to draw a drapery. It is not highly figurative; it is an abstract reference to his work. I think it helped create a stronger relationship between the story and its subject. I then added information about the paintings and a legend.

## Letting forms emerge from the data

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L07 · 01:54–02:25

I also like it when the data in the story itself suggest the shapes to use (captions: “store”). I love this quote by the Italian designer Bruno Munari: “How are the forms we have defined organic formed? Perhaps we can understand more if we provoke these forms, so that they reveal themselves with the same naturalness with which a river, when forming, reveals its shape, its insertion into nature.” The captions render the final words as “They raise its shade. It’s insertion into my tour.” I like to look for the same link between naturalness and the creation of forms in my work.

## Shaping stories of migrant essential workers

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L07 · 02:25–03:56

In 2020, the global think tank ODI contacted me. It was collecting stories about migrant essential workers’ contributions to the COVID-19 response and asked me to give them a shape. I started with a table. Each row was one story, with information about sectors such as healthcare or agriculture and levels such as local, regional, or national.

![4217 · U2 · L07 · 02:54.30](frames/U02L07-F0254.30.jpg)

On screen: "Migrant essential workers’ contribution to the Covid-19 response" / "Concept by Marta Foresti from ODI" / "COVID-19 and Key Workers Tracker" / "Policies & Actions" / "Geography" / "Country" / "Level" / "Resource" / "Description" / "Sector" / "Profession" / "Target" / "Target size" / "Key words" / "Date" [rest omitted]  
Shows: A spreadsheet titled COVID-19 and Key Workers Tracker displays rows of policies and actions.

The final project is the *Key workers* website, which I designed with Alex Piacentini. These are stories of contributions that come from within countries, which is why I used a simple image of a tree. Each tree represents a geographic region, each red dot a story, and the branches show different sectors and levels. I did not force the structure of the visualization; it came naturally from the data.

![4217 · U2 · L07 · 03:46.10](frames/U02L07-F0346.10.jpg)

On screen: "Key workers" / "Design by Federica Fragapane and Alex Piacentini for ODI" / "How to explore" / "Each dot represents a reform, new initiative or campaign." / "Larger branches represent sectors, from healthcare to agriculture. Smaller branches represent levels of action, from the national to the local." / "Each tree represents a geographic region." [rest omitted]  
Shows: A tree diagram is paired with instructions explaining what its dots, branches, and overall form represent.

Users can interact with the trees, read the stories, and explore them. Giving a new shape to these datasets helps reveal hidden stories that have already been told but are often undervalued or taken for granted. What is growing, as represented by the trees, is our awareness of those contributions.

## Challenging the usual migration metaphors

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L07 · 03:56–05:25

Sometimes it can also be interesting to challenge visual metaphors. I worked again with ODI on a piece about migration. This time they asked me to visualize data about public narratives and attitudes towards migrants in European countries. Migration is often shown with arrows and flows, or described using words such as “waves.” Sometimes these images carry negative feelings that do not help us understand the subject.

![4217 · U2 · L07 · 04:15.70](frames/U02L07-F0415.70.jpg)

On screen: "Hearts and minds" / "Design by Federica Fragapane and Alex Piacentini for ODI" / "How Europeans think and feel about immigration" / "Germany" / "Spain" / "United Kingdom" / "Italy" / "France" / "Sweden" [rest omitted]  
Shows: A project landing page presents mountain-like forms with country labels and explanatory text.

Migration is not always an emergency. It is a natural, slow phenomenon that is part of our history, even if the reasons behind it are artificial. That is why, in this case, I proposed another metaphor to challenge the usual one: mountains and stratifications. The creation of a mountain is a natural process that is part of our planet’s history. I worked on this image to talk about immigration and its perception.

![4217 · U2 · L07 · 04:44.10](frames/U02L07-F0444.10.jpg)

Shows: The project’s entry screen uses a layered mountain range above the title and a start button.

The project is called *Hearts and minds* (captions: “Arts and Minds”). It is a website, and I co-designed it with Alex Piacentini.
