## Choosing a topic and a trustworthy source

Anchor: 4217 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–01:39

*4217 · U03 · L08 · 00:00–01:39*

This lesson begins the first phase of the project: finding data for a story. Start with a reliable source. I often use the World Bank, FAO, and Our World in Data (captions: “our wording data”); I am using Our World in Data here because it has a helpful feature that lets you find datasets by topic.

Choose a subject you are passionate or curious about, or one that speaks to you. That matters especially for side projects, when you are exploring data visualization or building a portfolio. A project also tells the outside world what you want to talk about and how you want to do it. I might choose social or environmental topics, literature, or art. For this project, I want to talk about energy and the environment. The topic menu offers many subjects; I am particularly interested in renewable energy.

## Exploring the renewable-energy data

Anchor: 4217 · U3 · L08 · 01:39–03:39

*4217 · U03 · L08 · 01:39–03:39*

The renewable-energy page offers tables and datasets as well as contextual information and articles, which is useful when developing a storytelling piece. Its first visualization is a map showing the share of primary energy from renewable sources in 2020. I am interested in using this information.

The interactive view gives an initial overview on a map. The timeline lets you compare years. You can select specific countries and see how their data behave. The table shows the numbers behind the map and charts. The Sources view tells you where the data came from, which is an important feature.

There are several download options. You could save the map as a PNG image or an SVG vector graphic, but that is not what I need now. I want the dataset as a CSV file. CSV means “comma-separated values”: a delimited text file that uses commas to separate its values. I choose that option and download the data.

## Adding per-capita and regional context

Anchor: 4217 · U3 · L08 · 03:39–05:28

*4217 · U03 · L08 · 03:39–05:28*

The share of renewable energy will be the first information in the project, but I want to add layers of data for a more interesting overview. Scrolling down the page, I find related charts. The first is about per-capita generation of renewable energy, and I think per-capita data are interesting too. This page also presents a map, a chart, a table, and source details. I download the dataset as well. I now have my first two tables, which we will explore together.

I will not display the data on a map as this site does, but it would still be useful to include geographical information by grouping countries into macroregions. I often do this because it adds a narrative layer and provides an initial point of view (captions: “first-person eye view”). There is no single universal way to classify regions; different regional specifications exist.

## Adding population data

Anchor: 4217 · U3 · L08 · 06:50–08:44

*4217 · U03 · L08 · 06:50–08:44*

To recap, I have the share of primary energy from renewable sources, per-capita energy consumption, and the world-region information. Later, we will select some countries rather than visualize them all. I want one last dataset to add context: population.

For that, I use the World Bank, the other source I mentioned earlier. Links to the sources are in the Additional Resources section. The World Bank lets you explore data by country or indicator. Since I already know what I need, I search for “population.” The page plots the data as a line graph, a bar chart, or a map; in the bar-chart view, only the world is selected. Scrolling down reveals the underlying data. The most recent year shown is 2020, which matches the year in the Our World in Data datasets, so it is a good fit. I download the CSV file. Now that we have a topic, the next lesson will build the dataset together.
