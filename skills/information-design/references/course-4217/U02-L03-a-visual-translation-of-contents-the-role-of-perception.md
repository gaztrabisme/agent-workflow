## Information design turns content into visual words

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L03 · 00:00–01:32

In this lesson, I introduce data visualization, its potential for communicating stories, and the role of perception. I work as an information designer. Information design is the practice of visually representing content so people can explore and understand it. I translate content visually with shapes and other visual elements, designing data visualizations. I like Alberto Cairo’s definition: a data visualization is a display of data designed to enable analysis, exploration, and discovery. Cairo is a journalist and designer and holds a Knight Chair in Visual Journalism at the University of Miami’s School of Communication (captions: “an High Chair”).

Visual translations can be strong communication tools. They can support people as they explore and understand a topic, and help them discover something new. To visualize data, I use visual variables: position in space, size, shape, opacity, color, orientation, texture, line thickness, and multiple elements. I think of these as visual letters. Combining the letters makes visual words that let me narrate visual stories. The combinations can be simple and minimal or more intricate and experimental, depending on what the work needs.

## Choose visual channels by the precision they offer

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L03 · 02:18–04:02

It is important to know the basics of what makes a visualization clear. The additional resources include a review paper on guidelines for clear data visualizations. Its authors rank five commonly used visual channels by how precisely they let viewers compare numerical values. Knowing the ranking helps a designer decide which information to show most precisely: position first, then length, area, angle, and intensity.

Position is used in a line chart and a scatter plot. In the scatter plot, circles are positioned on horizontal and vertical axes according to two dimensions. A span chart compares lengths along a vertical axis. A bubble chart adds area to the two position dimensions, so it communicates a third dimension. A tree map also uses area: it displays data as nested rectangles whose areas are proportional to the represented values. Lines with different inclinations or angles, and with different widths, can compare data as well. Intensity can show values through opacity or a color scale.

## Account for perception and make scales honest

Anchor: 4217 · U2 · L03 · 04:02–05:37

The review paper also points to perceptual issues. For color, consider color-vision impairments. A fair suggestion is to avoid a green–red scale and make palettes safer for color blindness. Color Oracle is a free simulator for checking whether a palette is inclusive; its link is in the additional resources section. I also add indicators for values represented with color. For example, in a visualization of endemic species by country across several taxonomic groups, I identify the groups with both color and an initial.

When values appear on an axis, including zero in the scale is recommended because it shows differences accurately. A non-zero axis can be used as a trick to emphasize differences at the expense of clarity and intellectual honesty. There may be cases where that intervention helps reveal meaningful differences, but viewers tend to overestimate them even when the non-zero base is clearly marked.
