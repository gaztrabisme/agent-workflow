# The five elements

Use the time rule first: past knowledge and the motivating gap are **Context**; the
present argument contains **Claim**, **Evidence**, and **Reasoning**; future use and
work are **Implications**. Within the present argument, ask what was collected or built,
what was measured, derived, or compared, and what new assertion the authors make.

| Element | What it is | Common signals |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | Any argument the authors are making, including secondary or broad assertions; it need not directly answer the stated research question. | we find · we report · we show · we demonstrate · we detected |
| **Evidence** | What the authors collected or built to support a claim: data, observations, instruments, samples, models, or a simulation library. | we collected · we observed · we assembled · we surveyed · we simulated · using |
| **Reasoning** | Analysis and logic that connect evidence to a claim: measurements, derivations, comparisons, alternative explanations, and the inferences that follow. | indicates · consistent with · we derive · we compare · rules out |
| **Implications** | Why the result matters and what may follow: future work, use, or consequence. | this suggests · future work · will allow · remains to be |
| **Context** | What was known before the work and the gap that motivated it. | prior work found · however · remains open · had not been |

A Claim includes any argument the authors are trying to make, not only new knowledge
directly answering the research question. [book:research-methods-psychology], Three Claims

A **subclaim** is a subordinate claim that must hold for the main claim to hold. A
**hypothesis** is an untested claim. A **research gap** is the missing knowledge that
motivates the study. [book:critical-thinking-about-research], 2 Research Questions and Hypotheses

## Claim types and the support they need

Classify research claims as **frequency** (one variable's level), **association** (two or
more variables go together), or **causal** (one variable is said to change another).
The study must fit the kind of claim: frequency claims need an appropriate measure and
sample; association claims need appropriate measurements and evidence of the relationship;
causal claims need covariance, temporal precedence, and internal validity. Do not turn an
association into a causal claim. [book:research-methods-psychology], Three Claims

The four validity dimensions and the claim-specific questions are in
`grading.md` §Claim audit. A study is not simply valid or invalid: name the dimension
being assessed.

## Where elements may appear

Use the map as a set of leads, not a complete or exclusive index.

| Part of a source | Likely elements to look for |
|---|---|
| Title | Claim, when the title states the result |
| Abstract or executive summary | Compressed versions of all five elements |
| Introduction or background | Context, gap, rationale, and sometimes Claim |
| Methods or approach | Evidence and Reasoning |
| Results, figures, tables, findings | Evidence, Reasoning, and subclaims |
| Discussion, conclusion, recommendations | Claim, Reasoning, and Implications |

The section map is a reading aid: the introduction gives bearings, methods often explain
what was done, and later sections interpret results. [book:how-to-read-a-paper], Three preliminary questions to get your bearings; [book:how-to-read-a-paper], Summing up; [book:how-to-read-a-paper], Probability and confidence; [book:research-methods-psychology], Finding and Reading the Research; [book:research-methods-psychology], Presenting Results: APA-Style Reports and Conference Posters

Papers may use IMRaD, while reports, standards, theses, and supplements may organize
information differently. First use the table of contents, headings, appendices, and
cross-references to make a genre-aware map. For a source with no useful map, search the
whole document with the planned questions. Do not treat an unmapped section as absent;
check it before recording a missing element.

## Boundary rules

1. **Measured results are Reasoning, not Evidence.** The instrument, campaign, sample, or
   simulation library is Evidence. A result measured in the data, its stability across
   methods, a model comparison, or a derived mass is Reasoning. Ask whether the sentence
   could exist before the analysis ran. If yes, it may be Evidence; if it reports what
   analysis found, it is Reasoning.
2. **A broad conclusion can be a Claim.** A new assertion about what the results establish
   is a Claim, including a secondary claim. What can be done next is an Implication.
3. **Analysis is not itself a Claim; a future plan is not a Claim.** Analysis supports a
   claim. A proposed next study belongs under Implications.
4. **A derived number is usually Reasoning, even in a headline.** If the title itself
   asserts that number, mark the title's assertion as Claim.
5. **A hedge belongs to the element it qualifies.** Preserve words such as *may*,
   *candidate*, *consistent with*, *appears*, *not inconsistent with*, and *cannot be
   excluded*. Do not replace them with *proves*, *confirms*, *definitive*, or *certain*
   unless the source makes that same assertion. If the strength shifts between sections,
   record the shift as a finding. [book:research-methods-psychology], Three Claims
6. **Do not infer Context from implication alone.** Words such as *first* or *newly* imply
   earlier work but do not name it. Classify the phrase by what it says and record unnamed
   prior work as missing Context.
7. **Keep a method and its rationale distinct.** A method statement is Evidence; a stated
   reason the method is trustworthy is Reasoning.
8. **A phrase may carry two marks.** Give each mark its own reason, or choose the single
   element that best captures the phrase.

## Missing is a finding

For every absent or thin element, record what is missing, why it matters, and a question
that could follow. Do not fill the space from memory, another paper, an unavailable
companion paper, or what the authors must have meant. Outside definitions are allowed only
when marked † and attributed to their page or to the reader's own definition.

## Worked example: M87 abstract

The Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration's 2019 M87 abstract shows why these boundaries
matter. The title asserts a shadow (**Claim**); the assembled telescope is **Evidence**;
the resolved ring, its measured size and stability, model comparisons, and derived mass are
**Reasoning**; the broad assertion about black holes is a secondary **Claim**; and a new
tool for studying gravity is an **Implication**. Keep the source's cautious wording when
paraphrasing a result described as consistent with a model.
