## Reaching the essential

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L14 · 00:27–01:14

We have now reached the essential. We have been developing images that are more and more limited, not to say simplified, taking away more and more elements to reach a purer, simpler result: the quintessence of an image. We keep the same subject and limit the amount of resources, in this case the complexity of the shapes and the number of colors. That is what I call the essential (*lo esencial*).

In this lesson and the following ones we will see how limiting our options in color forces us to make the right decisions about color and the right decisions about form. But again, this course is above all about color, so color is what we will look at in this lesson and the next ones.

## A color code: Mexico and McDonald's

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L14 · 02:03–03:27

What they do is establish a color code, certain colors that go together, and develop them through a long and very broad communication campaign. In the case of a country, for example, this lets it use its colors, the colors of the flag, to create a recognizable brand. When we see the players of El Tri, the Mexican national team, we see the green, the white and the red, and we know it is the Mexican team. And when their fans paint these same colors on their faces, we immediately recognize the Mexico brand.

The same happens with McDonald's. It is so popular now that it only needs to show the yellow and the red, which are the hallmark of its brand, and the shape of its fries box for us to know it is McDonald's. I could have removed the McDonald's name here and we would still know the reference was to McDonald's. Same thing here: we can suggest a hamburger made of mobile phones and it is already understood to be McDonald's. At most, we can dress up a creepy, super weird guy (I hate this character because he scares me), put yellow and red on him, and we have our McDonald's character. We would know that in some way he refers to, or has to do with, the McDonald's universe.

## Form plus color creates identity

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L14 · 03:27–04:03

That is what we do, and this is what we need to understand. We humans take types of colors, in this case dark colors, and we take shapes, in this case a monster, and we mix them, and we create meaning. We create identity. The word identity is very important: it is what a McDonald's does, it creates a recognizable identity. And it is what we do when we put a monster with dark colors: we create a feeling, an idea of something terrifying, something creepy. That is how we are programmed to receive this mix of dark colors with monsters.

## Why dark colors frighten us

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L14 · 05:14–05:49

Here is my little explanation of why dark colors frighten us so much: they create ambiguity. What is most frightening here, besides all these dark colors that speak of dirt, or perhaps dried blood or something like that, is that they do not let us see her face (captions: *nos dejan ver su cara*). So there is a lot that is unknown. That is why we fear the dark: in the dark, at night, many things are not clear. We do not know whether a monster is hiding in these shadows or whether it is just two boxes and a broom that we think is a monster. Darkness gives us this feeling of not knowing and of potential danger.

## A harmless shape made terrifying

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L14 · 05:49–06:47

Do you think we can create danger, a sinister feeling, with dark colors, now adding stronger, more aggressive colors such as red, as we have seen, but using a potentially harmless shape such as the peacock? Yes, we can, as long as we know how to build the right, appropriate shapes. But above all, if we manage to create this palette with dark colors, red tones everywhere and a good variation of saturation, we can create a terrifying or dramatic scene with a character as gentle in theory as the peacock (captions: *Pabrel*, for *pavo real*).

And I have to confess that this is one of the great secrets of this course. It is the key to visual communication, because in the end that is what we artists do: we create images made to communicate ideas and concepts.

## Color palettes in animated films

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L14 · 06:47–08:37

This is very visible in animated films. What they do is create a tendency, a color palette, which we can see summarized here. You can check the work of this person on their site or blog, called Movies in Color (it will be in the resources), where they break down the palettes of certain animated films. It is very interesting because it gives us the general code of what the film is.

What animated films do is set out this color code and plan very meticulously how it will develop over the course of the film; that is the color script exercise we have already seen. Then, once we see the final result, we can see a certain continuity: they use the same type of color, so the whole film feels as if it works cohesively around a theme, so to speak. All these colors keep repeating, even in the most dramatic or enigmatic moments. The whole palette, and this way of using and developing it, is organized to create a feeling, so that the audience receives the message of the story in this way: through these key words, through this identity.

The film with this palette is going to be fun, colorful and aquatic. I write "fun" and not the Spanish word because I find it hard to translate this very American, very Hollywood definition of fun. They use it all the time, and fun does not mean only amusing but entertaining. Even here, in something that is not amusing in itself, it is entertaining because the shapes are a bit grotesque and the color is very pleasant. It is a term I hear a lot every time I work with Hollywood studios, and it is very important to have… (the sentence breaks off).

But what matters most to us is this: when we see the final result of what these great artists do, there is a feeling we all share. Looking at all this, we agree that the film corresponds to these key terms, to this identity, to these concepts.

## Brands do the same

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L14 · 08:37–10:12

Brands do exactly the same. That is why this whole lesson is meant to draw this parallel between brands, images, animated films and so on. What I want you to see is that everything we do is visual communication. And that is why in this last unit we are limiting our resources as much as possible: so you see that even with limited resources, as a brand like Apple does with its commercials and things like that, or even objects, you can say a lot, as long as the decisions we make about color are coherent and serve a purpose. That purpose is to anchor in people's memory and minds that everything we are now organizing in color, values and saturation is meant to represent these terms: elegance, luxury, sobriety (captions: *lujos sobre edad*).

There is no magic formula that says "ah, that is an elegant palette, that is a luxurious one." At the end of this lesson and in the next lesson we will do exactly this exercise of trying to put these terms and others into images, and you will see that it is a back-and-forth. That is where our mastery comes in, the expertise we looked at at the start of this course in recognizing what is elegant and what is not, so that we can reach an image that reflects it, basically by trial and error.

## In Photoshop: the elegant version

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L14 · 13:00–13:50

Here what I did was basically reduce the number of colors. As for form, I simplified this shape, because, if you remember, in Apple's identity everything is simple, everything is as minimalist as possible. This word, minimalist, is important. I could have kept my details and all that, but see how they limit the feeling of sobriety: they add too much play. Here instead we are in something more refined, simpler. Basically the key to all this is to reduce saturation and reduce the range of colors. There are not many colors. In general, everything is cold, and over these cold tones you add, now and then, very gradually, the odd warm tone. That is how we create, or translate, these concepts into images.

## Closing

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L14 · 13:50–14:17

Did you see how color can be even stronger than form, for creating memorable moments, striking brands, or even for manipulating what your audience feels? So do not forget that this great, great power comes with great responsibility.

### Key points (derived)

- Form plus color creates identity; brands, countries, artists and films fix a color code and repeat it until the colors alone are recognized.
- Color can carry a feeling more strongly than form: dark colors with cold greens read as disturbing, dark colors with reds and varied saturation can make even a peacock menacing.
- Dark colors frighten because they create ambiguity: what is hidden is unknown.
- Animated films plan a palette and its development (the color script) so the whole film coheres around key words such as "fun, colorful, aquatic".
- There is no formula for a palette that says "elegant"; reach it by trial and error, using your eye for what is and is not elegant.
- Fun version: more colors and contrast, textures that bring other hues into a flat area at the same value, a rare color that echoes another part of the image.
- Elegant, sober version: fewer colors, lower saturation, simplified shapes, mostly cold tones with an occasional warm accent.
