## Texture as the essence of reality: high-frequency detail

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L12 · 00:25–01:23

Over the years I have arrived at a very particular definition of textures. As I said, for me they are not brushstrokes in Photoshop; they are the very essence of reality.

This is a photo; this is reality. What I want you to notice is the number of tiny details everywhere. Almost no image is flat. If I pick a color here and say "I'll paint this part with it", you start to see the difference, because in a photograph the color changes all the time. There are very subtle gradients and a great many small details that create the realistic feeling. This is what we call high-frequency detail (*detalle de alta frecuencia*).

Why am I explaining this? Because these details are what we are trying to emulate or represent with textures, and they are what create the most interest in an image, by producing a great deal of very subtle color variation (captions: *muy suficiente*, "very sufficient").

## A scale of styles, from realistic to stylized

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L12 · 01:23–03:33

Here I have a small scale of styles, going from a realistic photo to a very, very stylized illustration. I tried to find photos and references with the same subject, in this case a lake and a boat, so the comparison is easier to see.

In concept art for video games, like this image, photographic details are used, so the finish is almost the same as a photo.

When we move to a more illustrated style, we start to have less detail than in reality. For example, if we look at these leaves, we have flat patches of color (*plastas de color*) and little variation. Even so, textural details are being used. For me, textural detail is everything that would be painted on top of an object. So texture can be the roof tiles, because from a distance we see them as a kind of pattern. That is the word: textures are also patterns. In the case of a tree, for example, these stripes are also textures. Everything that becomes high-frequency detail, meaning very small detail, starts to create texture.

In even more simplified illustrations, where the style is simpler and we want the shapes to be simpler, you have surely seen this kind of texture a thousand times: it simulates a kind of grain. But here it is being used to create color accidents (*accidentes de color*). Look how these little textures create greens that, as we have seen, help to harmonize the whole image and create the bridge color between our yellows and our blues.

In this illustration, the most simplified one, texture as such has disappeared. We have nothing but flat color. What we still have are several details that create a bit of texture by themselves, like these little dots, or these lines, which work like a pattern. Texture is always present in some form. There is also an extreme simplification of the patterns inside certain elements that makes them recognizable, as with the water lily here: without these lines, the lily could be mistaken for something else.

That is the evolution of texture, from the photorealistic, with a very high density of detail, to the simplest.

## Grass texture and the key word: variety

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L12 · 04:22–05:59

You can see it here on this ground. This is a texture. Its function is to simulate grass: just as in real grass some parts are greener and others are more yellowish, drier, more burnt. Or just as waves in the sea always generate a kind of pattern, in these illustrations we look for that with textures, meaning with somewhat abstract patterns that come close to what the textural detail of the object should be (captions: *DTE textural*).

But look at the richness it gives this illustration. Here in the grass it lets us create a lot of evolution, a lot of variation, a lot of difference in tones, so we can build a complete, varied palette. Variety is the key word of this lesson: textures allow us to create variety, and variety is what you want most in your image.

Once you have established your large color groups (all of this is globally green), and once you have established your bridges and everything else we have seen so far, then, inside my large green group, I can add different tones close to the general color to create a bit of this illusion of life.

The eye asks for variety because it exists in reality. What we do in drawing is exaggerate it, to create the equivalent of a marvellous scene that is real, but more than real: the kind of reality we would like to see every day but that belongs only to certain very special moments.

## Texture is density of detail

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L12 · 06:35–08:08

Another point: we have seen that texture is really the density of detail. So, speaking very generally, we can treat details like these small stones or rocks, at the scale of the whole image, as textural details. They are detail frequency that adds realism by adding a certain level of detail. Even the birds can be considered textures (captions: *texturistas*), or this little texture in the sea. If we did without these details, the image would be much flatter (captions: *plata*). Let's think of it this way: our images need texture.

Same example here. We actually have large blocks of shape, and the fact that there is so much detail inside each shape group works as texture (captions: *texto*). But that is not what matters most to us. What matters most is that here the texture resource, which is meant to simulate painted wood or paint strokes on wood, is a great pretext for creating variety in the colors. And inside this large shape, objects also simulate texture in a way, and they too are pretexts for adding more color variety. Seen from a distance, this whole color range seems to be a single one, a kind of large gray, but inside there is endless color variety that gives our image a great deal of richness, variety and interest. That is the secret of textures.

## Demo: subtle color variation in the trees

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L12 · 08:54–11:04

Now let's make our own attempts at creating textures. I have taken the trees from our illustration, and I am simply going to use textures and textured brushes to create some of this variety.

I take this layer, which is already separated, create another layer above it, and link them by pressing Alt between the two layers. That turns the top layer into what is called a clipping mask: everything I draw from now on will only appear inside the pixels of the layer below.

Before applying the color of the light, I want to create a very subtle variation inside my tree. This is my green. Since this is the shadow side, and we have seen that it is lit by a blue light, I will try adding a little blue. The key point is in this small window of the color picker: the lower color is the original color of the tree, and the upper one is my new color. I will try to make them look as if they have the same value. I will not go up much or down much, because I only want variation in color and very little in value.

I will give you a set of brushes as attachments to this course. We are looking for a texture that can somehow suggest foliage and lets us create this variation. This is probably too light; it shows too much. You have to keep looking for the right tone, or maybe it is too blue and we can be more subtle. I go back to my original color and move back so it is not so noticeable. That is what I want.

Then all I do is make very subtle passes of this blue. With just this I create the feeling that there is something more. It hardly changes the nature of the color, but I add an extra detail, and I give the feeling that this color is not just a flat patch but is made of more things, as it is in reality (captions: *en la red*, "in the network").

## Automatic color variation with Color Dynamics

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L12 · 13:40–16:03

I will give you a very important trick connected to what we just saw: how can we create color variety automatically? We will look at two things: first, how to create color automatically, and then what kind of feeling we can get with it.

Here I am taking a brush that has this grass feel (captions: *sensación de paz*, "feeling of peace"), which is good because here I am simulating this grass. What I just did was make a selection of this shadow and create a layer where I used that selection as a clipping mask. Now I want to take this color and paint, but of course nothing shows. And I do not want to do the same as before, painting with a different color inside.

What I do instead is take my brush, open my brush settings, go to Color Dynamics (*Dinámica de color*) and activate it. It will take the color I choose, which here is the color of the shadow, and then I choose another color. I start from the shadow color again and do the same trick as before: since I want a relatively cool shadow, I move it toward green, maybe a little lighter. Now I have these two colors, and Photoshop will take them automatically and place them randomly. That is great, because it is creating this feeling of texture and color variation.

But I can go much further. For example, if I tell it I want more variation between these two tones, it starts to become much bluer. Seen from a distance, this starts to be quite interesting: from a completely flat shadow, I start to have this bluish feeling. That is a great trick for creating more interesting shadows or flat areas.

We can go further with the hue variation (*variación de tono*) setting. We can increase it a lot, and then it gets completely crazy: it puts in any color at all, and that no longer suits me. But in a much smaller amount, why not? Look how a few pinks start to appear here, and from a distance my shadow still reads as a blue shadow. That is very good. It is a great way to create texture and variation within our colors.

## Optical gray: juxtaposed complementary colors

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L12 · 16:03–18:21

Now I want to show you the real master trick. There is something called optical gray (*gris óptico*). In fact, it is already working a bit here: up close we see all the colors, which are actually contrasting colors, reds and blues, but from a distance our eye no longer perceives the difference and reads it as a gray patch (captions: *plaza gris*). This is a great trick for generating visual interest.

Here we can take another kind of texture, probably flatter. We look for a complementary color of this blue. Starting from this color, its complement would be a kind of fuchsia or something similar. We look for the version of this color that barely separates from the green on the other side. We only want fairly spaced-out patches.

What I find easiest most of the time is to put the color down rather than searching for it swatch by swatch, and then press Ctrl+U, Hue/Saturation, and adjust it. Most of the time we will have to lighten it. Look at this result. You have to play with saturation (captions: *Conor. C3.*, unclear) until you find something that works, when the values blend. Look how pretty and rich it looks. If it is too bright, we can lower the opacity. It is an incredible trick: from a distance it normally will not compromise the feeling of shadow, but it adds a lot of variation and richness inside it.

Ideally, once we have done this, we should look for the same kind of tone, but complementary, to put beside it, because I notice that from a distance the pink shows too much. What we want is for the two to be juxtaposed. So it is a matter of finding the precise tone at which they start to live together and give us a lot of interest inside our shadow.

That is called optical gray: two complementary colors are juxtaposed, taking care that they do not create a difference in value. That is what happens here: there is none. If we look in black and white and I remove my textures, nothing changes. I have not altered the value, yet I have added a lot of interest in color.

## Recap: textures are an interpretation of reality

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L12 · 18:21–19:09

To recap, the most important thing to remember from this lesson is the idea that textures are not brushstrokes. They are an interpretation of reality, and we use them to achieve what is probably the most important aspect of color, or at least the most eye-catching and the one that looks best in a final result: variety. Varied tones on top of a robust, solid, well-thought-out structure.

Did you see how textures are a little treasure when we know how to use them? In the next lesson I will show you small tricks I use to finish my images and make them more attractive.

### Key points (derived)

- Texture means high-frequency detail and pattern, not only brush marks; it is present in some form in every style from photo to flat graphic.
- The simpler and more graphic the style, the more textures must be used deliberately to create color accidents.
- Use texture to add variety of hue inside an established color group while keeping value almost unchanged, so the image structure stays intact.
- Photoshop workflow: clipped layer (Alt-click between layers), locked pixels, textured brushes, same-value hue shifts for variation, lighter yellower strokes for light, darker bluer strokes for cast shadows.
- Brush Color Dynamics (foreground/background and hue jitter) produces automatic color variation; keep hue jitter low.
- Optical gray: juxtapose complementary colors of equal value; up close they vibrate, from a distance they read as one tone, and a grayscale check shows no value change.
