# Lesson 11 · Rhythm and balance

## What this lesson covers

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L11 · 00:00–01:01

In this lesson I explain why we must build the rules of physics into our compositions; if we don't, everything collapses. I also explain why, as human beings, we need to find a certain organization, repetition and surprise in the images we look at.

That is what rhythm (*ritmo*) and balance (*equilibrio*) are: two concepts that really work hand in hand.

The first thing we look at, once again, is these images that look very bad because they lack rhythm and balance. Right now that may not make much sense to you, but it will once we have explained a little what we mean by rhythm and balance, and why these images have no rhythm, at least in the musical sense, the way any good image should.

## Rhythm: sound and silence

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L11 · 01:01–02:40

Rhythm, as I have seen and understood it in my 12 or 13 years of experience, has a very interesting parallel between its auditory version and its visual counterpart.

By "interesting" we mean the same as in music: there is a beat, a rhythm, a base. On top of it there are notes, elements that add variety and interest over that fixed base. ## Balance

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L11 · 03:45–04:32

Before talking about color as such, let's explain a little more the second concept we want to cover here: balance. Basically, balance means giving our eye the satisfaction of balance. Why? Because we are used to knowing that if there is an excess of elements on one side and a lack of elements on the other, we will very probably start to create an imbalance. Like when everybody is on one side of a bus: that side sinks, right?

## Balance through shape and color

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L11 · 04:32–06:00

Let's finally look at this with color. What matters here is understanding that shapes are what create balance. In this image we see many shapes running on diagonals, as if a centrifugal force in this part of the image were pulling everything, or at least everything were sliding down towards this point. However, this triangular shape comes in to balance the image. We have a lot of diagonal force, and this shape that gives balance.

Same trick. If we take away this red, reduce its strength with saturation and push it more toward purples so it is closer to what happens on the ground, we see that the red, which had the strength we mentioned, has now gone completely slack. The contrast has dropped, and as a result the balance of this image is compromised. It would seem that now, with no counterweight to mark the gravity, so to speak, the whole image is starting to collapse.

You will remember that I had created this green as a bridge (*puente*) between my yellow and my purple, and I had also created these cooler greens to complete the bridge.

What I can do now to harmonize all these blocks is use the color of the light. Here I am assuming that the light is fairly overhead (*cenital*); we can tell from the shadow, which is not cast very far. That means my sun must be more or less vertical, or slightly diagonal. What does that tell us? That it is probably between 12 and 3 o'clock. And what does that imply? That the light can be slightly yellowish: it is sunlight. So we can have the feeling that the light is not completely white. This is very interesting because my ground is yellow. Having the sense that the light is a little yellowish and tints all the elements will let me harmonize the color of the ground with all the other colors.

How does that work? Let's see it here. If I take this color, it is a green very far toward the "south" (*sur*). However, remember the lesson on light: we saw that if we have an object and the light has a color, the object starts to take on that color in its lit part. So the color I chose to light these objects is not just this green made lighter. We can see it is a color shifted toward the yellows; it moves over to here.

## Direct and indirect light in the shadow

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L11 · 12:31–13:55

It works the same way with these greens. This green is here; this value is more yellowish. Same thing here: everywhere I have used this lighting, I have tried to apply this principle. I did not mention it at the time, but it is the same here.

Why is my shadow green? Simply because the lighting coming from the sun is yellow, but there is a secondary lighting that comes from the sky. This is direct light; this is indirect light, because it is filtered and diffused by the atmosphere. In every daytime exterior scene we always have to take into account the sun's lighting, which in this case will be a little more yellowish, and the lighting in the shadow, which in this case will be a little cooler.

So in the shadow it will probably take on a tint and move much closer to blue, the blue of the sky. Look at this jump here: we go from yellow to green.

Once again, this is a great tool because it lets us harmonize the color of this shadow with the color of the sky and, in this case on purpose, with the color of the trees, so that little by little the image feels like one thing, a coherent whole.

## Summary

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L11 · 13:55–15:01

In summary, the most important thing to remember from this lesson is that we have to try to create rhythms in our images and move away from anything where everything is static, boring and repetitive. That applies to elements as well as to color.

We have also seen that we have to take care of balance: our eye asks for the things in our image to be balanced. And we have seen that this balance can be achieved with shapes, but above all with color.

### Key points (derived)

- Rhythm is built from sound (shapes, positive space) and silence (voids, negative space); a steady base with varied notes on top is more interesting than even spacing.
- Balance comes from counterweights: an excess on one side needs a counterpart, or the image feels like it will fall.
- Color can carry balance: desaturating the cyan triangle in the Klaus hill image or the red glow in the forest image removes the counterweight.
- In the course illustration: tree trunks placed with rhythm, flow lines and a pink "family" to guide the eye, and yellowish sunlight plus cooler sky-lit shadows used as color bridges.
