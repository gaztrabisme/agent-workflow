## Two types of reflection

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L06 · 00:54–02:03

You know very well, because it is explained to you from childhood, that we see things because light reflects off them and reaches our eye. It is an interesting principle, but we can take our understanding of this phenomenon a little further, because as artists we will have to represent it all the time.

The important thing is that, depending on the type of material the light bounces off, two types of reflection appear.

If the surface the light bounces off is completely flat, the bounce is perfect. The light arrives in one direction and leaves in the other; all the rays arrive parallel and all the rays leave parallel. It sounds a bit technical and physical, but don't worry, you will understand it very soon, with images.

The other type of reflection happens when the surface is rough. We will call it a diffuse surface (*superficie difusa*), precisely because it has the effect of blurring the bounce of the light. Depending on which part of the roughness the light falls on, it starts bouncing everywhere and becomes diffused.

## Mirrors, disco balls and diffuse bounces

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L06 · 02:03–03:15

That sounds very technical and not very encouraging for us artists, but it has interesting consequences.

A perfectly smooth reflective surface causes effects like these. No shadow appears on a reflective surface; it is a mirror, and it translates reality completely faithfully. All the light that reaches this person bounces straight to the mirror, and the mirror bounces it straight to us, creating a perfect reflection. The same thing happens if light hits a disco ball: it creates these artifacts, which are nothing more nor less than the light bouncing off each of the little squares and reflecting perfectly into the distance.

With diffuse bounces we get effects like these. The light bounces off this flower, for example, and spreads very softly around it; it does not reflect the reality of what is happening here at all. Same thing here: the light bounces over here and creates a whole, very diffuse gradient, and even very diffuse shadows.

## Lighting is a diffuse reflection of the surroundings

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L06 · 03:15–05:03

These two types of material condition everything, and above all they give us a completely new reading of what lighting is. When I understood this principle, I swear it was like: it can't be that this is the reality of what I have been looking at and analysing for so long. Let me explain this moment of revelation.

A totally reflective surface, as its name says, reflects everything. Here, for example, we can see the sun: it is the brightest part of this reflection, but it is nothing more nor less than one of the elements around this sphere that is being reflected.

On a slightly less reflective surface, things start to get blurry, but the reflections can still be told apart. Here we can make out the reflection of this sphere, here the reflection of the light source, and so on.

The more diffuse the surface becomes, the closer we get to "classic" lighting, in quotation marks. But notice that the elements are still here: the light source, the reflection of the other spheres and the reflection of the floor.

And when we reach a totally rough surface, we get this. It is an extremely blurred version, but it is still the reflection. The light source is still here, and my colours are still here too: the green for the floor, the blue for the sky. You can still feel that the lower part is darker, because it is the reflection of the floor, now very blurred.

This is essential: understanding that lighting, this thing we find so hard to understand, is nothing more nor less than the reflection of the surroundings, very, very blurred. That is going to help us understand a lot of things about colour as well.

So here is the summary, and we can see why: perfect reflections and diffuse reflections cause two ways of seeing objects. Here we know that these reflections are the light sources that are around.

## Texture and specularity

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L06 · 05:03–06:42

What starts to interest us is that, thanks to this knowledge, we can divide what makes up the colour of an object into two elements.

The first element is the textural element (*elemento textural*). Basically it is what is painted on our object. It is as if I had painted a wood texture here with a brush; that is what is literally painted on it. On top of this texture comes how the light behaves: its material.

We can see this quite well here. Regardless of what is painted on the object, whether bricks or even a rug or something like that, what interests us is how the light evolves: from a perfect reflection, as we have already seen, going down, down, down towards something more and more diffuse. In this case it is hair, so the reflection is very broken, and when I say broken I mean blurred. Here too, brick is such an irregular and rough material that the light breaks up a lot.

This property of how the light reacts, which is independent of what is painted on the object, we will call specularity (*especularidad*). Basically it is the object's property for receiving light, and it is what we saw at the start: how flat and perfect its surface is, which determines how the light will bounce off it.

To repeat: one part is what is painted on the object, which defines its colour. We will call it local colour (*color local*), and we will explain it shortly. The other ingredient is how the light reacts according to the material: how smooth or rough it is. These are the main ingredients for creating objects with colour.

## Direct and indirect light

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L06 · 06:42–08:13

There are two types of light.

The first type is direct light (*luz directa*), which is what we were discussing. The light arrives in parallel rays and gives a perfect result. It is as if the light had already been reflected off a smooth surface, so all its rays arrive perfect: not altered, not diffused, not blurred, nothing.

Indirect light (*luz indirecta*), on the other hand, is like light that has already passed through a filter, such as clouds. Clouds create a kind of rough surface, so to speak, that turns the direct light of the sun into indirect light, that is, diffuse light. It is also called diffuse light (*luz difusa*), so you understand it is the same as light that has already bounced off something. This distorts the light, spreads it, and as a consequence gives us much softer lighting.

Here we can see that direct light has an end point and is unidirectional. Past this point the rays can no longer touch the object. This boundary, which we call the terminator (*terminator*), is the point where the light can no longer hit, and this whole part of the object is dark. With indirect lighting things are much softer and less precise: because the light does not come from a single direction but from all directions, this border between light and shadow is much more porous. You can also see it in the shadow on the floor, in how the shadow is rendered: very sharp here and very blurred there.

## Anatomy of a lighting: bounced light

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L06 · 08:13–10:43

Now let's try to compress all this information into a single exercise of building up, or simulating, the lighting of an object.

First, let's talk about light and remember that light bounces, and that is what lets us see things. So the light arrives on the object and bounces towards our eye. Very good, but quite basic for artists like us.

What happens next is that the light that hit the floor also bounces, reaches the sphere and creates a spot of light here. What also happens is that light does not bounce just once; it bounces millions of times, which we cannot even perceive. This light, bouncing everywhere, gradually fills the shadow around the lit parts and creates gradients everywhere. In this case the light bounced towards the sphere, as we saw in the previous image, and now it bounces off the sphere again, a second bounce, towards the floor, so this part will be lit. It also happens on the other side. We had not put any light here, but that is not right: the light that reaches the floor back here also bounces everywhere. Do you see how this result looks more and more realistic, closer and closer to something we could actually see in nature? Because in the end that is how light works, with bounces.

Once more: we have a light source here. This part we are seeing is a very diffuse version of that light source, just as we saw before, remember, that everything was really one big diffuse reflection. What we are seeing is the reflection of this. Here there is a patch of light, so my sphere will reflect a patch of light. Here there is a patch of light, so my object will reflect a patch of light. Here there is more darkness, so my object will reflect darkness. You can see how everything is really reflection, and it is much easier to understand it that way, and much easier to draw characters now. That always happens to me: I am trying to be serious and I draw pigs without meaning to. So that is quite important; thanks to this we will be able to see the evolution (the Spanish captions read "la evolución del álbum" here, which is unclear).

Finally, there are many subtleties, such as the fact that here the light, on arriving here, spreads out to the sides and contaminates the area, creating this small gradient. All of this, in the end, is for understanding how colour works.

## Light and colour: local colour and coloured bounces

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L06 · 10:43–13:51

Colour comes into play when one of the two elements of this scene starts to have colour. We stop thinking in white: one of them will have colour. This is where we use our term local colour again.

Let's imagine that the floor is no longer white, as it was before, but blue. Being blue, what happens, in the same way as we saw with a white floor, is that it will tint this object. If the object is white, what were white bounces of light will now be coloured. We start to get this lovely image where the colour of the floor seems to begin wrapping around the sphere. That is very much what happens in reality.

Now we change the parameters a little. The floor is still blue and the light is still white; we have not changed that parameter yet. But the object, the sphere, has changed: it is now red. What we have to see is that the white light hitting the red object will not change the object's local colour much. It will be dark red in the shadow and light red in the light. However, every time the light bounces somewhere, the place it bounces from becomes a kind of light source, diffuse and indirect. So here we will see that the reflections are now blue, and they turn our red into a kind of purple. We can see it: these colours here are reds that begin to approach fuchsias and purples, and here they get even closer. So here we have the base colour, and here we have the other part, where the red has drifted towards blue because it is tinted by the blue around it.

Now we change the last factor, the only one we have not modified yet: the colour of the light. If, besides changing the local colour of the objects, we change the colour of the light, we are acting on the last parameter, and things get really interesting. The local colour of this sphere is still red, but the light is now yellow. So we only perceive the red here; this is still red, because everything else is already influenced by some kind of bounce. Where the yellow light arrives, this part gets closer and closer to yellow. Here, at its highest point, it is a kind of salmon very close to yellow, compared with how red the original colour was. That is because the local colour moved towards the colour of the light, which is yellow; that is how it works. And again, in all these parts contaminated, so to speak, by another colour, we will see the red being pulled away as well, approaching the light source.

That is how we reach quite realistic results: by understanding that the local colour of objects is almost never intrinsic, almost never perceivable as such. It is almost always affected by some kind of lighting, because perfectly white light is an uncommon condition.

## Graphic light: simplifying lighting by convention

The last thing we need to look at now, because it is important for the future, is that this idea of lighting, of a light source that is reflected on one part which is then considered the lit part, gives us a great opportunity for an abstraction that is very common across the whole visual world as we know it. Instead of going into all the subtleties we have just seen, reflections, bounces and so on, people sometimes decide by convention to simplify this information.

What I put here is its most simplified, most basic version: a light part and a shadow part on the object, and the same thing on the floor with the cast shadow, which is subtracted.

This becomes interesting once we start seeing it this way. The reality of design, and of the differences between design styles, besides the type of shapes and how they are exaggerated and so on, is how light is treated and how much it is simplified.

## Towards realistic light: comics, painting and 3D

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L06 · 15:40–17:46

It starts to evolve a little when we move towards comics, for example, where the style still stylizes this but with a bit more precision. What is done there is an attempt to give the shadows complexity. Hatching (*achurado*) is used to simulate gradients, because from a distance it is perceived as a grey, as something graded, no longer as a single completely hard flat patch.

We still have the local colour, we have the light colour, we have a very subtle gradient between light and shadow, and we have certain specular properties. Here the light is treated as if it were something flat that does not depend on a material. ## Recap: three principles

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L06 · 17:46–19:52

To recap, what matters most in this lesson is understanding these three principles.

The first: what we know as lighting is nothing more than the reflection of what is around an object. The smoother the object, the more perfect that reflection will be.

The second: we can distinguish two aspects of any object in order to represent it in colour. Its texture, that is, what is painted on it, and the type of material, that is, how perfect and reflective it is.

And finally: light bounces everywhere, so the colour of objects is very variable. It depends a great deal on the light it receives and the type of light it receives, and on the environment the object is in, because the surroundings will affect it a lot through these bounces. We understand this by looking at this: obviously it is because there is blue in the sky and green on the floor that the colour of this object ends up being blue and green. And if the object had not been white but had a colour, the final colour of the object would have been that local colour plus the blue in the lit part and plus the green in the shadow part.

I hope all of this is clear. We will come back to this topic little by little throughout the course and wrap it all up so that it is very clear. These are fundamental principles of all our artistic practice. It is the base, the cement we must lay first in order to understand colour in depth and become masters of this hard subject, obviously with a lot of practice. What we are doing here is understanding it intellectually, and we will put it into a lot of practice in the next unit.

Now you see how understanding light is necessary to have control over your colour. In the next lesson we are going to take everything we have seen so far and use it to create a colour concept.

### Key points (derived)

- Smooth surfaces reflect light perfectly (mirror-like, parallel rays); rough surfaces scatter it (diffuse reflection).
- Lighting is a very blurred reflection of the surroundings: the light source, nearby objects, the sky and the floor all remain present in it.
- An object's colour splits into texture (local colour, what is painted on it) and specularity (how smooth and reflective its material is).
- Direct light gives a sharp terminator and cast shadow; indirect (diffuse) light gives a soft, porous transition.
- Light bounces many times; every bounce surface acts as a coloured, diffuse light source, so local colour is almost never seen as such.
- Styles differ largely in how much they simplify light, from flat local colour and shadow to fully realistic 3D rendering.
