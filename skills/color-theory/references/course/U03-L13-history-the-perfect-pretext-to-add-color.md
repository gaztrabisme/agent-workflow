## Asking what the image is about

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 00:29–01:43

Story is very important. Asking what the story of our image is should happen from the very start, but at any point it is good to ask again what the image is about, so that you can add elements.

What do I mean? Right now our image is the story of this object, a flower, in a field full of trees and other flowers. That is basically the whole story. It is not the story of a ninja warrior who is in this field to fight evil; so far it is a simple image. Around it we have even more peripheral elements, such as the stones and the little flowers: everything we added while creating the image to give context and a bit of detail, and all the pretexts we looked for to build a richer color palette.

What is missing now is a little more definition. I would like to know more about this flower, so that the image becomes more memorable. In the end, that is often what we are after: that people like what they see and can also remember it. If we zoom in on the flower, we will try to find the elements, the ingredients, it should have to tell us a story, so that it becomes something or someone more than just a flower.

## A nest and an egg: the payoff

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 01:43–03:03

What occurred to me was to imagine that inside this flower there is a nest. That opens a door to a story the people who see the image can imagine: whose nest is it, and why is it inside a flower? Inside, or on top of, the nest I put an egg, which raises even more questions. These flowers are now like little cradles (captions: *cuñitas*, "little wedges"; said: *cunitas*).

See how just adding a couple of details changes things. Right now the image is relatively bland, a bit flat. We have built a whole range of colors so that my attention arrives here, at the flower. But it arrives and says, "meh. All that fuss for this?" It is interesting, it is pretty, but it tells me nothing.

What we can do is add this story element. It is what cinema calls a payoff: everything has been preparing me, and now it really gives me something. Now it is justified that my whole composition works, or conspires, to bring me here, because here there really is something to see. It does not take much. A couple of well-thought-out details is enough to spark the viewer's imagination. In the end we are all more interested in the story of an egg inside this flower than in just looking at the same flower.

## How the nest serves the palette

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 03:03–04:50

We can follow this logic to keep adding small details. But first let us see how and why this part was integrated, because it is not only about adding details: they must again serve to build an interesting color palette (captions: *pared de color*, "color wall").

Over this pink I put a darker purple. That has a double advantage. First, remember that I am trying to build this palette from purples on one side and yellows on the other, and all the pink tones were bridges between them. By adding these pinks and purples here I have created that bridge, and I have brought a little of the blue that is in the sky, and in the foreground, into these pinks.

Within one area I have created a lot of variation in these tones. You can see it here: I go from pink to purple (captions: *moral*), which are relatively close, then back to pink, then back to purple. In between I placed this small detail that we added when we put in textural details; it is also a small bridge that adds variation. We go from these reds to these yellows.

So in this area, besides variation, I am creating color contrast, and you will see later that we push this contrast even further. It is the area of the image with the most color contrast, because it goes from a blue to a pink to a yellow: I have three extremes concentrated here, because this is where I want the attention to go. And I repeat the pattern here, where I have purples, blues and this light tone that is really still a yellow; even the straw is yellow. I am forcing and repeating these contrasts so that this area draws a lot of attention.

## Checking the value contrast

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 04:50–05:34

Another thing I am creating, and it is very important to always check it: what I just added is also a strong value contrast. If we look at the image in black and white, before there was only a relatively weak contrast between these tones; by weak I mean there was not much difference between the two values. Now we have a very strong contrast here, at the physical and visual center of the image, which justifies it drawing my attention. There is in fact a double contrast: from light to dark, and again from dark to light. That is very important. The colors I chose are not random and not gratuitous; they also support the construction of my image in black and white, its structure.

## Butterflies: movement, light and variation

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 05:34–06:51

Now let us see how to follow this logic and keep adding details of interest that strengthen the palette. Until now we only had secondary details: trees, a field and other flowers. I think we can add one more element: a cloud of butterflies, to create movement and to give the idea that there is life around this egg. We can also add, as a very peripheral element, a main butterfly that will sit in the foreground.

So we had the image like this, and what we are adding is this: a small cloud of butterflies that creates a nice flow, supporting the flow we already had, and that also adds light, this sense of brightness. In the foreground we have a variation of this yellow. I did not want much contrast there, because I already have a lot of contrast between my yellows and my blues, so I added some orange, to go from yellow to orange. It is a detail that gives variation to an area that did not have much before. Before, it looked like this: there was a kind of empty space here. It was not necessarily a problem, but it could be used to add something else. Now we have this element.

## Example: GopGap's butterfly hunters

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 06:51–07:48

Let us see how this works in other images. Here is an illustration by GopGap, an illustrator whose use of color to build stories I find very interesting. The composition is globally yellowish and orange, with this light spot at the center. But it would tell more, and would even be more interesting in color, if we had this detail he had put in, which I removed to show how much it was needed.

With it we have this light element that helps apply our yin and yang principle: with these elements here, the light values carry over into the upper part of the image. It also gives it a great deal of dimension: now we know what these people are chasing. Before, we could think he is saying, "Oh, look, there's a monster over there," or "Oh, look, there's my house, let's go have tea." But in fact we know they are looking for butterflies, so he is probably saying, "Look over there, I saw one!" That is why the boy looks happy. It is very important to tell a story with these elements and, again, to push our color.

## Example: the shop, lines of orange and blue

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 09:09–11:54

In images with this many elements it is hard to keep control of all of them, but this person does it very well and uses every detail to create quality in the color composition and to tell us stories. It is not by chance that the artist added these boxes. They tell the story of a shop that keeps its crates outside, and that this boy uses to sit on. All the elements here, these vases or whatever they are, also tell the story: although it is a shop, it is normal for it to display what it sells outside.

What I find interesting is that it helps the image create a lot of color variation. Behind this character all the details are more desaturated, which lets the character read as a saturated patch, so it stands out very well. And this orange here appears again over there in another form, but it is the same orange. So we have this big curve of oranges that helps us travel through the image. We find these oranges again here, in a slightly redder and much darker form, and again here, also darker. We have two big lines of these orange tones, even including this tyre, which serve to guide the eye and create an interesting color composition.

You will see the same happens with the blues. We have the blues here, and the artist uses different details, like the bikes here, to create variation in the blue. Look: it is a blue, but not the same one; it is not as purple, it is more greenish. And this blue carries us up to this window, which is exactly the same blue, and to this part of the roof, which is the same blue but desaturated. On this side there is also a flow within the blues, with this T-shirt and this object, until we reach the blue of the sky.

So the colors, the elements, everything we put in the image, is always placed on purpose, to create interest and variation in color, composition and movement, and above all to give this sense of harmony, of these colors spreading around the focal point of the image and its story. Here we have this orange spot; here this big spot between pink and orange, which also connects with this roof. Knowing that the image is globally gray, very desaturated overall, all these spots help us move around; even these flowers are here to create more movement, and these parts too. All these lines I am drawing, the artist really has them in mind, and is making us move around to generate interest.

## Example: the child's room, designing with color

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 11:54–13:17

The same happens with this kind of image. With an image like this, it can be a headache: we start wondering what on earth to put in this spot. Or, the other way round, we can start thinking: I have a big blue wall, very good. What I need on top of this blue is to create contrast and to spread the other colors I have here, which are these ochres. If you look, the whole image is made of many ochre, orange and green tones, close to the yellows. On the other side it is the same, just a little redder. All the time it is these three colors mixing, with the greens as a bridge between all of them, because green is basically the mix of blue and yellow.

So when creating all these objects we can ask ourselves: what if I put a dinosaur here? Of course, because it is green, so I can integrate it well. What if I put a plant? Of course, it is green too. What if I put this case? And so on. That is where it gets really interesting: we start thinking about design at the same time as we think about color. We are literally designing the color, because we have the rules we have discussed very clearly in mind. This serves not only to add story (here they are children's toys, which makes us understand very quickly that it is a child's room) but also to improve and push the color composition.

## Final adjustments to the flower image

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 13:17–16:28

Let us see how we can push our image a little further. It works now, but it could have more interest, more harmony and more coherence if we take it in this direction. I made a couple of small adjustments to give it a bit more warmth and visual interest, and we will break them down together. I made a folder with all these small modifications, and I want to explain them one by one, because each has a reason.

The first thing I did was raise the saturation, because I feel this image could be more vivid. Right now it is quite dull, relatively gray when seen from a distance; it has room to go up. But I am raising the saturation everywhere, so next I take some saturation back out of the areas where it should not be. Remember that what we want in the image is variation; we do not want the whole image to have even saturation. So I remove some saturation from the shadows, from the foreground and even from the background. That takes us from this to this: a more saturated version, but not fully saturated. After that I darken the image a little, because it looks slightly washed out. And, I do not know if you can see it, these yellows need to come down a bit in saturation; I pushed them too far. Even with these adjustments, we started there and now we are here, and it is still much more saturated, but I have more control over the saturation level. There is nothing worse than an image that feels very saturated; it feels visually aggressive.

Another thing: I feel these two tones are merging too much because of the light I added here. There is not the separation they should have; in black and white one almost bleeds into the other. That is not what I want in my main object; I want distance and contrast. So I made all this pink more pink and less orange. This tone was closer to the oranges than this one, which is closer to fuchsia (captions: *fusion*). I moved it away from the yellow so there is a very clear, very visible line between the two, and it was also darkened a little so there is more difference.

I also pushed this line a little so it moves away from this detail; they were too close and produced something that did not read well, which looks a bit confusing from a distance. We want clarity here. Then I added a few details in this shadow to give the shape a bit more interest. Finally I adjusted the saturation a little, because I still felt the foreground drew too much attention; it is very contrasted. We can lower it so it feels different from what happens here. And now we have an image that is a little more advanced, a little more complete than the previous one.

## A Levels layer to tint the whole image

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 16:28–17:34

Mainly, what I did is something I will show you very quickly. I took a Levels (*Niveles*) adjustment layer. What interests us about Levels is that we can modify the color, not only the value. If I go from RGB to Red, for example, and I want to give my image a slight red cast, I can raise this value a little, and that creates a slight red veil over the image.

That is partly what I did: I tried to harmonize the image a bit more by having one color predominate a little more. Here it felt a bit greenish and a bit desaturated, so I wanted to move all the tones a little toward reddish tones and purer yellows, so that the image would feel more vivid. Remember that the more color, the more vividness, and the more sense of happiness and life we get. That is what was done to this image to bring it to its final result, adding the last details.

## Closing

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L13 · 17:34–17:58

So remember: if you feel your image is not very memorable, just ask yourself how you can add more quality and more story to it, and enrich it that way. In the next unit we will finally talk about color theory.

### Key points (derived)

- Ask what the image's story is, at the start and again near the end; a couple of story details (a nest, an egg) give the focal point a payoff.
- Every added story element should also do color work: bridge the palette, repeat a color elsewhere, or add contrast where attention should go.
- Check new elements in black and white: the focal point should carry the strongest value contrast.
- In reference images (GopGap, the ice cream truck, Ross Tran, the shop, the child's room), added objects spread a color around the image and create lines that guide the eye.
- Final pass: raise saturation globally, remove it where it is not wanted, separate merging tones, and use a Levels layer on one channel to tint and unify the image.
