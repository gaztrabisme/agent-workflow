## What a color concept is

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 00:00–00:50

In this lesson we review some fundamental concepts of image composition, and I show you how I convert my images from black and white to color until I reach the final finish. We will go through it together.

The image we work on is not extremely developed or detailed, but it is a color concept (*concepto a color*). A color concept is one of the things most often requested when making animated films, or even in illustration. Its purpose is to make sure that the image's approach to color, the mood it has and the feeling it conveys are right. Only after that can we go into producing the details.

## The eye sees values before color

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 00:50–01:50

Before anything else, to start making a color image you have to understand that the first thing our eye sees is not color. It does not actually see the image that way. The first thing it sees is an organization of different values (*valores*); we have already talked about values. There will be light parts and dark parts, and that is all it will see. There is no color without this first structure in values. I will propose a definition of it shortly.

These different values are made up of two groups, so to speak, which we have already mentioned a little: the local colors (*colores locales*), and the local colors plus the light. Together these make up our layout (*diagramación*).

Ask yourself right now: what type of image does this illustration belong to? Do you remember the categories: gloomy, happy, mysterious, and so on? Keep this in mind, because we will come back to it. In theory, the idea should already be relatively clear at this point, so that the color can later follow it.

## A definition of composition

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 01:50–02:46

This leads us to a first attempt at defining what an image and a composition are. My definition is that a composition is the organization of the values created by light and by color. Remember this well. Right now we are only looking at values created by the light and the local color, without seeing the color itself, but color will be added.

This is the most fundamental point. If it seems a little ambiguous or too ambitious right now, don't worry. We will make this whole journey step by step across the next unit. Follow me and trust me: I am leading you by the hand toward a deep understanding of all this. For now, though, we have to talk about composition, because there is no color without composition. I will go over a few basic principles of composition so that we are sure we share the same background and are talking about the same thing.

## Focal points and the hierarchy of attention

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 02:46–03:53

In every composition we have to create focal points (*puntos focales*). You will see again that color is a great tool for reinforcing them, and even for creating them, but they have to exist beforehand, already in the layout of values. There does not have to be only one focal point. Here we have a main focal point, a secondary focal point and another secondary focal point.

I find this definition interesting: focal points exist to rank information into a hierarchy. When you read a newspaper, or an article on any online news site, you get the headline in large type and then different font sizes that tell you what is important to read and what may be secondary. Exactly the same happens in an image. We create a hierarchy that says: this is what matters most, then you can look at that, then at that, and everything else you honestly don't need to bother with, because it is purely decorative.

This hierarchy is created by contrasts (*contrastes*). Remember this term well; it will be central to this course. Every time we talk about color, we will necessarily talk about contrast.

## The three contrasts: value

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 03:53–04:21

There are three contrasts we are going to create. These three apply to black and white only; once we are working in color we will add more contrasts. But these are again the structure, the skeleton of your image.

The first of the three is value contrast (*contraste de valor*), which is the most obvious. Every time there is a very light value on a very dark value, or a very dark value against a very light value, it will catch our attention, and that is where a focal point will be created.

## Detail contrast

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 04:21–04:54

Another contrast is detail contrast (*contraste de detalle*). Look at how in this image there is a lot of detail, a lot going on around the edges of the image, and here, directly around my focal point, there is nothing any more. If you create an emptier zone and a detailed point in the center, the eye will tend to go there.

Look at what was created: a kind of target, a natural bullseye, with detail on the periphery, detail at the center, and in between a zone without detail that draws more attention to the center.

## Silhouette contrast and the Dust & Scratches check

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 04:54–05:48

The last contrast is silhouette contrast (*contraste de silueta*). It is an idea we can show, and it is clearly visible here in color: the strongest silhouettes, again where the contrast is greatest, are the ones that draw attention. If you look at this simplified version, you can see there are only a few very hard lines, such as this line, this one and this one, and they are mainly found and concentrated at the focal point.

I'll show you how this magic is done. Go to Filter > Noise > Dust & Scratches (*Filtro > Ruido > Polvo y rascaduras*). Photoshop takes the existing contrasts and turns some of these lines into blurry areas where the contrast is not very strong. It is a great tool for checking whether your image has definition where it needs to have it.

## From black and white to color on a single object

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 05:48–07:32

Now let's see how this works on a single object. This is how we go from black and white to color. I'll also show you how it is done in Photoshop, or at least how I do it.

First we take our object in black and white overall, to make sure the levels are correct. Here I know I have this dark level against this light level. Then I convert all these tones directly to color, trying to keep that: my darks are still dark and my lights are still light.

Next I add what is decorative: what we called local color, or the textures on these objects, in the previous lesson.

Then I add the light. Remember I said that light could be stylized? Here I am representing the light in a relatively graphic way, not in an extremely realistic one. I put in my lit part here. This is my terminator (*terminador*), the zone of separation between the lit area and the shadow. Here I also use something we saw in the previous lesson, my specular (*especular*), which means the surface here is fairly smooth.

On the other side I create another light source, as if it were a large bounce of the lights around it: knowing I have a light source here, it is very likely that, bouncing everywhere, it ends up contaminating this area. We can also see that the main light hitting the ground bounces and lights the underside of my plant, and even its sides. Although it is a little stylized, it is consistent with what we have already seen. Finally, the main thing added is this light, as if there were a light inside the flower itself, to create some interest.

## My Photoshop method for adding color without losing values

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 07:32–09:31

Let me go back a little to show you how I go from the black-and-white version to the color version. People often ask me how I do it, whether there are tricks or anything like that. The only thing I care about is not losing my value structure.

So the first thing I create is a black-and-white adjustment layer (*capa de ajustes de blanco y negro*), so that I can check anything I do in color right away. Then, depending on how my file is organized, I make a selection. Ideally that selection is already made, or all the parts are on separate layers.

What I do next is very personal; it is just that I feel it gives me the most control. I create a Hue/Saturation adjustment (*tono/saturación*), either this way or directly with Ctrl+U, and the dialog appears. Here I tell Photoshop: preview, of course, and colorize whatever I give you. This lets me create my color live. In this case the aim was a relatively fuchsia color. Then I turn on my black-and-white adjustment layer again to check that what I have done has not affected the value.

Then I keep adding more colors. I wanted this one to be dark and more toward blue. The advantage is that I am not painting the color in here directly. I apply it afterwards with Edit > Fill (*Edición > Rellenar*) using the foreground color, and then I can say "no, I don't like it" and change it. What I really like is that it can be altered immediately, because color is something subtle, and sometimes I just want something a little greener or similar.

That is how I create color, keeping my eye on the black-and-white version to make sure I am keeping my original value structure.

## Applying the process to the whole image

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L07 · 09:31–11:15

Now let's see how this carries over to the whole image. We had seen the black-and-white structure; now we see the first phase of color. Again, it is pure local color, except on the ground here, where I am already starting to put in the color of the light, but in principle it is mainly local color. You can see it already has these little circles and so on.

Next I add the lighting proper: where the light hits here, where it hits the rocks, where it hits toward the back or how it reacts. And how this light is now hitting the left side of this flower.

After that I add all these textural elements, which are adding a lot of color. Even though I wanted a relatively dark image, I also want it to be compensated by color. This is a color class; it would be a shame to make a gray image. So I am adding several details that complete my color palette and add more color, again trying not to betray my original structure.

At this point, though, the image is starting to flatten a little; everything is becoming somewhat gray. So I add more details and more contrasts, to make the image more obvious. Do you see the difference in black and white? Now look at the difference in color. Here all the colors are already in; it doesn't change much from the next image, but it is much more contrasted and lets this part stand out.

I am not going into the details of why each color was chosen, nor into all the technical details; that is exactly the point of this course. For now, all I wanted was to show you the process: how it works and how to get something like this.

### Key points (derived)

- The eye reads an organization of light and dark values before it reads color; build the value structure first.
- Composition is the organization of the values created by light and color.
- Focal points rank information into a hierarchy, and that hierarchy is created by contrast: value, detail and silhouette.
- Filter > Noise > Dust & Scratches blurs low-contrast edges, which shows whether definition sits where it should.
- Object workflow: values, local colors, patterns, light; whole-image workflow: local colors, light, secondary colors, final adjustments of values and colors.
- Keep a Black & White adjustment layer on top and colorize selections with Hue/Saturation or Edit > Fill, checking value after every color change.
