## The four layers of an image

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L08 · 00:31–01:51

To create color as such, there are several things we need to know that matter a great deal by the time we reach the color stage. I consider that creating an image or any visual, from a final illustration to a character, or even a graphic design in a magazine or anything else, is all graphic art, and all of it is governed by the same rules.

In my view, any design element is conceived and built through these different tools. First, the idea (*idea*): what idea do we have? Second, the design (*diseño*): what is the shape of what we are going to draw? Then the values (*valores*), which we saw in the second unit: what is the contrast relationship between the different elements? And finally, which colors (*colores*).

Color is the topic that matters most to us in this course. However, there are several things we have to understand in the earlier layers to be sure that whatever we learn here about color is well grounded. I would like to tell you that I can talk to you only about color and that even if you have everything else badly done your color will look incredible; unfortunately, no. That is why I place color as the last layer. If everything else works, this layer adds a new dimension to whatever we have made.

## Where the failed images went wrong: values

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L08 · 03:34–05:03

We start to fail when we get to the values. The first thing to know is that when we remove the colors we are left with the structure, the skeleton of the image. We already discussed this a little when we talked about contrasts and focal points.

In the case of this image, what fails? We have the main contrast where it should be, at the focal point (*punto focal*). The problem is that many lines are guiding my attention all over the place. All of this is leading me towards this point, and that goes against the plan of my image, because I wanted this to be my focal point. This image fails for that reason.

Here the problem is that there are many contrasts in peripheral areas, and by contrast I again mean a dark part against a light part, that are not where they should be. And in this image, for example, nothing is identifiable. There are no contrasts to define the interesting elements. Seen from a distance, this silhouette cannot be read, so there is no protagonist as such, no main actor in this image.

In this image, by contrast, we have everything that should work in an image like this. The greatest contrast is in the focal point area, where this very dark tone sits against this very light tone. All the flows (*flujos*), as we have discussed, lead directly where we want them to. And finally the contrast of detail is applied well, as we have also seen: a lot of detail here, a lot of detail there, and a silhouette with no problems.

## From values to colors: keeping the same plan

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L08 · 06:31–09:58

We are not going to stay here long. What we want is definitely color. But it was important to recall this, and above all to understand the path of any color composition. As I said, we set out the basic structure of our image (in the following lessons we will analyze in great detail how a basic structure is made). Then we check whether it works as it should: we have contrasts that take us where we want, balance, harmony, things we will see later. Once the structure works very well, it is time to move to color.

What matters most is that you remember very well what my intention was and what the rules of my composition will be. I had decided I wanted that to be the protagonist, and I had decided I wanted contrasts here and here. So I will organize everything so that the kinds of colors I use follow the same logic.

One of the most frequent and serious errors when creating an image or any design is having a value plan that does not follow the same rules as the color plan. For now we will again look at this in a rather open, general way; trust me, I will give you the keys little by little. What matters here is to see that, just as we placed a light value on a dark background to generate contrast, we are going to do the same with color. We take a purple, in this case, and put it against a green. These two colors, as we will explain more and more, are very different. You probably heard the term complementary (*complementarios*) at school, because they are colors that are very different from each other and therefore generate contrast. Again, don't worry, we will explain all of that. For now you need to understand this idea: the plan I have here in black and white, I follow in color. If there was contrast in black and white, there must be contrast in color. That is what will allow us to have compositions that feel coherent, where each of these elements supports the others to reach a good image.

That is why it is so important to consider that the design of anything is an accumulation of several things, and all of them must have the same intention to arrive with power. We see it here. The composition attempted with the values, these curves of color, this rhythm, is followed here. It is no accident that I did not choose different colors for all the flowers. Here they are all purple, and no other part of the image is purple right now, because I want it to be very clear that this is a place where these flowers stand out and create a little path of flowers: a rhythm, a flow of purple over a background of another color. What happens outside this does not matter much (we will explain that later too). What matters now is to understand that my color decision is completely deliberate. It is made so that color comes to support the composition I had created in black and white, and that is why the image works in the end. I have created many things around these elements, these flowers, but it is very clear that my eye can travel between these different domes of flowers, and it will find purple elements here that somehow all feel part of the same family.

## Less color is more

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L08 · 09:58–11:48

Now, at last, we will start making color. We are going to learn to create this image, but what I want you to understand is that we are learning to understand color so well that we will need less and less of it. Let me explain. They say the human eye can perceive and distinguish up to 4 million colors. Four million. That means every time we work with color we have to make decisions across this range: 4 million options for every decision. However, we will see that if we understand the structure well, and everything we are seeing in this course, we can reduce the number of decisions we have to make, understand which few fundamental colors matter, and then add complexity and many more colors without ever betraying the original plan.

In the second unit of this course we talked about this image. With this image there was no limit on the number of colors: there are gradients everywhere, there are textures. It is a very complex image in itself. In this unit we are working on this image, which is already much more simplified; things are flatter. I sum it up here by saying we have gone from 4 million colors to 100 colors. This is the one we will follow step by step throughout this unit. Finally we will reach a much more synthetic version, and we will see that it still works as an image, because we will have understood all the basic, fundamental concepts behind creating this kind of color palette, so that it works as an image, and not only as an illustration but as branding, as character design, as whatever you want to make, because these principles are totally universal.

## The three ingredients of contrast: shape, value and color

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L08 · 12:59–14:13

Throughout the evolution of our image I want to explain that, in the end, the only thing we are looking for, as I have been explaining since the start of this course, is contrast: having control over a composition, and having control over flows and focal points. What I really want you to see is that color, the main and most important ingredient of this course, is one of three ingredients for creating contrast. The others are shapes and values. These are the three elements at our disposal for creating contrast. Earlier, on other slides, I have called this concept of shape "design", because it is the same thing.

How can we create contrast and build images with these? With shape, the elements at our disposal are sizes, for example: I use large sizes and small sizes so that they create contrast and generate differences in the image. With values, as we have seen, I literally have values from white to black to create contrast. And with color, I also have different colors to create contrast.

## Distance on the scale creates contrast

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L08 · 14:13–15:39

Seen another way, the scale available to me for values is clear: I go from black to white. For shapes, I go from small to large; and if I want to change shapes, which is another way of making contrast, I go from circle to square, and not much more. For color I have the whole visible spectrum, the whole rainbow, and we will look at it in great detail later.

The further apart two colors are, the more contrast, just as in black and white: the further apart two values are, the more contrast. If I use black and white, the contrast is at its maximum, and if I reduce the distance, the contrast is minimal. We will see that color works the same way. If we want to create color contrast, we move our colors apart, and if we want very related colors with very little contrast, we bring them together. That is why we see a lot of contrast here, almost the equivalent of white against black: here we have a purple and a yellow, and on the color wheel (*círculo cromático*) purple and yellow are very far apart. So we are creating contrast.

This is one of the things that will matter most in our course: understanding that creating color is generating contrast, and that color is one of the elements at our disposal to generate that contrast.

You have seen it: we all have bad attempts. It doesn't matter; all that matters is analyzing them in order to improve. Remember that a good image works when it has a good composition of values, and only then can color come in to improve it. In the next lesson we will talk about harmony.

### Key points (derived)

- An image is built in four layers: idea, design (shape), values, colors. Color comes last and only improves an image whose lower layers already work.
- The failed sketches shared idea and design with the successful one; they failed at values: incoherent flows, contrasts away from the focal point, unreadable silhouettes.
- Check values by removing color, then removing further detail in black and white.
- The color plan must follow the value plan: where there is value contrast, there must be color contrast (e.g. purple against green, complementary colors).
- Giving all the flowers one color, used nowhere else, creates a rhythm and a flow the eye can follow.
- Understanding structure reduces color decisions: 4 million colors, then 100, then about 10, and the image still works.
- Shape, value and color are the three means of contrast; on each scale, the greater the distance, the greater the contrast.
