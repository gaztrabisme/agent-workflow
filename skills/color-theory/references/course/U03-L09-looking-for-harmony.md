## My definition of harmony: interaction with clarity

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L09 · 01:17–01:59

To explain what harmony is and how to harmonize colors, I will explain how I understand it, starting very generally and moving to the particular.

For me, harmony is when there is interaction with clarity (*interacción con claridad*). That is, it is clear who is who: which is white and which is blue. But there is interaction: these shapes are playing with each other, and above all, inside the large blue shape, where it is clear that blue dominates, there is a little white, and vice versa, a little blue inside the white. That is the definition of yin and yang, and you will see that visually it makes a lot of sense.

## Two people who interact

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L09 · 03:02–03:57

This may seem a little strange right now, but we will see that it matters. Now that we know how important a solid structure under our color is, I think you understand why I insist so much on clarity.

It is like two people. Each one is a different world: one likes books, the other likes films, and so on, it does not matter. When they decide to interact, they do not stop being one and the other; they start to have a relationship. It is very corny, but it is what should happen with our colors. Colors should always exist in identifiable fields, which is why we have a structure, and it has to be clear and create contrasts. But they should also merge in some way, try to give each other a little of their colors (captions: *darse vellitud de colores*), so that they can interact.

## Maximum contrast with color

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L09 · 03:57–05:00

To create these two blocks of the yin and the yang, we have to create a contrast. If they were values, it would be a dark value against a light value. If we remember, to create the maximum contrast between two values, we only have to choose the ones farthest apart.

It is the same with color, but what is great about color is that any color we take that is fully opposite another will be the equivalent of this black and white. So every time I have one color fully opposite another, I have the maximum contrast, the equivalent of black and white. If I take a green and a purple, I already have my strongest contrast. If I take a cyan and a red, I already have my strongest contrast.

![1140 · U3 · L09 · 04:05](frames/U03L09-F0405.jpg)

That is the great strength of color, because we can create many contrasts. With black and white there is only black and white, and everything in between is already less contrasted. As we move our colors around the wheel, we can keep a maximum contrast.

## Building bridges with a third color

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L09 · 05:00–06:14

However, as we have seen, creating contrast is not enough to create harmony. We also have to create these bridges (*puentes*), these in-between places that let two complementary colors meet in some way.

How do we create these bridges? Basically it is easy: we take any color that is less distant than the other. This [the opposite pair] is the maximum space. But if I go from this one to this one, the space is smaller, and if I go from this one to this one, the space is smaller too. So each time we take a third color that is far enough from the two contrasting colors we chose, this color will act as a bridge.

![1140 · U3 · L09 · 05:15](frames/U03L09-F0515.jpg)

On screen: "CREAR PUENTES CON COLOR" [Creating bridges with color]  
Shows: A color wheel with blue, cyan and yellow points marked; below, blue, cyan and yellow swatches over black, light grey and white swatches.

We can see it here: we have these two colors very, very far apart, and this color sits in between. That is one way to unify colors: adding a third color that will necessarily be less contrasting than the other two. Because they are already at the extremes, this one will necessarily be on the path between them. It is what would happen with the same example in black and white: it would be the equivalent of a middle grey. This helps us create these bridges.

## Building bridges by lowering saturation

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L09 · 06:14–07:00

That is one way: choosing another color. But there is another way, which is to lower the saturation (*saturación*). The great advantage of colors is that they are distributed on a circle, and so far we have stayed on the edge of this circle. What is the edge? The colors at their full intensity. We talked about saturation at some point: these are saturated colors.

To create a bridge between two colors we can simply lower this saturation, because the great advantage is that the lower the saturation, the relatively closer the colors are. For example here: if I drew this inner circle and took a blue and a yellow again, being more desaturated they are already closer. This distance is shorter than this distance.

## Bridges in the composition: shape, value and color

Anchor: 1140 · U3 · L09 · 07:00–08:37

Up to now we had seen this: shape, value contrast and color contrast. We now reach the next level: creating bridges.

To create bridges with shapes, we add intermediate shapes: we had large shapes, and now we have large, small and medium shapes. To do it with values: before we had nothing but black and white, and now we have black, white and a grey. So we are reducing our contrasts to create harmony.

How do we translate that into color (captions: *al colón*)? Now that we have this in black and white, the color equivalent without our third color would be this: I have my purples on one side, and all I did to simulate my greys was take a slightly lighter purple, and I still have my yellow. What we have right now is still an extreme contrast; we do not have harmony yet.

If we add our third color, we start to have harmony: a yellow and a purple that, to have a relationship, call in a third member to be the bridge between them. And look how adding this color straight away gives dimension, depth and interest to our image. The other one is boring, very, very literal: two colors that do not want contact and are only there because they had no other choice.
