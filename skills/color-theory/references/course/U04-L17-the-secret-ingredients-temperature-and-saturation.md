## The color wheel split into warm and cold

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L17 · 01:30–02:37

What matters to us about color when we talk about temperature is, above all, understanding that the color wheel (*círculo cromático*) we have seen and analysed quite a lot by now could in fact be divided into two super-abstract groups. They are abstract because they have no physical basis; this is purely a human projection: claiming that all the colors in the upper part are warm, that they evoke heat (captions: *Kalos*), and that all the colors below somehow evoke cold.

It is not biological or morphological. It is not because our retina is built a certain way that we can perceive this cold or warm sensation, as is the case with color itself. A color such as yellow is a physical matter: it has a reason for being, related to the light spectrum and a certain range within that spectrum, and to how it vibrates and how it produces heat in our eye, something very technical. Temperature, cold and warm, is entirely a human invention. However, we do not much care where it comes from or why it is so. What matters to us is knowing that it does exist, and that it gives us an immense tool: one of the most general contrasts.

## Warm against cold: a super contrast

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L17 · 02:37–04:32

We have seen many types of contrast: of value, of color, color compositions such as complementary, and so on. In terms of color, the contrast between cold and warm is a super tool, because it allows a kind of super contrast. In the end it will not matter so much that my image contrasts a blue against a red; what will often matter is that a whole family of cold colors forms and contrasts with a whole family of warm colors. After that we apply the same principles of harmony, and so on.

In this image it is very clear. It is globally cold, made of many tones that belong to the lower part of the color wheel: blues, cyans. In fact it is easier when it is a vertical bar: all the colors from the center downwards are warm, and from the center upwards are cold. If we look at our background, most of the tones belong to the upper part. And then, obviously, the big color that contrasts belongs to the reds. Remember that what is at the very top is actually attached to what is at the very bottom, so we are right on the border between cold and warm.

That is one of the things I wanted to show you. Here the contrast between colds and warms, between many greens and reds, is very evident. However, so that there is some harmony (because we never stop talking about harmony), the artist was careful that the reds do not go towards this kind of red: the carmines (captions: *carminos*) that lean more towards orange. The artist actually stayed on the side of these reds, which are subtly a little colder, so the contrast is not as strong as it could be. The orange version would perhaps have been too obvious as cold against warm, but this still works. We will explain that in a few seconds; it has to do with the relative nature of temperature.

## Warm, cold, warm: temperature for depth

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L17 · 05:51–07:09

In this image, for example, which we are going to break down this way, we have a whole foreground that is very warm. The difference in temperature is often used to create contrasts and to create depth. The whole foreground is warm, then we have a cold middle ground, and then in the background we have warm colors again. It is as if we had a black in the foreground, a grey in the middle ground and a light grey in the background: it lets us create this depth, this distance.

It does not matter that there are greens, pinks and so on here; the only thing that matters is that they are relatively colder than these. Again, all these colors in this part belong to the cold side; all these colors belong to the lower part: warm colors, oranges, reds, warm greens. And when we reach the background, after passing this whole barrier, this whole cold group, we return to warm colors.

Warm, cold, warm: a great way to compose an image and give it richness of color. And look how it makes life easier. Instead of having to think "which color is complementary to which", no. We can simply think in temperature groups, and inside each of these groups we will see that the important color…

## Classic temperature compositions

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L17 · 07:09–08:00

What can we do with temperature exactly? It is very interesting, because the most classic compositions are often these: a very warm image with a very cold focal point. That is what we saw with the mouth of the monster with many teeth. There is also the opposite option: very cold images with a warm protagonist. Or we can do as in the previous image, this back and forth between colds and warms, as long as it helps us create contrasts.

For example, here inside this group, this mountain, I am creating a group of warm colors, even though this green comes close to the colds; it is still warm. And here, if I want contrast, this one can cross the barrier and go towards the colds. This idea of warms and colds is really wonderful, because it lets us organise the image very efficiently.

## The eye looks for temperature: the old optical trick

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L17 · 08:00–09:13

However, what I find even more interesting is that this idea of temperature is not as strict as what I told you, that the whole upper part is cold and the whole lower part is warm. What actually happens is that the eye, as always, will look for temperature even when there is none.

Let's do this test, which you have probably done a thousand times, where we look at this small grey square. Somehow it seems to be not only grey but a little pinkish. If we look at this one for a while, it seems to be a kind of blue-violet that is not grey. What is happening? It is a phenomenon called retinal persistence (*persistencia retiniana*), a principle that is also used to create the illusion of movement in animation. Our eye is filling up with one color and demanding its counterpart. As always in life, our eye asks us for balance. If we give it an excess of one thing, in this case a cold color, it will invent a warm color here.

This is very interesting and opens the door to a slightly subtler understanding. It is not a revolution; it is simply very practical to understand it this way in order to know how to apply this idea of temperature.

## Saturation

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L17 · 10:19–12:11

The other very important topic of this lesson is saturation. Saturation, as we have seen, is how pure, how intense a color is, and the opposite of something saturated is something desaturated, which is basically a grey.

As with temperature, several kinds of composition are made with saturation; we will see examples afterwards. Here we can see an image with many saturated colors and a desaturated protagonist, which creates enough contrast to attract attention. Then the opposite: a context very low in saturation and a very saturated protagonist.

And here something very interesting, related to what we said about the relativity of temperature. We use a fairly saturated color and put next to it a relatively opposite color that is desaturated. Optically, this color will look different from what it is. This color you see here is this one; it is the same. If we see it above, it looks like a kind of greenish grey. But if we see it here, it looks like a kind of desaturated golden ochre.

That is very practical, because in this image in particular I took advantage of this phenomenon to create a color here that is not an ochre or a yellow like these ones. Let's see the difference: these yellows really are orangey and ochres, but this color is a green, literally a green. However, if we look at the image, these two colors seem to belong more or less to the same family. So by using this color we can very subtly create the principle of harmony we have seen: repeating my dominant or repeated color a little in certain places in order to harmonise it. It is a very, very important principle, and the truth is that beyond that it is pure practice and observation, because it is a very subtle matter, very much about sensation.

## Saturation gradient: desaturating towards the antagonist

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L17 · 12:11–13:10

So here we are to analyse several images and see how saturation and temperature behave. In this case, saturation. This type of image is classic: there is a lot of saturation at the periphery, where we want to show the protagonist, the pretty character, and as we approach the evil object, the object of fear, the antagonist in this case, the colors desaturate.

But something really cool happens here again. All these colors are very warm; they belong to the lower part. And these colors here also belong to the lower part: they are actually relatively warm greens. But because they are desaturated, our eye perceives them as the opposite. It is the same thing we saw with the squares a moment ago: if I put a big orange square, any grey I put here will look colder than it is.

One of the main lessons of this lesson, if you will excuse the redundancy: put greys and desaturated colors in a fairly saturated context and you will create the illusion of temperature.

### Key points (derived)

- Warm and cold are a human convention, not a physical property, but they give one of the most general contrasts: a whole family of colds against a whole family of warms.
- Keep harmony inside a warm-cold contrast by choosing the member of each family that sits closer to the border (for example cooler reds against greens).
- Classic temperature compositions: warm image with a cold focal point, cold image with a warm protagonist, or alternating warm, cold, warm planes to build depth.
- Removing the one cold patch from a warm image removes most of its focal contrast.
- The eye invents the missing temperature: a grey or desaturated color inside a strong warm or saturated context reads as cold, and the reverse.
- Color is relative: place a color, then adjust it in context until it gives the temperature you want.
- Saturation contrast works both ways (saturated context with desaturated protagonist, or the opposite); an image reads as very colorful when saturation is placed in key spots against large desaturated areas.
- In Photoshop, a masked Levels layer (lowering red) or a masked Hue/Saturation layer can push everything outside the focal light colder.
