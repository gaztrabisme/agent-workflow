## Value, color and saturation as scales

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L16 · 00:00–01:56

Back to our explanation. Besides contrast, each of the big ingredients of color we have discussed, value (*valor*), color and saturation (*saturación*), lets us create a scale. This brings us back to the previous lesson, where we talked about meaning, identity, concept and keywords.

With value we can create a scale. I called it drama and mystery here, though it can be more than that. It is a scale of sensations, running from very luminous, generally happy colors to very dark images. That is what value gives us, and we will break it down shortly with very concrete examples.

With color we can manipulate the intensity of an image. We can go from an entirely red image to one that starts to bring in complementary colors, such as this blue or the skin tone. We can open up the range: we start to have not only red and one complementary color, but red, blue, green, and we keep adding colors. That is the difference between monochromatic and colorful, and our response to these images is quite different. That is our second resource, the second scale we can create: how colorful will the image be?

![1140 · U4 · L16 · 01:08](frames/U04L16-F0108.jpg)

Then there is saturation, which we have also seen. The more we take out of an image (captions: *restar imágenes de una imagen*, "subtract images from an image"), the less active it will be and the weaker the emotional response. We start talking about sadness, mystery and so on. That is our third ingredient.

What is great about color is that with these three ingredients the number of recipes we can create is virtually unlimited. As long as we know these are the ingredients we are playing with, and as long as we know where we want to get to, the possibilities are infinite.

## A colorful but gentle image

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L16 · 01:56–04:24

Here I have taken different images made from this layout and I show you which ingredients were modified to reach which result.

In this image the intention was to make an image that was colorful but gentle. I did not want the kind of colorful of the fan image we made, which we will see right after. This was different: I wanted something a little more melancholic, a little softer, subtle. So I favored a harmony toward the warm tones. There are many warm tones here: yellows, reds, oranges; the greens are warm too. That gives the image a more tender feeling in a way. And I used complementary colors to give the sense that it is a colorful image. One does not exclude the other.

I also want you to see how I handled color. In this image I mostly used light colors. Even though there is this large dark patch, most of the image, or at least the part where all the visual interest happens, is quite light. This is how we should look at our images: always asking ourselves what is happening with each of the ingredients.

It is fine to have some saturation in a peripheral zone. Here, for example, I have saturation, but it does not matter, because this shape works more as a frame for the image than as an element that could distract. What really matters is that this saturated color is surrounded by colors that are less saturated. And since this one does not touch that one, nothing happens; there is no conflict. That is how this image works, and how I got this feeling, the idea of an image that is colorful but relatively calm.

## Terrifying images

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L16 · 05:37–07:21

Now let's go back to the terms we saw in the second unit, when we learned to recognize terrifying images, mysterious images and even happy images; this one would fall into the happy range. These are basically the keys. We already discussed them, but now we will see them in practice.

Terrifying images generally require an absence of light. The image is mostly dark, with points where light stands out, but overall it is dark. The other resource is high contrast: plenty of dark colors directly against light colors. That gives the high-contrast feeling that makes it somewhat gloomy.

How this image works is simple. It is a complementary scheme, as we discussed. Most of the image moves between these two cool colors, this purple-blue and this green. Thanks to that I create my point of contrast, my focal point, which is here: this color, more of a dark ochre or something like that. We can see it down here: a whole range of cool, dark colors with an ochre in the middle.

What is interesting here, again, is to analyze the saturation. Around the whole image there are many saturated colors, and when I get to the center of the image the saturation drops drastically. I have colors so light they are no longer saturated, this desaturated green, and this shape, which is quite desaturated compared with what happens over there. That is a saturation contrast opposite to what people usually consider, which is "I'll have an image low in saturation and a saturated point in the center". Here it is the other way round, and it works very well too.

## Mysterious images

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L16 · 07:21–08:28

What was done here is to create much less value contrast. Across the whole light part, the value contrasts are very close to each other. That gives this misty feeling where we do not know exactly what is happening. And the saturation of the image was lowered a great deal.

A slight complementary scheme is used in the colors: here we have several cool tones, greens and so on, and over here their complement, this yellow. I even used a little saturation to give the sense that there was something a little more noticeable here in the center. I also used other resources, such as the out-of-focus foreground, to give a sense of greater confidentiality (*confidencialidad*).

## Harmony and contrast

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L16 · 08:28–09:04

You have seen it: color theory is really only a way of building a system to explain all the terms related to this subject. But remember, the most important thing is always to keep in mind that we want to create harmony and contrast. Thanks to that, in the next lesson I will be able to talk to you about temperature and how to use saturation properly.

### Key points (derived)

- Value, color and saturation each form a scale: light to dark (happy to dramatic or mysterious), monochromatic to colorful, and saturated to desaturated (active to sad or mysterious).
- The same palette can read as gentle or aggressive: keep neighboring tones close for a gentle image, jump across the hue scale and juxtapose complements for an aggressive one.
- Check a finished image in Photoshop's color picker in S mode; saturation should rise toward the focal point, and a saturated shape at the edge is fine if it frames rather than touches the focal color.
- Terrifying: mostly dark, high value contrast, cool complementary scheme with an ochre focal point; here saturation is high around the edges and drops at the center.
- Mysterious: low value contrast in the light areas, very desaturated and light palette, a dark out-of-focus foreground, a slight complementary accent.
