## The color wheel

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L15 · 01:20–02:52

So I officially present the color wheel (*círculo cromático*). It is a way of laying out all the colors of the visible spectrum on a circle. You could also put them on a line, but the circle is practical because it lets us show the colors, the saturation and even a little of the values.

Look closely: this edge is the color, and as I move toward the center I lose saturation and gain value. My value gets lighter and lighter, I get closer and closer to white, and I lose more and more saturation. That is what you can see here in the evolution of this color.

![1140 · U4 · L15 · 01:50](frames/U04L15-F0150.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A color wheel with fully saturated hues at the rim fading to white at the center, a black-to-white value bar below it, and the cursor at the center.

For example, take this orange. We start here with its most saturated version and I move toward white. I will necessarily lose saturation as I get closer to white, since white is a color that has no saturation at all. It is a pure, totally luminous color, as black is. There are also color wheels that do not go toward white but toward black. That would be the equivalent of pulling, pulling, pulling toward black, and we would also have lost saturation, because on this scale saturation is the horizontal axis: the further right, the more saturated; the further left, the less saturated.

## The three ingredients of color

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L15 · 02:52–03:38

These are the three ingredients of color we have seen throughout this course:
- value (*valor*), which goes from black to white and from white to black;
- color, which is this whole rainbow of possibilities that lets us create compositions;
- saturation or intensity (*saturación* or *intensidad*).

Here I put other names for these words. Value is really the luminosity (*luminosidad*) of a color. Color is its hue (*tono*). Saturation is the intensity, the purity of the color.

## Contrast with each ingredient

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L15 · 03:38–04:20

Once again, all of this matters to us so that we can achieve one of the most important things in images, as we have already discussed: contrast.

With value we can get contrast by setting the darkest value, black, against the lightest value, white. To create contrast in color we only need to take two opposites on the wheel, wherever they are, and we get our maximum contrast. We will explain in a moment what this implies. For saturation it is the same: we take the two extremes, which in this case are any color in its purest, most saturated version, and a white or a black, which are the totally desaturated colors (he says *saturados*), or a gray. Basically it is taking a color, a pure color, which is one extreme, and taking it to any extreme of the value scale, which is a scale that no longer has any color.

## Complementary palettes: maximum contrast

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L15 · 04:20–05:08

This is where I find it interesting to have seen things the way we have so far, because we have spared ourselves terms that are a bit ambiguous when you see them on their own: complementary, monochromatic, triadic, quadratic colors, these rather wild terms that make you say "OK, that's all very well, but I don't understand a thing".

Here, what we call a complementary color palette (*paleta complementaria*) will make much more sense; we will see examples later. It is really a palette based on maximum contrast. It is the equivalent of using white against black: a color and its opposite color. Period. That is the basis of any complementary palette, and that is why there are endless complementary palettes, because they can sit anywhere around the color wheel.

## Monochromatic palettes: null contrast

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L15 · 05:08–05:53

At the other end is the monochromatic palette (*paleta monocromática*). Here we use a single color. We do not create complementarity with an opposite color; we choose the same color, only with a variety of values. So we take a yellow, and the other color (the "complementary" color in quotation marks, that is, the second color of the image) will be the same yellow, only a little darker.

![1140 · U4 · L15 · 05:13](frames/U04L15-F0513.jpg)

On screen: "MONOCROMÁTICA" (Monochromatic); "CONTRASTE NULO" (Null contrast)  
Shows: A wheel with a short line from a yellow at the rim to a slightly darker yellow just inside it.

So the contrast we saw with complementarity, which was maximum, as strong as possible, like white and black, is almost zero in this example. It is a contrast from a black to a slightly lighter black. That is what these monochromatic images produce: much less color contrast, which can be replaced by value contrast.

## Varied palettes: medium contrast

Anchor: 1140 · U4 · L15 · 05:53–07:02

Between these two there is a whole range of palettes that are neither of maximum contrast nor of null contrast. They sit in between, with a medium contrast.

Remember what I explained when I talked about bridges (*puentes*)? In the end, what created the contrast between two colors was the distance between them. Every time we add a new color, we reduce that distance. This means that any palette that is neither complementary nor monochromatic will necessarily be less contrasted than a complementary one. What we lose is color contrast, because the relationship between blue and red is less contrasted than the one between blue and yellow. What we gain is the sense of how colorful the image is. So it will be less contrasted but more colorful.

That is what we see here. This image is less strong, less contrasted than this one, but it starts to have more variation. You can see it even in the grays.

### Key points (derived)

- The color wheel places hue around the rim; toward the center (or toward black, on other wheels) saturation drops and value changes.
- Three ingredients: value (luminosity), color (hue), saturation (intensity). Each has its own maximum contrast: black against white; two opposite hues; a pure hue against white, black or gray.
- Complementary palette: a color and its opposite, maximum color contrast. Monochromatic: one color in several values, almost no color contrast, replaced by value contrast. Varied palettes sit between them: every added color reduces the distance between colors, so contrast drops and colorfulness rises.
- Derivatives of complementary: composite (a color and one near its opposite) and split complementary (a color against two colors near its opposite). Triadic: three families at roughly equal distance.
- Bridge colors, often desaturated, appear even in complementary images.
- Color distribution always supports the underlying value plan.
- Practice palette types in Adobe Color.
