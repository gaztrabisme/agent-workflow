## Definition of contrast

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L05 · 00:28–01:03

Here we are basically in the dark, in black, but a word is hiding in this darkness. Contrast is the difference between one thing and another thing, in this case between values (*valores*): extremes, a tone light enough to stand out against a dark background. That is our definition of contrast.

It is the most important thing in visual development and in visual art in general, and we will talk about it throughout this course.

## Contrast in everyday experience

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L05 · 05:39–06:26

This question of contrast is funny, because you can even compare it with the experiences we have. For example, you finish a video game: you set a super score, you reached a brutal level of experience, or you finished it and it was a super difficult game. You feel like the best in the country, or wherever. And the next day, darn, you see that what you did was just done by a 12-year-old in 5 hours. That is also contrast. Did we lose anything? When the contrast is favorable, our experience seems good to us. The context changes and our experience seems bad. It is the same experience.

I insist on this because it is one of the fundamental principles of human experience: contrast, and how much we depend on the visual context to analyze and feel things.

Because I want to present an exercise to you, a practice that is very common in the industry where I have the most experience and, I must confess, the most affection: the animated film industry. It is the color script.

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A row of seven thumbnails: sunset beach, neon city, color powder crowd, sunset beach, forest road, abandoned house, sunset beach.

Making a color script means becoming a master of context and contrast. It is creating several light, colorful images so that when you reach a dark image it is a surprise, it is interesting, and it looks darker than it really is. It is creating a whole evolution of tones to arrive at green, desaturated, dark images in order to present the villain of the film. I guarantee you that this red would not look so impressive if it were not surrounded or preceded by much duller colors.

The color script is the application of the principles of contrast and context. It is telling stories no longer with single images but with sequences of images. In the end the principle is the same: how I will perceive and interpret an image depends on what happens before and after it.

## Local color: Wes Anderson

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L05 · 07:51–09:18

This exercise is very instructive in many respects, and we can see it in different films.

So far we have mostly seen color tendencies in one image or another dictated by light. We talked about light images and dark images; here the image looks red because it has a red light. But there are masters of the image, such as Wes Anderson without doubt, who use another kind of resource to create color and to create contrast. They use, for example, the resource of what we call local color (*color local*). In the next lesson we will explain in depth what local color is and how it differs from the color of light. Local color here is simply the color of the objects.

### Key points (derived)

- Contrast is the difference between one thing and another; with values, it is a tone light enough to stand out against a dark background.
- Perception is relative: an image's lightness or colorfulness is judged against what surrounds it and what was seen just before it.
- To make one color look stronger without changing it, remove that color from its surroundings (for example, grey around a red).
- A color script is a sequence of images arranged so that each one's light and color are set up by the ones before and after it, as in Clement Griselain's art for Minions.
- Color and contrast can come from local color, the color of the objects themselves, rather than from light, as in Wes Anderson's The Grand Budapest Hotel.
