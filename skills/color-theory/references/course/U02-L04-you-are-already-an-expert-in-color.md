## You are already an expert: value

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L04 · 01:41–02:57

This little game leads to the most important conclusion of the whole course, which is why it was important to start with it: we are experts in color. You are already an expert in color. You already know how to identify which colors work and which kinds of images give which kinds of feelings, and that is the main point of color.

![1140 · U2 · L04 · 01:50](frames/U02L04-F0150.jpg)

On screen: "- Todos somos expertos en color - CONFÍA EN TU INSTINTO" [We are all experts in color. Trust your instinct]  
Shows: Text slide on a green background with a color wheel below the words.

What I wanted to highlight is what happened in the game: you knew instinctively, without any theoretical introduction, that light values made happy images.

What do I mean by values? Value (*valor*) is how luminous a color is, that is, how close to white it is; the absence of value, or dark values, is how close to black it is. In this whole first unit I will not go deep into technical terms. Again, I want you to approach color in a much more sensory, much more perceptive way, and to trust your instinct about color. So for now we will leave it at that: value is the idea of how luminous a color is.

And you knew straight away that light values corresponded to happy images, and dark values to sad images, or in this case terrifying ones.

## Saturation

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L04 · 02:57–03:30

The same goes for color as such: the purity of the color. Here we have a very pure red, and here a much more washed-out orange; we call that desaturated (*desaturado*).

What matters is that you also identified by instinct that the more desaturated, or lacking in intensity, the colors of an image are, the sadder or more mysterious it can look. Conversely, saturated, very vivid colors speak of greater intensity and happiness in an image. That is a very important point.

But what matters for this lesson, and for the course in general, is to see that red is not necessarily always anger, power, violence or the like. Look at this Elmo: he cannot be violent or anything terrible. ## Perceiving versus practicing color

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L04 · 05:55–06:54

What mattered most in this lesson, and what I really want you to be aware of, is that you are already an expert in color. I keep insisting because it is very important: sometimes when we make a piece in color we say, "this is really bad (*está bien chafa*), I'm no expert at all." You are not yet an expert at making color, at practicing color, but you are already an expert at feeling, perceiving and understanding color.

What you still lack, and what I intend to give you in this course, is control over the theory, understanding it now in theoretical terms with all the right vocabulary, and practicing color so that you have a very real, very concrete understanding of it. I have to be honest with you: this is a long process and it takes a lot of practice.

## Value and saturation set the emotional tone

Anchor: 1140 · U2 · L04 · 06:54–07:47

The other thing we have seen is that color is made up of values, meaning how luminous or dark a color is, and of color and saturation as such, meaning how pure the color is. Together these define the emotional tonality of an image: how an image makes us feel. That is extremely important, and it will be at the center of this course and of this practice.

As you saw, you already have a lot of culture and vocabulary on this subject. Don't forget that color is a very powerful tool for creating atmosphere and tension in an image. In the following lessons you will see that hues and values are your best allies for controlling this.

### Key points (derived)

- Viewers, including you, already read mood from color instinctively: light values read as happy, dark values as sad or terrifying, desaturated colors as sad or mysterious, saturated colors as intense and happy.
- Value is how close a color is to white (light) or black (dark); saturation is how pure or intense it is.
- Charts of color meanings (such as StudioBinder's) list commonly accepted associations, but no color has a universal meaning: it depends on how it is made and what it is associated with in context.
- What remains to learn is theory, vocabulary and a lot of practice, built on the perception you already have.
