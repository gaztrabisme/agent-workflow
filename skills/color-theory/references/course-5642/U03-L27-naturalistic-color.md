## The denotative function: naturalistic color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L27 · 00:00–01:18

In this lesson we tackle the denotative function of color, starting with its first chromatic variable: naturalistic (*naturalista*) color. We explain the use of the realistic representation of color.

If we look up the term "natural" in the dictionary, among the definitions we find, we can highlight the following: what pertains to, or conforms to, the quality or property of things as they are found in nature, without transformation, or what imitates nature faithfully, with all its traits and characteristics. If we look up "naturalista," we find it refers to naturalism (*naturalismo*): the disposition toward a faithful representation drawn from reality. Nature here is the reference point for what we call reality.

Naturalistic color, in this sense, is an iconic chromatism: it tries to imitate the appearance of things in our surroundings as faithfully as possible. It is, in this sense, a realistic color that usually goes hand in hand with figurative form.

## Color accelerates identification: color versus black-and-white

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L27 · 01:18–02:48

In a black-and-white image, a situation of perceptual indeterminacy can arise, which is why some effort is needed to read the objects in the image — especially when there are elements of similar shape and size. In these cases, the chromatic expressiveness of iconic color acts as an accelerator for identifying what is represented in the image. Adding a thing's natural color accentuates the effect of reality, makes the figures more easily identifiable, and increases the overall intelligibility of the image. An orange is more real when reproduced in orange. In this way, naturalistic iconic color becomes an essential element of the realistic image that speeds up the identification of the elements represented.

Forms of reality are more real in an image when shown in color than in black and white — color provides information that the same monochrome image cannot achieve. In this sense, a color image can be easier to understand and identify, since its representation is more faithful and more complete in its informative dimension.

Even so, within naturalistic color we can distinguish two variants: hyperrealistic (*hiperrealista*) color and realistic color. In the application of hyperrealistic color, the aim is to show an image more real than reality itself. In the use of realistic color, the aim is to represent the real exactly as it is.

## Naturalistic color in its hyperrealist form

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L27 · 02:48–06:19

Let's look first at examples of naturalistic color in its hyperrealist facet. The hyperrealist function of color is not only about aiding the identification of what is represented, nor about the intelligibility or clarity of images. This variable of color has the power to impress, to fascinate — color manifests itself by calling the observer's attention. At this level color becomes more detailed and precise, as for example in the photographic hyperrealism of the artist Richard Estes. The image you see on screen is not a photograph but a pictorial work that represents visible reality with exactness, both in form and in color.

The hyperrealist form — the almost microscopic detail in the representation of reality — is reinforced when it is rendered with its natural colors, or the closest approximation to them. Naturalistic color, combined with the microscopic texture of the things represented, and not only with their form, produces a sensory result. You can observe this in this pictorial portrait made by the artist Marco Grassi, which appeals to different registers of optical and tactile sensation. This is how an effect of fascination arises in contemplating the image, making it more seductive than reality itself.

## Naturalistic color in its realist form

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L27 · 06:19–08:30

Now let's move to the other side of naturalistic color: its realist dimension. This realist chromatic variant aims to reproduce the external world — the object of interest — faithfully, exactly as it is. The idea is that, in this realist variant, the image preserves representational fidelity in both form and color. Color can likewise be more or less iconic, more or less faithful, more or less naturalistic or abstract. This idea is easy to see in this work by the French painter Gustave Courbet, who led the Realist movement during the 19th century: this artistic movement sought to represent the external world in all its beauty and ugliness, without idealizing it as the Classicists and Romantics had done.

![5642 · U3 · L27 · 07:06](frames/U03L27-F0706.jpg)

On screen: "Función denotativa" sidebar [rest omitted, full list at 03:24]  
Shows: A Gustave Courbet painting of a fox in the snow, gripping its prey, rendered with realist detail in its fur and the winter landscape.

The same was true of the painter Jean-François Millet in this Realist work titled The Gleaners (*Las espigadoras*), showing, in a very humanist way, the reality of 19th-century rural society. The idea of realist color would be the same: to represent reality through color exactly as it is, or as it is understood to be.

In the world of illustration and graphic design, we can find the resource of naturalistic color, for example, in scientific illustrations that aim to inform, to show how things are. Also in illustrated encyclopedias, where the function of the images is to describe objects, plants, animals, etc., exactly as they are. In these formats we could say that realist color takes on a didactic function.

## Naturalistic color as an aid when form is abstract

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L27 · 08:30–09:56

In graphic marks, because of their particular characteristics and their tendency toward simplicity and schematization, it is very difficult to find naturalistic colors. Those graphic marks with iconic colors try to imitate the color things have in reality, but it is more common to find exalted or schematic colors, because these marks need to be memorable.

Although exalted and schematic color, which we will analyze later, are more typical in the graphic field of posters, we can find some posters with a natural treatment of color — this naturalistic treatment is normally tied to figurative photographic representation.

Although naturalistic color is normally tied to figurative forms, we can find cases where the form has a lower level of iconicity than the color. It is in these cases that naturalistic iconic color becomes essential for identifying the figure. When an image contains a fairly high degree of abstraction in its form, color helps individualize the details — it provides more data for identifying its referent. If we remove the colors, this identification becomes harder. In this LEGO advertising poster, the colors speed up the identification of René Magritte's surrealist work: since the image is somewhat ambiguous in its forms, the application of iconic colors is necessary to identify what each element is.

### Key points (derived)

- Naturalistic color is iconic chromatism that imitates the real appearance of things as faithfully as possible; it has two variants: hyperrealistic (more real than reality) and realistic (reality exactly as it is).
- Adding natural color to an image accelerates identification and increases intelligibility compared with black and white, because color adds information monochrome cannot supply.
- Hyperrealist color has the power to fascinate, not just to inform — seen in photorealistic painting (Richard Estes, Marco Grassi), hyperrealist illustration (Marcelo Barenghi), and packaging/book covers that appeal to touch as well as sight.
- Realist color, tied to the 19th-century Realist movement (Courbet, Millet), aims to show the world "as it is," and carries a didactic function in scientific illustration and encyclopedias.
- Graphic marks rarely use naturalistic color, because they favor memorable exalted or schematic color instead; naturalistic color becomes essential, however, when a form is too abstract or ambiguous to read without it (the LEGO/Magritte example).
- Several packaging and poster examples build their whole design logic around one naturalistic color: fruit packaging, a Mars-colored poster, a Haiti-earth-colored relief poster, a fern-patterned water bottle, and fruit-shaped toilet-paper rolls.
