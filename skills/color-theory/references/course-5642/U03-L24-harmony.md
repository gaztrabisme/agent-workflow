## What studying harmony means

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 00:29–01:36

Studying a chromatic composition means essentially considering its formal presence. A design product must be, of course, the interrelation of its parts in a composition — a harmonic system. To harmonize means to coordinate the different values color takes on in a composition. Harmony is synonymous with unity, and the goal of creating that unity is to strengthen the work's visual effect: its power of impression and its power of attraction — aspects essential to any process of visual communication.

## Harmony as order

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 02:23–03:37

Harmonizing colors means ordering the chromatic values of a composition: establishing a proper balance, proportion and correspondence between the different parts of a whole, while at the same time provoking and moderating the various forms of contrast (covered in the next lesson). The harmonic principle treats a color composition as a whole where everything fits everything else. The terms harmony and disharmony are equivalent to order and disorder — terms comparable, for example, to the study of musical harmony. Musical harmony is produced by planning sound across its dimensions of pitch, duration, intensity and timbre; failing to attend to these dimensions produces what we call noise, really a kind of physical disorder of vibrations.

![5642 · U3 · L24 · 03:18](frames/U03L24-F0318.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A piano keyboard with each key tinted a different spectrum color, illustrating the analogy between musical and color harmony.

Keep one principle in mind from this analogy between color and music: color, too, can produce noise. Don't forget it — harmony is built by establishing a relationship shared through one or several qualities of color: tone (*tono*), luminosity and saturation.

## The three qualities of color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 03:37–05:08

Tone is the specific type of maximum chromatism a color has, and what distinguishes it from others. The eye clearly perceives six extreme chromatic positions: the primary colors — yellow, red and blue — and the secondary colors — green, orange and violet. Luminosity is how light or dark a color is compared against a luminosity scale running from white to black.

![5642 · U3 · L24 · 04:34](frames/U03L24-F0434.jpg)

On screen: "Dimensiones del color — Tono / Luminosidad / Saturación" (Dimensions of color — Tone / Luminosity / Saturation), a 10-step luminosity scale (1 near-white to 10 black) crossed with a row of hues, credited "Frans Gerritsen" (captions: *Franz Erichsen*), with circles marking yellow at step 3, cyan at 4, magenta at 5, green at 6, red at 7 and violet at 8.  
Shows: A grid ordering each hue by its luminosity value against a 10-step white-to-black scale.

This scale was proposed by professor and consultant Frans Gerritsen, who ordered colors by their degree of luminosity on a 10-step chromatic scale numbered from white to black. On this scale, for example, yellow would have a luminosity of 3, followed by cyan at 4, magenta at 5, green at 6, red at 7 and violet at 8.

Saturation is a color's degree of purity. A color is highly saturated when it shows its greatest degree of purity — that is, its tone as it appears on the color wheel, with no mixture of black, grey or white. A saturation scale for a color registers the neutralizing of an intense color's coloring capacity, and that neutralizing can be done by mixing a given color with white, grey, black, or even with its complementary color.

![5642 · U3 · L24 · 05:08](frames/U03L24-F0508.jpg)

On screen: "Dimensiones del color — Saturación" (Dimensions of color — Saturation): "Mezcla con blanco / Mezcla con gris / Mezcla con negro / Mezcla con complementario" (Mixed with white / Mixed with grey / Mixed with black / Mixed with complementary), each row showing red stepped through five degrees of that mixture.  
Shows: Four horizontal five-step gradients showing how mixing red with white, grey, black or its complementary green progressively desaturates it.

## The color wheel

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 05:49–06:28

Before covering the harmony types, one basic tool needs introducing: the color wheel (*círculo cromático*). On it you can establish chromatic chords, in musical terms, to control the overall harmony of the colors available. This wheel is based on the three primary colors — yellow, red and blue — the secondary colors — orange, violet and green, which arise from mixing two primaries — and the tertiary colors, which appear from mixing a primary with an adjacent secondary: yellow-orange, red-orange, red-violet, blue-violet, blue-green and yellow-green.

Shows: Four color wheels: a full reference wheel, then three isolating just the primary triad, the secondary triad, and the six tertiary colors.

## Monochromatic harmony

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 07:03–07:48

Monochromatic analogies (*analogía monocromática*) are the most restricted tonal harmonies: they rely on a single color and its different values and intensities — for example, as seen in a Picasso work, a blue and its gradations toward white, grey or black. Monochromatic compositions are harmonic by nature, though they can sometimes feel monotonous; to counter that, it's important to seek nuance sensitively within a single color. It's a very simple harmony to use, and as these posters show, the result is sober, elegant, with a unifying and harmonious effect.

## Contiguous analogous harmony

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 07:48–08:42

Contiguous tonal analogies (*análoga por contigüidad*) are built on two or more colors adjacent on the color wheel, generally composed of one dominant tone and other colors derived from it; these colors harmonize through their resemblance, something found continually in nature itself. In these posters, designed for the 1972 Munich Olympics by Otl Aicher (captions: *Lottie Liger*), a strict harmonic criterion of analogous colors is visible, also used to coordinate every poster series produced for the games. This same resource of contiguous color analogy also appears in a further selection of graphic brands: the close relationship or mutual similarity of adjacent colors easily and effectively produces a harmonious feeling.

## Cool analogous harmony

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 08:42–09:19

A cool harmony (*armonía fría*) has blue as the dominant color in the composition, together with the colors adjacent to it on the wheel, from yellow-green to violet. Just as the term "cool" describes this range, synonymous terms are generally associated with these colors too: indifferent, calm, quiet, reserved, distant, moderate — a point the course will return to when covering this color's psychological meaning.

## Warm analogous harmony

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 09:19–09:59

Unlike cool harmonies, in warm harmonies (*armonía cálida*) it is red that dominates the composition, together with its adjacent colors on the wheel, from yellow to red-violet. This warm range is typically used to accentuate sensations associated with that temperature: energy, excitement, intensity, vitality, violence. An example of warm color use appears in this chromatic analysis of fuel-brand logos — products that logically associate with energy, movement, fire and heat.

## Complementary harmony

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 09:59–10:45

Complementary harmony (*armonía por complementariedad*) comes from using colors accompanied by their opposites on the color wheel — a harmony that makes tangible the principle that opposite poles attract. Complementarity is a vital principle found even in the colors present in nature, which logically shapes our sensory relationship with color. Using complementary colors is a common resource for achieving effective contrast, as the colors mutually reinforce each other and appear more intense. This harmony suits provoking an immediate, strong impact.

## Triadic harmony

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 10:45–11:32

A triadic harmony (*armonía triádica*) uses three colors equidistant on the color wheel. Forming an equilateral triangle inside the wheel gives three colors that produce a harmonious effect — a harmony in which none of the three colors resembles the others in tone, for example red, blue and yellow, or green, violet and orange. This type of harmony offers a strong visual contrast while keeping color richness; a triadic harmony is not as contrasting as a complementary one, but the resulting compositions are more balanced and harmonious — a very suitable resource for conveying vitality through color.

Shows: Rothko's stacked yellow, red and blue color fields beside Gauguin's Tahitian scene in orange, green and violet — both built on a triad of hues equidistant on the wheel.

## Tetradic harmony

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 11:32–12:17

A tetradic harmony (*armonía tétrada*) rests on the ordered effect generated by two pairs of complementary colors. It is a harder harmonic composition to achieve, requiring the distances between tones to stay equidistant. As you can also see in design work, the effect of this harmony is multicolored, varied and vibrant — a type of harmonic composition more complicated to control than the previous ones.

## High luminosity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 12:17–13:03

Moving now to high luminosity: these harmonic situations appear whenever colors share the same degree of luminosity. Any luminosity level established as a general link across the colors in a work can form a type of luminous harmony. High-luminosity harmonies show an overall predominance, or a particular point of interest, of light, luminous colors. Also called solar harmony, it generates a cheerful, light effect. Adding white to colors often produces a range of pastel colors, as in the poster by Massimo Vignelli for Pirelli or the one designed by Milton Glaser for Bob Dylan.

## High saturation

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 13:21–13:51

A high-saturation harmony occurs when colors appear at their greatest degree of saturation — the colors as they appear on the color wheel itself. High-saturation harmonic works are generally sharp, vital and strong. This heightened saturation can also be reinforced through geometric shapes, straight profiles, and the cool, mechanical-looking treatments characteristic of machine reproduction.

## Low saturation

Low-saturation harmonies tend toward colors we might call exquisite, requiring great control — raising or lowering saturation harmony in a painted medium demands real skill and knowledge. The effects produced by a low-saturation harmony are very soothing, intellectual, elegant, solemn, and could even be seen as luxurious. Low-saturation colors let form be appreciated without drawing attention to themselves.

## Grey harmonies

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L24 · 14:26–14:51

Grey harmonies are those in which colors have lost their chromatic power and become greys of different shades. As these posters show, grey harmonies place all their emphasis on form and tend to convey seriousness, austerity, coldness, sobriety — and, as a consequence, can sometimes acquire a degree of elegance.

### Key points (derived)

- Harmony means ordering a composition's color values into balance, proportion and correspondence — the visual equivalent of musical harmony, where a lack of order becomes "noise," whether in sound or in color.
- Every harmony type in this lesson is built from just three color qualities: tone, luminosity and saturation, measured against tools like the color wheel and Frans Gerritsen's 10-step luminosity scale.
- Tone-based harmonies range from a single hue (monochromatic) through adjacent hues (contiguous, cool, warm) to structured contrast (complementary, triadic, tetradic); luminosity and saturation each split into a high, low (and, for saturation, a fully grey) variant.
- The same painting or logo can be read for more than one dimension at once — Van Gogh's self-portrait and Rothko's color-field paintings each anchor two different named-artist examples of a specific, defined harmony type, not just "nice color."
- The lesson closes by mapping all twelve harmony types onto real corporate logos, showing this is a working design vocabulary, not just an art-history exercise.
