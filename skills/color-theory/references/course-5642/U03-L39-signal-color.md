## What signal color is

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L39 · 00:00–02:06

This lesson covers the second variable of sign color (*color sígnico*): signal color (*color señalético*). Here color can work both as a signal color (*color señal*) and as a sign color (*color signo*).

Señalético comes from the word *señal* (signal, sign). Among its dictionary definitions we can highlight: a mark, indication or gesture; a thing that represents or substitutes another; a mark placed on things to make them known and distinguish them from others; a sign or means used to later remember something; a distinguishing mark or brand; an image or representation of something; and a notice communicated, in whatever way, to gather at a set place or to carry out some other action.

Looking up *señalético* itself, two definitions turn up: belonging or relating to signage (*señalética*) — note that the dictionary sends us here to signage design (*diseño señalético*) — and a set of signals to inform about something. Within these definitions, signal color is material color (*color-materia*): yes, it is a color with autonomous expression, such as can be found inside paint tins, printing inks, or the color charts of plastic, vinyl and other material manufacturers for chromatic signage.

So signal color, as material color, can be seen as a concrete functional variable of schematic color: the signal variant extracts from color all its schematic power, all its strength, turning it into a strongly sensory code. What signal color and schematic color share is that both are flat, autonomous colors with a sign-carrying (*sígnico*) character.

In summary: signal color is at once color-señal, because it transmits an instant, immediate light sensation, and color-signo, because it implies a meaning — even if arbitrary.

## Signal color vs. camouflage

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L39 · 02:06–02:18

First, signal color is a color-señal because it seeks the opposite effect to colors that try to blend in or camouflage with their surroundings. Signal color's nature is to be strongly visible in whatever context it appears — to display itself in its surroundings, to contrast, in short, not to be discreet.

## Signal color as sign: meanings and codes

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L39 · 02:18–02:36

Second, it is a color-signo, because each color can signify a concept or idea — red for prohibition, yellow for danger, green for safety. These meanings can sometimes be arbitrary, but on other occasions, as you may have already noticed, they have a symbolic basis rooted in psychology. A color's value as a sign, carrying meaning, is only defined within a system that integrates it.

## Where signal colors appear: traffic and industrial signage

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L39 · 02:36–03:20

What colors could count as signal colors? Besides traffic-light colors, there are the colors of vertical road and urban signage, the colors of horizontal markings for vehicles and pedestrians, and the colors used in industrial facilities — pipes, machinery, electrical, gas and liquid conductors. What defines this type of signal color is its function as a code: through that code, each color has a meaning in a given context because it is information.

## Transit maps: New York City subway

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L39 · 06:47–07:17

These schematic, saturated colors also appear in maps designed to guide users through different routes — public-transport maps for bus routes, trams, the metro. One example is the use of color in the New York subway map, in the tradition established by Massimo Vignelli (captions: *Máximo Viñel*). Nobody reads psychological or symbolic meaning into these colors: given the context of use, it's understood that color's job here is simply to tell the subway lines apart. Nobody thinks "if I take the red line I'll go faster" or "if I take the green line I'll be more eco-friendly." The context of use alone makes clear that color is being used to mark each line.

## Legibility study: figure/ground color pairs

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L39 · 08:03–09:04

On this point of optimizing visibility, here's one of the studies on the legibility of color pairs in figure and ground: a table prepared by Karl Borggrafe (captions: *Karl Borgreifs*), ranking pairs of colors by how legible typography becomes in visual designs. To reach these conclusions he used a tachistoscope, timing exactly how long it took to read and identify text on different graphic media — how easily text on different logos, for example, can be read and understood. These figure/ground discrimination values might also carry over to icon marks (*iconotipos*), helping a shape register better against its background. The same conclusions apply to warning, information and danger pictograms.

## Signal within the sign: highlighting a key point

To close this lesson: this chromatic variable isn't only used to mark out the spaces where people act — it can also signal an element within the graphic space itself. Color then draws visual attention according to a precise strategy set by the designer: signals are placed inside the graphic sign to focus attention on certain key points, guiding the viewer's gaze and setting the scale and the fixations their eye will make when reading the piece. ### Key points (derived)

- Signal color (*color señalético*) works two ways at once: as color-señal (an instant optical signal, the opposite of camouflage) and as color-signo (a color with an assigned, sometimes arbitrary, meaning).
- It shares schematic color's flat, pure, autonomous, saturated character, but its purpose is narrower: to function as a code within a system (traffic, industry, weather, maritime, recycling).
- Orange and yellow test as the most visible colors over time and distance, which is why they dominate safety and hazard signage.
- The same logic scales from wayfinding systems (zoos, offices, seafronts, museums, transit maps) down to a single product detail (a red jeans tab, a yellow sticky note, a colored accent inside a logo or book cover).
- Color meaning is contextual: the same violet that signals "no lactose" on a milk carton carries no such meaning elsewhere.
- Signal color sets up the next variable, emblematic color, covered in the following lesson.
