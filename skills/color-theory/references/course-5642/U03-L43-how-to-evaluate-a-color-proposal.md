## The semantic differential: Osgood's instrument

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L43 · 00:26–01:52

The teacher proposes a measuring device to support analysis, evaluation and synthesis of how a color proposal is perceived by its potential users. This instrument, the semantic differential, was developed by a team led by American psychologist Charles Osgood. Its foundations were first published in 1957 in the book *The Measurement of Meaning*, and it is still widely used today by social- and market-research organisations as an evaluation technique — a tool for weighing connotative meanings.

Its basic principle is that the diversity of meanings a stimulus (such as color) can carry is reducible to variations along a limited number of functional dimensions. The hypothesis behind the technique is that a word's meaning includes, for an individual, not only its most basic denotative sense but other components with subtler, harder-to-describe connotations. For example, the word "warm" (*cálido*), beyond its basic denotative meaning of giving heat, carries multiple shades of meaning that we recognise and understand — and, importantly for designers, that can be shared socially.

## Building the scale: bipolar adjectives across seven steps

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L43 · 02:41–03:16

Returning to Osgood's semantic differential: to apply this technique, one must first establish a series of descriptive adjectives that define the intended meaning of a color proposal. These adjectives are set up as bipolar pairs — two general limits of meaning, such as warm and cold — and the space between the two limits is divided into seven possible categories. This lets one register reactions to a color's meaning by marking how close or far they sit from one or other of the polar adjectives.

## Three dimensions of color's communicative function

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L43 · 03:44–04:34

To help set the different polar adjectives, the lesson proposes considering three dimensions of color's communicative function: the aesthetic dimension, the indicative-instrumental dimension, and the symbolic-social dimension. The aesthetic dimension relates to the syntax of color, letting us judge color against our preferences for a series of perceptible traits; here the emotional stimulus comes from color's physical presence — its initial impact. As a generic example, the lesson shows certain polar adjectives for this dimension; in practice, the ones chosen should depend on the piece being analysed, the communicative intentions behind the color, and the intended audience.

## Aesthetic dimension: examples across drink packaging

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L43 · 04:34–06:56

To show what chromatic situations might sit at each pole of the aesthetic dimension, the lesson works through examples on a single kind of support — drink packaging.

### Frío / Cálido (Cold / Warm)
Cold colors impose distance, separation and reserve, and are often used to evoke cold water, winter, cleanliness. Warm colors suggest physical and emotional heat, energy, stimulation.

### Pasivo / Activo (Passive / Active)
Passive colors tend to recede when seen from a distance, conferring stability, calm and security. Active colors can generate an intensely vibrant, vigorous image — colors that stand out, stimulating vitally.

![5642 · U3 · L43 · 05:12](frames/U03L43-F0512.jpg)

Shows: "Relax" wine and "Hatsu" tea bottles, muted blue and teal (passive), beside bright orange Lucozade and red Gatorade bottles (active).

### Natural / Artificial
Natural colors evoke landscape, nostalgic for the simplicity and charm of our ecosystem. Artificial colors evoke human adulteration — less subtle colors with strong pulling power, creating a transgressive, brash, modern visual language.

### Exclusivo / Popular (Exclusive / Popular)
The exclusive is, by definition, for the minority — it tends to exploit the fascination of scarce natural materials such as precious metals, and colors associated in context with moderation and elegance, such as black. Popular colors, depending on context, can be identified with cheap, mass-produced products: saturated primary colors in strong contrast, always seeking popular attraction and impact.

![5642 · U3 · L43 · 05:57](frames/U03L43-F0557.jpg)

### Individual / Colectivo (Individual / Collective)
Individuality includes freedom of personal expression, resulting in new, eccentric, different concepts — strange and unusual combinations that surprise and draw attention. Collective colors aim for a result that is agreed, safe and socially proven, highlighting collective solidarity and sometimes leaning on stereotypes assigned to colors in given situations — for example, blue for water.

Shows: Unusual "Speed" and "220" energy-drink bottles (individual) beside a plain Font Vella water bottle and a Johnnie Walker Red Label whisky bottle (collective).

### Tradicional / Moderno (Traditional / Modern)
By traditional we mean what has been practised for a long time — colors rooted in a social group, associated with its past. Modern colors take a different turn from traditional ones: clarity, sharpness, saturation, brightness, fluorescence and so on, trying to generate an eminently current image.

## Symbolic-social dimension: examples across drink packaging

### Agradable / Desagradable (Pleasant / Unpleasant)
Colors can slip into our brain, triggering psychological springs of interest, well-being, seduction, pleasure — or, conversely, of discomfort, dislike, harshness. Colors are proven to affect our appreciation of flavour and, in that way, influence our appetite.

### Vulgar / Selecto (Vulgar / Select)
Vulgar color implies total indifference to the chromatic stimulus, either because it is absolutely common and repetitive in its context, or because it connotes something simple, insignificant or uninteresting. Select colors, by contrast, seek differentiation and association with elitist, distinguished, cultured, sophisticated qualities.

### Infantil / Maduro (Childlike / Mature)
Childlike colors suggest the world of children through symbolic colors tied to their environment — toys, illustrations, clothing tend to be pastel, luminous, bright. Mature colors are associated with adult environments and their connotations of seriousness, sobriety, prudence; their colors tend to be earthy, metallic or brown.

Shows: A playful illustrated "Big Bear" bottle and a pink-elephant "Delirium Tremens" bottle (childlike) beside earth-toned "Pikes" and "Mayflower" bottles with vintage labels (mature).

### Insólito / Habitual (Unusual / Habitual)
Unusual colors are those that arise surprisingly — unexpected in the context where they appear; often their aim is to transcribe a supposed social norm. Habitual colors are those already considered a social norm, repeated in the same situation of use — for example, the gold and earth tones that identify beers wanting to present themselves as traditional.

### Barato / Caro (Cheap / Expensive)
Nothing has a fixed meaning in our society, and whether something looks cheap or expensive is a matter of pure perception — as Leonardo da Vinci already said, all our knowledge originates in our perceptions. Cheap and expensive are both social judgements shaped by our sensations, experiences and associations with color. Saturated, vivid, luminous, popular colors are normally associated with low prices, unlike sophisticated colors, metallic materials and combinations with black, which are associated with expensive products.

### Interesante / Irrelevante (Interesting / Irrelevant)
"Interesting" relates to the idea of motivation — the cause of movement. An interesting chromatic situation is one that lifts the spirit toward a support, generates curiosity, suggests some benefit or usefulness. The irrelevant is that in which we perceive no special reward, fundamentally a matter of insignificance.

Even as gender barriers are breaking down, these terms are still current in visual communication generally, and in color specifically. Intentions to communicate the feminine tend to use light colors, muted contrasts and the persistent symbolic resource of pink. Colors read as masculine tend to be saturated, sober, earthy.

## Purpose of the technique, and closing

### Key points (derived)

- The semantic differential (Osgood, 1957) rates a stimulus's connotative meaning on 7-point scales between bipolar adjectives (e.g. warm–cold); it remains a standard social/market-research tool.
- Color's communicative function has three dimensions: aesthetic (color's syntax and initial impact, 8 adjective pairs), indicative-instrumental, and symbolic-social — 24 pairs across all three in the worked example.
- Worked pairs shown on drink packaging: cold/warm, passive/active, natural/artificial, exclusive/popular, individual/collective, traditional/modern, pleasant/unpleasant, vulgar/select, childlike/mature, unusual/habitual, cheap/expensive, interesting/irrelevant, feminine/masculine.
- Case study: Benham's Gin — individual student responses were averaged into a polarity profile for each of two bottle variants, then the two profiles were compared to discuss differences and similarities in perceived meaning.
- Applying the tool: get a semantic map, check/estimate ratings, study perceptual shifts across the map, and where interpretations diverge, analyse each factor's position and intensity.
