## Brown: a realist, everyday color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L36 · 00:00–00:53

In this lesson, the teachers show some of the psychological and symbolic aspects of the colors brown, white, black and grey.

Brown is a complex color that comes from mixing other colors. It is a very common color in nature, and this is the reference that gives its many shades their names: coffee, chocolate, mocha, "carmelita", cinnamon, "pardo", chestnut. These shades of brown evoke very different things. It is the realist color par excellence, probably because it is associated with the ground we walk on, and by extension has cosy evocations.

With so many bright, eye-catching colors available to choose from, brown rarely comes to mind when picking a color for a graphic brand. However, from the point of view of visual communication it has an obvious significant value that can communicate important values: tradition, nobility, craftsmanship, antiquity, durability, warmth, etc.

## A universal, recurring symbol

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L36 · 05:43–06:21

In general terms, white evokes the clean, purity, and by extension innocence. In this sense it is a very strong and recurring symbol in different cultures. In almost any part of the planet, white refers to the immaculate. Why? One reason may be that it is relatively easy to make something uniform and homogeneous with white, more so than with other colors. Snow may also have strengthened this association of ideas, when an unbroken blanket spreads evenly over the fields, taking on a monochrome, pure appearance — hence the appearance of feelings of serenity, silence, peace, etc.

## Wedding dresses: white and its history

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L36 · 06:21–06:40

The tradition of the color white in wedding dresses has its origin in the ancient tradition of marrying in white, when the woman's virginity was important within Christian marriage. But wedding dresses have not always been white: in the Middle Ages, for example, they were red, since red represented royalty and power.

## Good and evil, white and black

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L36 · 07:50–08:23

White sits alongside black at the extremes of the greyscale, which is why the two have a limit value. In this way, good and evil are usually connoted through these two colors: evil is the color black, and good is the color white. In this context white carries a strong symbol: that of divine light. Angels, God's messengers, are also represented in white, in opposition to the black angels sent by the devil. Death is dressed in white when sent by God, and in black when sent by the devil.

## Black: technically not a color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L36 · 10:06–11:00

Technically, black is not a color, but the result of any color at its darkest luminosity. Like white, black can be considered an absolute color, sitting at either end of the whole range of colors: absence or presence. Black represents darkness. The concepts attributed to it are night, death, sadness or pessimism. And because of its association with silence, it can also represent seriousness, sobriety, discretion or elegance.

We have gone over a review of the meanings attributed to the colors brown, white, black and grey. Next, Leire will show the possible meanings attributed to pastel colors and some of the colors considered metallic.

### Key points (derived)

- Brown is common in nature (earth, wood, coffee, chocolate) and reads as realist, cosy and reliable; it rarely gets chosen for a brand, but communicates tradition, nobility, craftsmanship, antiquity and warmth (Ofrenda coffee, Vegamar wine, Eiro, Frangelico, Soulbeer, Son Moragues).
- Brown also carries negative associations through decay (rot, rust, wilting, autumn) and, via Van Eyck's Arnolfini portrait, a traditional link to masculinity.
- White is a neutral, absence-of-color value that reads across cultures as pure, clean and immaculate (reinforced by snow), but its meaning depends on context: virginity in wedding dresses (an ancient tradition, though medieval brides actually wore red), divine light and angels, or its opposite, the indistinct white of ghosts and spectres.
- White functions practically as the default background of graphic design, which drains it of meaning as anything other than a separator — but designers who make white space the subject (Müller-Brockmann, The Pressery, negative-space logos) restore its value.
- Black is technically not a color but the darkest state of any color; it splits into a negative reading (night, death, mourning, fear — Goya's Black Paintings, horror book covers) and a respected one (silence, temperance, authority, and by extension elegance — judges' and nobles' dress, fashion logos, luxury packaging).
- Black's contrast power is used both to shock (skeleton and death imagery) and to make other colors pop against it (Flyte energy drinks).
- Grey is the most consistently negatively-coded of the four: doubt, melancholy, boredom, ruin and isolation (ash, cobwebs, Guernica), and reads as a lifeless, personality-free color on its own.
- Grey's flatness becomes an asset in two ways: strong tonal contrast within grey can revitalise a composition (Isidro Ferrer, Müller-Brockmann's black-and-white photography), and its conservative neutrality suits elitist or luxury products that want the product itself, not the color, to carry the message (jewelry, perfume).
