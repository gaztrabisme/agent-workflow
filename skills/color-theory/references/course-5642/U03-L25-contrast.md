## Contrast as a compositional principle

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 00:00–01:17

Over this lesson we analyze seven kinds of contrast: tonality, luminosity, temperature, complementarity, simultaneity, quality and quantity.

Contrast is the foundation of every good chromatic result. At first sight it can look like a compositional approach opposed to harmony, but in practice harmony and contrast are two different forms of the same underlying action. We define color contrast as situations where, between two or more colors interacting on a graphic support, there are sensible differences in values that affect how those colors are perceived. You can check this on a book cover designed by Niklaus Troxler.

## Contrast as comparison: context and Itten's principle

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 01:17–02:34

We illustrate a fundamental principle with an example: what is the point of making juice from oranges and mandarins? That is one thing. Making juice from oranges and strawberries is another matter entirely — the pleasant taste of a different drink is a question of judging the contrast between the flavors being mixed. Something similar happens with color.

The opposite of similarity is difference. Within this principle of difference, contrasting always implies a comparison. In general terms, everything we grasp through our senses is grounded in a comparative relationship. The perception of a color depends on the context in which it is perceived. ## Functions of contrast and Johannes Itten

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 02:34–03:59

Among the functions of contrast in graphic design we can list: the need to build attention-grabbing devices, the intention to prioritize zones of significant interest, the possibility of exalting color, the purpose of expressing relationships of space and atmosphere, the ability to convey sensations and feelings, the requirement to provoke physiological reactions, the goal of creating an aesthetic effect, or simply the fact of differentiating.

As already noted, we base ourselves on the approach to color contrast developed by Johannes Itten, painter, designer and professor at the Bauhaus, and a key figure in the history of the study and teaching of color. He was a pioneer in experimenting with color perception and its optical effect, synthesizing his studies on color in the book The Art of Color (*El arte del color*). In this book, for the first time, he developed a theory on the different possible types of contrast produced by the distinct characteristics of colors. He identified seven distinct contrasts: tonality, luminosity, temperature, complementarity, simultaneity, quality and quantity. Understanding how colors behave in combination with others gives you an essential foundation for using color when designing.

## Contrast of tonality

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 03:59–05:05

The contrast of tonality is the simplest of all the color-contrast situations we can observe in a composition. It inevitably arises as soon as we put saturated colors together. The more saturated or pure these colors are, the more intensely the contrast is produced. As examples of this search for intensity and expression through tonal contrast, you can see, on the left, this portrait painted by Matisse, and on the right, Blue and Black Fox by Franz Marc, where the animal appears trapped by the strong effect of the contrast of the near-pure tones surrounding it. In these posters you can observe the use of a tonal contrast that generates a noticeable effect of vitality — energetic, powerful and multicolored as soon as the triad of primary colors is used. Contrast with a white background lowers the luminosity of these colors, while contrast with a black background increases it.

## Contrast of luminosity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 05:05–07:15

Also called light-dark contrast, luminosity contrast represents a fundamental vital experience for the whole of nature. Light and darkness, night and day, light and dark, determine behaviors and are one of the greatest resources of expression. It occurs in the achromatic colors — black, white and gray — and also through the juxtaposition of colors with different degrees of luminosity. This contrast increases the greater the difference in luminosity between the colors.

Luminosity contrast is a resource widely used in art. Equal brightness levels, with no contrast or low light-dark contrast, bring colors to the same level, while a strong light-dark contrast can accentuate the plasticity of a work, since bright colors tend to advance and dark ones tend to recede into the background. In this work by Rembrandt, the light-dark contrast plays an essential role. In Seurat's drawing you can see how light-dark contrast is used to bring out the contours of the figure, creating a sense of body and accentuating three-dimensionality. In these poster examples you can assess the application of luminosity contrasts that, as in the artworks shown before, help to highlight spaces and shapes.

Gray is neutral and inexpressive, and as you can see in the first two posters, on contact with other colors it weakens and softens them, as if it were absorbing their strength. Itten called the color gray a "vampire" (*vampiro*). The use of grays lets you soften combinations of tones that might otherwise be strident. In the photographic poster on the right, the strong light contrast makes the hand stand out, which allows meanings associated with light, or its absence, to be added. For graphic designers, this luminosity contrast is one of the most effective resources when it comes to highlighting legibility between figure and background, as can be the case with typography or signage.

## Contrast of temperature

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 07:15–09:30

Regarding temperature contrast, Itten established that the left half of his color wheel groups the colors generally classed as cool, and the right half the warm ones. According to him, the most extreme cold-warm contrast is created by blue-green colors against red-orange ones. This character of cool and warm colors can also be defined through different contrasting linguistic pairs: shaded–sunny, calming–exciting, distant–close, and so on.

In principle it might seem strange to speak of a sensation of temperature when it comes to the optical perception of colors. To demonstrate this thermal-sensation effect, Itten himself described an experiment he carried out: people who had spent time in a room painted blue-green felt cold at 15 degrees, whereas those same people only felt cold at 11 or 12 degrees in a room painted red-orange. According to this, it could be inferred that blue-green calms the circulation while red-orange activates it.

On the other hand, cold-warm contrast has great importance for producing an impression of space, or a color perspective: the greater the distance between things and our eye, the bluer, and therefore the colder, their color appears to us. In Paul Cézanne's painting you can see a contrast between the coldness of the sky, mountains and sea and the warmth of the houses, which accentuates the sense of spatiality. Temperature contrast accentuates the degree of warmth and coldness of forms, as you can see in Van Gogh's painting. In these poster examples you can distinguish this essential principle of temperature contrast. Colors reinforce their warm or cool value when placed next to contrasting colors in terms of temperature value. With this analysis of the meanings of three graphic identity brands we want to help you appreciate the conscious use of color-temperature contrast to evoke ideas associated with water and fire, movement and stability, or energy and nature.

## Contrast of complementarity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 09:30–10:23

Complementarity contrast is perhaps one of the most widely used chromatic devices. Given their luminosity characteristics, complementary colors reinforce each other, establishing a balanced or harmonic relationship, as we saw in the previous lesson. In the work on the left, the contrast occurs between the violet background and the orange hair. In the work on the right, the girls' red dresses contrast strongly with the greenish tones of the setting; in this second painting, tones arising from mixing these two complementary colors, green and red, are also used around the scene, producing a chromatic reinforcement effect that unifies and balances the composition. Through these posters you can clearly see one of the main effects of combining complementary colors: it lets us perceive the color in the sharpest possible way.

## Contrast of simultaneity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 10:23–13:14

We move on to simultaneous contrast, a chromatic situation based on the fact that a given color diminishes or intensifies its luminous intensity, also modifying its tone and saturation, depending on the colors surrounding it. Simultaneous contrast describes the synchronic interaction of adjacent color areas. This interaction between colors results in an optical flooding. The eye seeks out a color's complementary in order to create a totality of color; if this color impression is missing, perception produces it as a tinge in an adjacent color. Such subjectively perceived colors are called induced colors (*colores inducidos*).

Let's try to explain this better with some examples. In this image you see a red skull and an orange one, don't you? Well, no — both skulls are the same red-orange color, we are not tricking you. Another example: do you see spheres of various colors? Well, no — all the spheres are the same color. As we noted earlier, our eye, faced with a given color, simultaneously demands its complementary. In this next example, if you stare fixedly at the black central point for a few seconds, you will see a green color effect appear where the circles disappear. This new color that appears almost magically is called an afterimage (*posimagen*), and is caused by the adaptation or fatigue of our eyes' receptor mechanisms. It produces the trace of that image transformed into its negative — that is, the opposite sensory experience.

## Contrast of quality

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 13:14–13:56

The qualitative notion of color is grounded in its degree of purity or saturation. Qualitative contrast, also called intensity contrast, is produced by the opposition between saturated, luminous colors and dull, lusterless colors. As you can see in these two paintings, the strongest contrast occurs when combining saturated colors with grays — that is, neutrals. As an effect of this contrast, we perceive saturated tones as more vivid and less saturated ones as more muted. As a resource in graphic design, this qualitative contrast can be useful when we need to highlight some shapes in vivid tones by placing them next to colors of low luminosity or with reduced purity.

## Contrast of quantity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L25 · 13:56–16:15

As the last contrast situation we will show you, we have quantitative contrast, a type of contrast that arises from considering colors as dimensional values. For example, yellow tends to look bigger, since it gives the sensation of expanding. Red is static, tending toward balance in itself. Blue, for its part, gives the sensation of concentrating — it seems to contract. Combining colors in proportions that match their dimensional capacities lets us balance them — that is, harmonize them.

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, one of the most influential intellectual writers of the 18th and 19th centuries, was the first to establish luminosity values for colors and translate these values into surface dimensions, creating a new concept of the color wheel. According to Goethe, one part orange corresponds to two parts blue, and one part yellow to approximately three parts violet; red and green are in equal, balanced proportions.

For example, you can see in these posters a demonstration of harmonic color balance by quantity: yellow occupies approximately a third of the space of its complementary violet; in the center, orange occupies half the space of its complementary blue; green and red occupy the same space. Quantitative contrast is neutralized by the use of harmonious quantities, giving rise to calming aesthetic effects. However, when a composition uses quantitative proportions different from the harmonious ones — that is, when one color dominates — a strongly expressive, standout effect is achieved. The minority color, so to speak and in Itten's own words, is "in danger," and defends itself in its own way by becoming more luminous and energetic.

Having reviewed the compositional forms of harmony and contrast, in the next lesson Leire will begin to reflect on the functions and variables of color.

### Key points (derived)

- Itten identified seven types of color contrast: tonality, luminosity, temperature, complementarity, simultaneity, quality and quantity.
- Contrast and harmony are not opposites but two forms of the same compositional action; perception of any color depends on the colors around it.
- Gray weakens and softens neighboring colors (Itten's "vampire"); it is a tool for calming otherwise strident combinations.
- Simultaneous contrast produces induced colors and afterimages: the eye demands a color's complementary and will generate it subjectively when absent.
- Goethe assigned proportional "weights" to complementary pairs for balance: orange 1 : blue 2, yellow 1 : violet ~3, red : green 1:1.
