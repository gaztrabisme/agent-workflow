## Defining an emblem: a conventional, coded sign

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L40 · 00:22–00:50

An emblem can be defined as a figure, with its color, used in a conventional way to symbolically represent an idea or a thing — a definition that treats it as a sign constituted as a representation of something else. An emblem is a conventional sign that has been coded for social use.

## Emblematic color sits between the symbolic, the cultural and the social

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L40 · 00:50–01:35

Signal color (*color señalético*) is especially sign-based, while emblematic color has to be discussed as something more symbolic in character. In emblematic color we can recognize a practical, that is utilitarian, symbolism — one that results from a code embedded within a society as a whole. Emblematic color is the sign-color that keeps its meaning through tradition: a color raised to the status of emblem by its cultural reach, for an essentially social use. It is very widespread, and can be found woven into everyday culture alongside a diversity of utilitarian signs.

## What counts as emblematic: corporate, national and institutional colors

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L40 · 01:52–02:44

What colors, then, could be considered emblematic? Corporate colors, like the red of Coca-Cola, which work both to identify and differentiate the brand and to make it memorable — emblematic color is a very powerful visual sign of a corporation. The colors of national flags are emblematic colors. So are the colors of the five Olympic rings, and the institutional colors of uniforms — incidentally, some of these colors have a clearly signal-based origin but, through social use, become emblematic of the police, the military, and so on. Also the colors of sports teams or clubs. Or the red of the Red Cross (the transcript literally repeats "roja... y roja" here) — an example of how an emblematic color is, in many cases, at the same time a signal color.

## Emblematic colors of social movements, and a summary

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L40 · 02:44–03:21

## Color as a brand's most durable, most recognizable sign

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L40 · 04:56–05:26

This identifying, associative function of color with respect to a brand or product is so strong that when a redesign takes place, color is normally the last thing to be modified, because users recognize a brand above all through its chromatic emblem — it is the easiest thing to associate and remember. Without doubt, in a context of corporate communication, color can be said to be the most recognizable sign there is.

## Red Cross: from signal color to institutional emblem

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L40 · 11:25–12:05

We mentioned earlier that the color of the Red Cross identity sign is an emblematic color, since it identifies a worldwide humanitarian-aid organization, becoming, through its use, an emblem. It is the mark chosen by voluntary aid societies to make visible, from a distance, those who were helping the wounded in war. It also represents the colors of the Swiss flag in reverse, as a tribute to the organization's founders and to Switzerland's neutral stance. It is an emblem that arose as a signal color and, through institutionalized use, became an emblematic color.

## Closing: the end of the classification, and what comes next

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L40 · 14:56–15:15

With this we have reached the end of the classification of the functions and variables of color in graphic design — moving from the denotative and connotative functions to the sign-based function. In the next lesson, the teachers will show their own projects.

### Key points (derived)

- Emblematic color is the third variable of sign-based color: a conventional, coded, practical-symbolism color that keeps its meaning through cultural tradition and institutionalized social use, distinct from schematic color (flat, autonomous) and signal color (a functional warning/instruction color) even though it often overlaps with both.
- Corporate identity leans on color as the single strongest, most durable recognition device — often outlasting a logotype or wordmark through a redesign (Penguin, Movistar/Orange/Vodafone, Heineken's "Piensa en verde", Ferrari, Correos, which dropped its logotype but kept and expanded its yellow).
- National flag colors recur as an origin-marking device across very different design contexts: general national branding (Russia, Cuba, Catalonia, Spain), place-based marks (Madrid Sonríe, the Galician seafood mark), airline liveries, Europe/UN/UNESCO's blue, national-themed posters, and food packaging (Italian pasta and pizza, Norwegian seafood, Miller Lite's stacked-flag cases).
- A coded color can become emblematic purely through institutionalized social use, independent of any flag: Eusko Label's Basque colors and the "etiqueta negra" (black label) convention for premium consumer goods both work this way.
- The Olympic rings and the Red Cross are treated as the clearest cases of color that is emblematic and signal-based at once: the rings' six colors are chosen so that every national flag shares at least one of them, the Montreal Olympic Park mark echoes the 1976 Games identity, and the Red Cross emblem began as a signal (visible from a distance on the battlefield) built from Switzerland's reversed flag colors before becoming a full institutional emblem, reused in a Haiti-earthquake poster and in the "SOS" body-cream packaging.
- Social movements have their own emblematic colors: the rainbow for gay pride (adopted for a few days each year by major corporations, and by an ironic "cure for homophobia" product), and violet for feminism (recurring across book covers and in the Global Fund for Women's identity, where an equals sign hides in the negative space of a capital E).
