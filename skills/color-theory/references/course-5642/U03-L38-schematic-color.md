## What schematic color is

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L38 · 00:00–01:18

This lesson covers the first variable of color's sign function (*función sígnica*): schematic color (*color esquemático*). In this variable color is used with value in itself — as a sign color (*color signo*).

First, what is a schema (*esquema*)? A schema is the mental representation of something material or immaterial, or of a process; it can also be the summary or synthesis of something, attending only to its most significant features. Schematic (*esquemático*) is what belongs or relates to a schema — something that interprets a subject without perceiving its nuances.

So schematic color is no longer a realistic color, a figurative color with all its nuances. Instead, in this chromatic variable, color becomes flat (*color-plano*), pure, autonomous: it concentrates on the essential in order to signify — to function as a sign (*color-signo*).

## Color freed from iconic representation

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L38 · 01:18–02:44

When color is used at its purest, with no nuance or gradation, it appears simply as surface — as in this work by Wassily Kandinsky (captions: *Basil y Kandinsky*), a study of how different color combinations are perceived.

Just as shapes can be synthesized into basic geometric forms, as in this work by Piet Mondrian, colors can be schematized until they recover their value as pure chromatic substance — the opposite point from iconic representation. Color becomes an autonomous element, independent of figurative representation, as in these works by Mark Rothko (captions: *Matt Rocco*).

Color frees itself from the iconic representation of things; it no longer depends on the image represented, as this painting by one of the great exponents of abstract art, Fahrelnissa Zeid (captions: *Farel Nyssa Zeid*), shows.

Schematic color no longer serves iconic fidelity to heighten sensuality through hyperrealism, nor to reproduce things as they are. Nor does it serve an exalted, expressionist or fanciful iconicity, nor does it try to act in the connotative register of psychological perception or color symbolism — though it can sometimes carry that kind of interpretation too.

## Schematic color as an autonomous graphic element

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L38 · 02:44–03:28

Stripped of chromatic nuance, tonal subtlety and half-tones, schematic color becomes a graphic element for coloring surfaces or graphic marks in the visual message, serving whatever objective the designer sets for each specific project.

This flat graphic nature — simplification and concentration on the essential — lets color function autonomously for different purposes within graphic design. Like typography, or graphic resources such as the point, the line, texture or pattern, color is now just another graphic element within a piece, there to signify and communicate visually.

## Corporate identity built on open data: Open Knowledge

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L38 · 04:48–05:58

Here's another use of schematic color: the identity project for an organization that promotes full openness of information from governments and other bodies worldwide. This identity is based directly on one of the foundation's main projects, the Global Open Data Index, which uses bar charts to show how open different countries are with the information they manage: an especially open country shows a mostly green bar, an especially closed one mostly red.

Johnson Banks synthesized this graphic system into a circular mark, placing all the red bars at the center and the green ones on the outside — suggesting planet Earth, while also evoking an eye and the "O" of "Open". It reads as a very clear diagram style that quickly makes us think of data.

## Schematic color in brand differentiation

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L38 · 05:58–06:38

Schematic color also appears in many corporate identity marks that have nothing to do with charts or other so-called functional supports: it's the color found in purely graphic inventions, in most cases aimed simply at standing out from the competition. That's the case with the corporate colors of certain financial institutions, or, for example, mobile-phone service brands. As already noted, schematic color is more common than naturalistic color in identity marks — because of its tendency toward synthesis and the need for identification and memorability, pure, concentrated, synthesized schematic color favors recognition and recall.

## Case study: Rotterdam Architecture Biennale identity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L38 · 06:38–07:33

In this visual identity for the International Architecture Biennale Rotterdam, color is applied with absolute freedom of abstraction; its aim is essentially plastic. The project addresses climate change and design, developing a whole series of graphic supports in which black text on a white background is partly hidden by irregular, shifting chromatic masses — flat, arbitrary color masses that generate different shapes around the informative text. The shapes generated by the color and the typographic composition are placed in contrast, in conflict, calling for change. Color here has constructive power: it carries its own meaning, capable of building the communicative graphic language of an idea by itself.

## Editorial design: abstract book covers

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L38 · 07:33–08:45

The autonomy of schematic color also shows up in editorial design — for example, in abstract book covers built around optical play, harmonies, contrasts and so on. As already noted, schematic color is generally applied for a range of purposes: pursuing plastic effects, creating highlights, balancing colors, optimizing typographic legibility, and more. Keep in mind that schematic color's communicative effectiveness always depends on the designer's expressive intent, the intended audience, and the content and style of the message.

This editorial project is the cover for the book *El trayecto de un anhelo* (*The Course of a Longing*), designed by Zenlitros Metrocadrado. The book's outer covers represent the skin of a building, reproducing the modulation of its façade through vertical folds; the color refers to the contrast between materials, and repeating the covers suggests the rhythm of the elevation.

## Case study: Waldo Trommler Paints

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L38 · 11:32–12:31

This flat graphic nature of color — abstract, simplified, concentrated — turns it into a plastic resource of near-infinite manageability. But it stays flat color throughout, spreading across the surface of the graphic support, as in this paint-packaging project.

The paint brand Waldo Trommler (captions: *Waldo Tromler*) set out to stand out and be different in a competitive market. To do that, the brand was built without corporate colors: it simply places common iconic elements over a wide range of bright hues. You could call the color here fanciful, yet it isn't entirely — this use of schematic color doesn't try to distort the reality we know, but to generate a graphic, plastic, aesthetic, communicative sense: color as pure sign.

### Key points (derived)

- Schematic color (*color esquemático*) is flat, pure and autonomous: it drops realistic nuance to work as a sign, not as a depiction.
- It appears in functional graphics (diagrams, infographics), corporate and brand identity, editorial design, brand marks and logos, posters, and packaging.
- Because it favors identification and memorability, schematic color is more common than naturalistic color in identity marks.
- The form doesn't need to be abstract for the color to count as schematic — flat, pure color applied over a recognizable shape (Fukuda's posters, Waldo Trommler's packaging) still counts.
- It sets up the next two sign-function variables: signal color (traffic signs) and emblematic color (national emblems).
