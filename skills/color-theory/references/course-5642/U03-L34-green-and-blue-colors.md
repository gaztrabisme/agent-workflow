### Nature, freshness and the senses

Green is the color between blue and yellow in the visible spectrum. It is the color that can express nature and the environment, evokes the peace and tranquility of the countryside, and is considered the calmest and most sedating color. Green has been a traditional resource in art for creating specific emotions, not only for imitating nature.

### Luck, money and instability

A question: do you know why casino card tables are green? Green once had the particularity of being a chemically unstable color. It wasn't very hard to obtain, because plants, leaves, roots and flowers can serve as green dyes — but stabilizing the color green was another matter entirely. Green's symbolism of luck has been built entirely around this idea of instability: it represents everything that moves, changes and varies. So green is the color of hope, chance, gambling, fate and fortune. In the West, green is considered a symbol of luck.

### The divine, royalty and religion

Blue has traditionally been a symbol of the spiritual and the infinite. Through its relationship with the blue sky, it became a symbol of the divine, of what is higher. In many religions, gods live in the sky, which is why blue is the color associated with gods — the Egyptian god Amun even has blue skin so he can fly through the sky unseen. The Hindu god Krishna, or the god Rama, also communicate their celestial origin through this color.

![5642 · U3 · L34 · 11:19](frames/U03L34-F1119.jpg)

On screen: "Dios egipcio Amón" ("Egyptian god Amun"); "Dios Krishna hindú" ("Hindu god Krishna"); "Dios Rama hindú" ("Hindu god Rama").  
Shows: The Egyptian god Amun with blue skin and a feathered crown, beside blue-skinned depictions of the Hindu gods Krishna and Rama.

You may understand the use of blue in these next projects through the saying that royalty has "blue blood." Some claim this comes from the fact that, in the past, members of royal houses were never exposed to the sun and so had very pale skin, through which their veins showed bluish. In reality, this claim comes from a mistranslation of the Latin phrase "caelesti sanguine ortam" ("born of celestial blood"), referring to the divine, heavenly origin claimed by descendants of the Roman emperor Augustus. "Celestial blood" was translated as "sky-blue blood," from which the term "blue blood" derived.

![5642 · U3 · L34 · 11:49](frames/U03L34-F1149.jpg)

In this symbolic sense of blue, here is a visual-identity project made by "Sagn Mesteir" (as heard in the captions) for New York's Jewish Museum. The essential color of this project is blue, inspired by tekhelet, a color mentioned in the Hebrew Bible and very important in Jewish culture. Its origins trace back to the semi-precious blue stone lapis lazuli, imported from Afghanistan and associated in Mesopotamian, Egyptian and Canaanite cultures with the heavens, the dwelling place of the gods.

One of the most important laws of Judaism commands that Israelites tie threads of blue tekhelet into the fringes (tzitzit) of their prayer shawls as a reminder of heaven, of God and of his commandments — which is why this color is considered divine.

Until the 12th and 13th centuries, blue is absent from Catholic worship. At that point, for the first time in the West, skies began to be painted blue — before that they were black, red, white or even gold. From the 12th century on, the Virgin appears in images covered with a blue mantle or dress, promoting a symbolic association of blue with what is considered most elevated, most important.

However, once blue's symbolic value shifted toward power, the values swapped, and blue became masculine.

### Key points (derived)

- Green sits between blue and yellow; it is the calmest, most sedating color and the traditional color of nature, freshness (forest, moss, pine) and the senses (menthol, mint).
- Green identifies museums, science and organic/ecological brands; it is used deliberately by companies (BP, McDonald's, Teknion, AIS, Parking) to signal an ecological or sustainable repositioning, sometimes specifically to break from a sector's usual colors (fuel = fire tones; junk food = red).
- Green is the color of pharmacies (with the cross), of freedom (open country) and of hope (things turning green again); in the West it also symbolizes luck and money, rooted historically in its chemical instability as a pigment.
- Green has a negative pole too: greed, envy, inexperience, toxicity, decay and the devil — the same instability that gives it luck also gives it an uncanny, ambiguous reputation.
- Blue is discreet, spacious, calm and controlled; it is strongly linked to water, hygiene, technology (59% of tech brands use it) and finance/banking, where its unremarkable, consensus-building quality is an asset.
- Blue is historically tied to the divine and the sky (Amun, Krishna, Rama; the Virgin Mary from the 12th–13th century on) and, from there, to royalty ("blue blood," a mistranslation of "celestial blood") and to institutions wanting authority and stability (American Express, hospitals, insurers).
- Blue vs. pink gender coding is a historical reversal: blue was feminine in the Middle Ages (via the Virgin), red was masculine (power, war); blue only became masculine once it took on connotations of power.
