## A functional classification of color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L26 · 00:00–00:55

In this lesson we present a color classification that aims to identify and characterize the possible functions and variables of color.

As already mentioned, graphic design implies the visual organization of content through the choice and combination of iconographic, typographic and chromatic signs. We want to stress once again that designing consists of building a laborious web of relationships of meaning that are effective for communication. Graphic designers are, in a sense, controllers of signs, and it is very important to know how to steer the meanings that a color, as a sign, can generate in a receiver's mind, always within a given communicative context.

## Color as signifier: signs beyond mere representation

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L26 · 00:55–01:40

Before anything else, it is clear that we need to recognize color as a property inherent to things in the world, to the things around us, because color does not exist in a pure state. Beyond its value for representing things, color is capable of acting as a signifier in the world of graphic design — not only as an iconographic signifier, but as a psychological, symbolic and emblematic one. The perception of the world is one thing, and graphic perception, in essence something quite different, is another. That is, designing and visualizing ideas means using colors, and these do not always relate to the colors as we see them in reality; they can instead be applied with expressive or communicative intentions.

## Meaning is not intrinsic to color: the role of context

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L26 · 01:40–04:06

A color does not always create the same meaning. That would imply that a color's meaning is intrinsic to it, and clearly this is not the case. The meaning of a color is not found in the color itself; it is generated in the receiver's mind, always determined by the influence of context. For this reason, we cannot say, for example, that the color red means the same thing in Coca-Cola as it does in signage and symbols for medical first aid, on book covers about pornography or the devil, on a box of chocolates, on the label of a red tea, or even in the pictogram marking a fire hose.

We are often used to reading, in the name of color psychology, sweeping statements like "orange means energy, joy, vitality" and so on. It can mean those things — it can — but it always depends on the context. Color generates its own meaning always on a pragmatic level. Sometimes this meaning will be psychological, other times it will be inherited from a given culture's tradition, and other times it will be a conventional code created for operational use.

## A three-part classification: denotative, connotative and sign functions

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L26 · 04:06–05:14

To identify and characterize all these possible functions of color in graphic design, we propose a classification system, based with some variations on the classification Joan Costa put forward in his book Diseñar para los ojos (Designing for the Eyes). As you can see, three basic functions can generally be established: the denotative function, the connotative function and the sign function. This is, as you can observe, a classification of a semiotic nature that tries to theoretically order the various applications of color in graphic communication — an analytical approach to a reality as complex as how colors function in visual communication through graphic design. The main purpose of this classificatory proposal is to offer theoretical reflections, an ordering, and frameworks of understanding so we can grasp how chromatic meaning is established in graphic design.

Before moving on, we think it is important to clarify that this conceptual scheme does not aim to limit each category — quite the opposite: these are not meant to be closed drawers, because each defined category overflows, in some way, its own limits, which are obviously artificial and theoretical. In a single graphic piece, color can perform several of the functions proposed here.

## The denotative function: the world of representation

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L26 · 05:14–07:40

Once that is clarified, we can see how the denotative function of color is carried out by those colors that can be valued for their capacity for figurative representation. It is applied and perceived as a natural property of a realistic form. The variables of denotative color are defined in relation to the image — that is, the color associated with greater or lesser realism for identifying the elements represented, according to our empirical perception of the world.

In its denotative use we can distinguish four types of color according to their degree of iconicity relative to the figure represented: naturalistic color, exalted color, expressionist color and fanciful color. In all four gradations of this first functional category, the iconicity of the form is always recognized — an iconicity of form that can sometimes be more figurative and other times more abstract, but there is always a recognition of the figure represented.

Naturalistic color is the realistic expression of color that appears when the color is, or aims to be, faithful to reality. It will be hyperrealistic when the color used aims to show an image more real than reality itself, and realistic when it aims to represent the real as it is. Exalted color remains an iconic color like naturalistic color, but here much brighter and more saturated; through the exaggeration of color it aims to represent a prettier, happier, more stimulating reality. Expressionist color, while keeping a relative iconicity, is concerned with changing the effect of reality; this kind of color tries to be more expressive in the image than in reality itself, and to do so it operates by altering color and light, generating a kind of theatricalization — though expressionist color does not go so far as to fully contradict the color things have in reality. That contradiction, on the other hand, is exactly what fanciful color does: this last variable of the denotative function proposes a chromatic manipulation that produces unreal images contradicting the color of the figures represented. Here color abandons the real as its iconic reference, and can generate rhetorical, cultural and psychological meanings, in both its imaginary and its arbitrary form.

## The connotative function: psychological and symbolic evocations

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L26 · 07:40–09:10

If the denotative function of color refers to a use that helps identify the figure represented, the second functional category we cover here, the connotative function, points to values that figure does not itself contain. Precisely because of its capacity for connotation, color can carry values that go beyond the object represented itself. In this way, connotation triggers internal sensations of an intersubjective kind, both in its psychological variable and in its symbolic variable.

Psychological color creates states of mind, evoking sensations and feelings in a generalized way in the unconscious of a group belonging to the same culture. The representation no longer needs to be iconic, since psychological color operates both in the environment around us and in every colored graphic manifestation. Psychological color is, therefore, the color that activates part of our unconscious through the projection of colors onto the psyche. Within this consideration of color, we should mention the representation of symbolic codes that correspond to particular societies and are sustained by tradition. Unlike psychological color, symbolic color constitutes a coding that establishes a kind of collective psychology, becoming a cultural phenomenon.

## The sign function: functionality and coding

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L26 · 09:10–10:58

This functionalized symbolic variable, together with fanciful color which led us toward abstraction, brings us to the sign function of color. The sign function is a category belonging to the world of functionality and to the world of codes. It is an abstract color with a value in itself, since it frees itself from the references of our empirical view of the world — color here moves away from images that imitate reality. Color is now an autonomous substance, a true sign that signifies by itself.

The three sign variables we will distinguish within this function are schematic color, signal-like color and emblematic color. With the first variable, schematic color, we properly enter the world of graphic design: here color operates with real graphic value and with absolute freedom of abstraction. Schematic color concentrates on the essential in order to function as a true sign — one more sign in the graphic discourse, with a value equivalent to iconographic and typographic signs.

In the second chromatic variable of this category, signal-like color, some aspects that define schematic color are also involved; it too is a flat, autonomous color, a sign-color, but it has the particularity of also functioning as a signal-color. Signal-like color functions as a sign-color because it implies a meaning, even if arbitrary, and as a signal-color because it conveys an instantaneous, immediate luminous sensation. In the last variable of the sign function we consider emblematic color, a variable that is schematic color because it is purely a sign, but has the singularity of being more symbolic — that is, it operates with a utilitarian symbolism.

## Degree of iconicity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L26 · 10:58–12:21

Now, to close this lesson on a functional classification of color, we want to better explain a concept touched on earlier that seems worth clarifying: the degree of iconicity of color. Just as a decreasing scale of iconicity is established for images, color can also follow this same law of decreasing iconicity. In this way, the denotative function of color covers all the figurative variables of the image; as you can see in this diagram, its variations run from the most iconic to the most abstract, starting from the most naturalistic to the most exalted, and from the most expressionist to the most fanciful.

If, in the representation of forms in images, the opposite pole to maximum iconicity is the functional sign — abstract signs, alphabetic signs, numerals, musical notation, and so on — then in the realm of color this corresponds to the pure chromatic sign, whether in its schematic aspect or in its signal-like or emblematic function.

### Key points (derived)

- Color has no meaning on its own; meaning is generated in the receiver's mind and depends entirely on context — the same red means different things in Coca-Cola, a first-aid kit, or a fire-hose pictogram.
- The classification (adapted from Joan Costa's Diseñar para los ojos) has three functions: denotative (representation), connotative (psychological/symbolic evocation) and sign (functionality/coding) — the categories overlap, and one piece can use several at once.
- Denotative color has four variables ordered by iconicity: naturalistic (faithful to reality), exalted (brighter, more saturated), expressionist (alters color/light without fully contradicting reality) and fanciful (contradicts real color entirely).
- Connotative color splits into psychological (individual/collective mood) and symbolic (culturally coded, tradition-based).
- Sign color splits into schematic (pure abstract graphic sign), signal-like (sign + immediate light signal) and emblematic (schematic sign with utilitarian symbolism).
- Color follows a decreasing scale of iconicity, from the fully figurative (naturalistic) to the purely abstract chromatic sign (schematic/signal/emblematic), mirroring the same scale used for images in general.
