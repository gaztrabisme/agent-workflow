## What is expressionist color?

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L29 · 00:08–01:34

This lesson introduces the expressionist variable of color, where color is used to introduce a kind of dramatization.

"Expressionist" points to the expression of emotions as against the faithful depiction of reality — a term associated with the early-20th-century artistic and literary movement characterized by the intensity of its expression of feelings and sensations. In the study of color in graphic design, this term implies a significant chromatic alteration, which can be of hue, saturation, or even luminosity. If exalted color exaggerated the color things have in reality, expressionist color alters or transforms that color to generate a dramatization, to change the effect of reality — but without fully contradicting the color things actually have in the real world.

Naturalistic color tries to stay faithful to how things look. Exalted color can give these aspects of reality more prominence through exaggeration. Both of these chromatic variants appeal to optical sensation. Expressionist color, by contrast, has a more rhetorical character, appealing to the cultural, the psychological, the emotional.

## Van Gogh and Munch: dramatizing through color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L29 · 01:34–02:36

In this work by Van Gogh titled *The Starry Night*, color here aims to lend the image a kind of theatricality, to make it more expressive, more moving, more sensitive. It looks less to reality, as naturalistic color does, and more to the expression of the image itself.

![5642 · U3 · L29 · 01:54](frames/U03L29-F0154.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: Van Gogh's *The Starry Night*: swirling blue sky with bright stars and moon over a village with a cypress tree in the foreground.

One of the most representative artists of Expressionism was Edvard Munch. This capacity to express psychological emotions or inner sensations appears very clearly in his works. Here we show *Anxiety* and *The Scream*, in which that dramatization of color to express emotion is clearly visible. His whole body of work centers on dissecting the most tragic human feelings, placing them as central themes. The tremendous psychological and expressive charge captured in Munch's works is reinforced by using strong, pure colors combined with some murkier or greyer ones — a use of color that expresses an existential anguish toward life.

## Gauguin, and "global impression" in advertising

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L29 · 02:36–03:27

In Paul Gauguin's works, the emotions underlying the expressionist colors he uses are more positive than Munch's. We could say that expressionist color operates on the image by giving it a global impression, an overall impression, generating a mood that makes the image's meaning go beyond a partial understanding of the figures shown in it.

## Book covers: harmonies of grey and low luminosity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L29 · 03:27–05:19

On this book cover, the color used neither aims to be faithful to reality nor is it exaggerated to be brighter and more attractive. The color tries to express the emotion of the situation, a dramatic, theatrical, moving atmosphere in keeping with what happens in the scene depicted. A chromatic harmony is generated here, making the colors lose their chromatic strength and turning them into greys of different shades. This harmony of greys emphasizes the seriousness of what the cover image expresses.

![5642 · U3 · L29 · 04:00](frames/U03L29-F0400.jpg)

On screen: "NOBODY IS EVER MISSING — A NOVEL — Catherine Lacey"  
Shows: A book cover repeating an illustration of a woman's face three times in warm pink and dark blue-green tones, shown flat and as a 3D book.

In this first book cover, given its high chromatic luminosity, and in the second cover through low luminosity — coming very close to an achromatic expression — both create scenarios of an overall over-expressiveness that changes how the whole image is perceived.

Expressionist color remains an iconic color, as in the naturalistic and exalted variables: color still represents the forms in reality, but it doesn't affect the naturalistic form-color relationship of things as much. This chromatic treatment in search of expressiveness can be found on many book covers reflecting content about suspense, horror, violence, or themes of love, melancholy, hatred. After all, book covers often aim to generate a mood and an emotion that matches the book's content. Color, its hue and its luminosity, are very valuable resources for communicating sensations, whether intimate, romantic, aggressive, and so on.

## Horror film posters and the dimension of fear

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L29 · 05:19–06:25

As shown earlier, low-luminosity harmony is formed by colors that share the same or a similar degree of darkness, and it is a chromatic resource widely used, for example, in horror-film posters. These posters present an overall darkness, sometimes broken by a bright contrast — a tenebrist harmony with a strong dramatic effect. As we'll see later, when discussing the connotative function of the color black, one of the meanings attributed to this color in relation to darkness has to do with humanity's deepest, most intimate fears. Through this color, remember that in certain contexts we spontaneously think of negative things — fears, shadows, and therefore death. These posters are also examples of drawing on an expressionist dimension of color, since they alter reality's colors to generate emotion. One of them is even inspired by Munch's *The Scream*, the artist we've already discussed in this lesson.

## Adolfo Hohenstein's theatrical posters

## A broader, more emotional visual language

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L29 · 07:42–08:26

This chromatic variable of denotative color alters or transforms the real color of things in many different ways to generate a new expression, changing the effect of reality. Through this chromatic variant, the intention is to express a new reality, a world of heightened sensations, more expressive, more emotional. It uses an increasingly rhetorical discourse, a discourse more open to new meanings — a chromatic discourse more open to a more suggestive, broader reality. Expressionist color generates more encompassing images, extending the possibility of generating more emotion.

## Blue: book covers and Picasso's Blue Period

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L29 · 08:26–09:00

That sensation is also conveyed through color in this work by Pablo Picasso, painted during his Blue Period — a time when the artist saw the harsh side of life: misery, loneliness, poverty, despair, melancholy — and wanted those feelings expressed in his paintings. To do so, he predominantly used the color blue as an expressive device to accentuate these emotions.

## Patty Núñez's rainforest chocolate packaging, after Rousseau

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L29 · 09:38–10:38

In these illustrations used by Patty Núñez for chocolate-bar packaging, the chromatic treatment helps generate landscapes evoking the exotic. This is a range of chocolates made with cacaos that have fruity and spiced aromas, which suggest the exotic when tasted. The design takes this last word as its communication concept, naming the range "Selváticas" (Jungle) and using jungle illustrations to add that exotic touch. Daytime, more lit landscapes are used for the milk chocolates, and nighttime, less lit ones for the dark chocolates. The illustrations are by Eva López and are based on the style of the jungle paintings by the artist Henri Rousseau. The color harmonies, using different greyish shades, lose their chromatic strength in order to evoke the exotic mood typical of Rousseau's paintings.

## Closing: toward fantasy color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L29 · 11:16–11:33

With the expressionist variant of color, we begin to move away from iconic color. And with fantasy color, which comes next, color is extracted from things entirely. With both of these chromatic variants, we're moving closer to the world of connotations. Having covered how expressionist color works, Leire will now explain the fantasy color variable.

### Key points (derived)

- Expressionist color alters a thing's hue, saturation or luminosity to dramatize an image, without fully breaking from the object's real color — a middle point between naturalistic fidelity and exalted exaggeration.
- It works through an overall, global impression rather than through the accurate reading of individual parts, appealing to cultural and psychological association rather than optical accuracy.
- Low-luminosity, grey-toned harmonies recur across this lesson's dark or serious examples (book covers, horror posters) to signal seriousness, mystery or fear; high-luminosity, pastel harmonies (the Audi ad, Upstream cans) signal elegance or positivity instead.
- Recurring reference artists: Van Gogh, Munch (anguish, fear), and Gauguin (more positive emotion) — plus historical poster design (Adolfo Hohenstein) and contemporary packaging (Matsu wine, Selváticas chocolate, Upstream water).
