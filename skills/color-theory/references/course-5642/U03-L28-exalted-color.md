## What is exalted color?

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L28 · 00:08–01:21

This lesson looks at the second variable of denotative color: exalted color (*color exaltado*). It is a color that, while still iconic, is exaggerated.

To exalt (*exaltar*) means, by dictionary definition, to raise something up, to enhance someone's standing, or to intensify a feeling. Applied to color, exalted color is iconic color that has been highlighted, enlivened, raised: more saturated than the faithful naturalistic color covered in the previous lesson. In short, exalted color is an exaggeration of iconic color.

In color theory, saturation can be defined as the intensity of a specific hue. A highly saturated color looks more vivid and intense, while a less saturated one looks more washed-out and grey. Saturation is therefore a quantitative matter — more "quantity", so to speak, of color. With this chromatic intensity, images surpass reality itself in attraction, satisfying eyes that seek an exultant chromaticism.

## Gauguin and Matisse: exalting a scene's own colors

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L28 · 01:21–02:08

We can see this, for example, in many works by Paul Gauguin, who through chromatic force sought to represent a prettier, happier, brighter reality. The elements he depicts keep the color they actually have, but shown in an exalted way. In this exaltation of things' own colors, the colors are denser, purer, more luminous and more contrasted in the depiction than in reality — as can be verified in this work by Henri Matisse titled *Music* (*Música*). In one of his most famous works, titled *The Dance* (*La danza*), the exalted, vivid and intense colors are one of the painting's unquestionable protagonists, conveying power, energy, dynamism, joy, fullness.

## Exalted red: film and book covers

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L28 · 02:08–03:02

The exaltation of chromaticism responds to a need created by the strong competitiveness of the visual communication world. At this point in a film you can see a kind of exaggeration of the red of blood, to accentuate the drama of the scene. It's clear that the red of the blood here is not a natural color but an artificialized one; to give this color greater prominence, it has been surrounded with white, to add more drama to what is happening. This saturation of the red of blood can be seen almost as a commonplace in graphic communication. It seems that things around us, blood included, can look more attractive, more appealing, by exalting their colors.

Because of this stimulating effect, the exalted resource of color is used on many book covers, which seek to draw attention through this color variable. As you can see, this color has a density, chromatic purity and sharpness that faithful naturalistic reproduction cannot achieve.

## Posters and advertising intensify color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L28 · 03:02–04:27

This stimulating effect of color is even more evident with posters. Because of a poster's persuasive nature and its own characteristics as a communication medium, it often uses this chromatic variable. In these two examples we can see how the graphic designer has retouched the photographs, intensifying the purity of the colors. In these two posters designed by Tomás Boulaski this use of color can be seen: the reds have been saturated so the posters feel more striking, more powerful, in keeping with the images' message.

Very clear examples of the exaggeration of figurative color are undoubtedly found in advertising imagery. In these two ads the saturated iconic color is shown — that is, color pushed to a greater degree of strength. Reality thereby becomes something more radiant, more lucid, more intentionally real. Where naturalistic color sought a harmonious impression of the real, exalted color raises every chromatic tone to make that reality more colorful, more spectacular and more attractive to our eyes.

## Pictograms and brand marks in flat, saturated color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L28 · 04:53–05:51

Posters generally answer the search for greater visual impact, the exaggeration and artificialization of color being a very important graphic resource for heightening enthusiasm. This exalted chromatic treatment gives more predominance, more prominence to color in its relationship with form. With graphic brand marks, when an iconic color is used it is normally presented in an exaggerated, saturated way. As explained in the previous lesson, it is hard to find an identity mark in naturalistic color, keeping all the nuances, tones and chiaroscuro of what it represents; because of their tendency toward simplicity and the need to be reproducible across different media and conditions, they are usually flat, solid colors. The bull is black, a tulip is orange, an ear and the sole of a foot are orange-toned — as we can see here, but in flat, enlivened colors.

## Port de Barcelona and the San Juan water-splash icon

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L28 · 05:51–07:27

In this other graphic mark for the Autonomous Port of Barcelona this chromatic resource can easily be seen. The straight geometric shape alludes to the port and the wavy shape to the water. This identification is possible not so much through these morphological features but, above all, through the iconic colors used: the grey of concrete and the blue of water. From this level of saturated chromatic denotation, the viewer's mind builds both the idea of what is real — an iconic reproduction — and the idea of what has been emphasized. In this way, exalted color, being an iconic color just like naturalistic color, also helps identify the form it represents.

This icontype (*iconotipo*) designed by San Juan, in a version we made ourselves in black and white, could at first only be identified as the depiction of a water splash. But in its original presentation it is shown in different colors to identify the different cultural sites it stands for: blue for the Maritime Museum, purple for the Royal Observatory, and gold for the Queen's House. Three different colors with the same shape which, thanks to the reference color provides, let the image take on different meanings — meanings that are, of course, built through the pragmatic context of their use. Blue, water, for the National Maritime Museum. Violet, galaxy, for the Royal Observatory Greenwich. And gold, crown, for the Queen's House.

## Landor's "Comer Sano" identity and editorial illustration

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L28 · 07:27–08:28

In this project designed by Landor for the "Comer Sano de Forma Simple" (Eat Healthy, Simply) campaign, forms are abstracted while keeping color in its iconic dimension. It is an exalted iconic chromaticism built from the colors proper to different foods, which allows their identification. This project is the visual identity of a healthy, tasty, takeaway food line. With vivid colors and clean geometric shapes, it aims to reflect the harmony of this balanced approach to eating, while the generous white space creates a sense of luminosity and well-being.

In the field of editorial design, especially in productions made for a children's audience, illustrations with exalted colors are generally found. In most cases this responds to a pedagogical function of the illustration: every element keeps the iconic color of things, but these colors are enlivened so they provide the visual stimuli that children need to enrich their neural connections.

## Packaging: socks, soup, snacks, juices and honey

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L28 · 08:28–11:16

The field of packaging is very rich in examples of exalted chromatic application. This is closely tied to some of the goals colors serve in commercial packaging design: attracting attention, differentiating, standing out, generating visual impact, or holding attention as long as possible.

The appeal of this line of sock packaging for the BIC brand, designed by Mouse Graphics, comes above all from the chromatic impact of these very vivid colors. On one side you see the socks in their real, saturated color, and on the other a full-color depiction of the matching shoe. Through color, a playful image is proposed that recreates the act of getting dressed: each sock package is the image of a type of footwear — women's, sport, men's, casual, and so on — with the matching sock.

## Closing

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L28 · 11:16–11:32

After analyzing how exalted color functions as an exaggerated color across different pieces of graphic design, the lesson moves on to the third variable of color: expressionist color.

### Key points (derived)

- Exalted color is iconic color that has been intensified through greater saturation — an exaggeration of naturalistic color, not a change of hue.
- Saturation is described as a quantitative property: more saturation reads as more vivid and intense, less saturation reads as washed-out and grey.
- The same iconic shape can carry different exalted colors to signal different meanings, as in the San Juan water-splash icon (blue/water/Maritime Museum, purple/galaxy/Observatory, gold/crown/Queen's House).
- Recurring domains for exalted color in this lesson: painting (Gauguin, Matisse), film and book-cover imagery of blood, posters and advertising, brand marks and pictograms, and packaging (socks, soup, olives, juice, snacks, honey), plus children's editorial illustration for its stimulating, pedagogical effect.
