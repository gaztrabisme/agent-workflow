## What is psychological colour?

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L31 · 00:00–01:14

This lesson covers the first variable of colour with a connotative function: psychological colour (*color psicológico*), the variable through which we connect with the world of the senses.

Psychological colour concerns whatever affects the psyche and belongs to psychology — psychology meaning the way an individual or a community feels. In its psychological variable, colour activates part of the human unconscious by projecting itself onto the psyche, generating subjective ways of feeling, both individual and collective. Every colour is open to individual interpretation and produces personal sensations. But when people share these subjective personal sensations, we can recognise an intersubjectivity of colour within our social environment, which allows it to be put in common, to be communicated. Psychological colour generates what could be called sensitive meanings, or synaesthetic effects, shared by a large part of the individuals within a society.

## Painters who worked with psychological colour

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L31 · 01:14–02:42

In Francis Bacon's *Tres estudios de figuras en la base de un crucifijo* (Three Studies of Figures at the Base of a Crucifixion), psychological colour is perceived not through recognising the represented form, but as an action on the psyche, provoking more intimate impressions. Likewise, in this work by Jean-Michel Basquiat, the colours do not try to represent reality but to generate emotions. The same can be appreciated in these works from Picasso's Blue Period, in which colour aims to affect the sensations that shape a human being's sensitive perception — a different mode of knowledge that helps complement our rational perception of the world around us.

We must accept that colour acts on the receiver's subconscious, which is transferred to consciousness as a sensation, and that these sensations generate reactions in the individual. If pink, for instance, looks lighter than red, that is a purely sensory phenomenon. But if pink suggests feelings such as intimacy, sweetness or softness, we are speaking in psychological terms. The sensations that colours produce can be very subtle and delicate, as in this work titled *Nenúfares* (Water Lilies) by Claude Monet.

## A film example: Pixar's Up

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L31 · 02:42–03:45

A good example of colour acting on the subconscious is the film *Up*, produced by Disney and Pixar, whose opening section we believe is the most moving and best-told story in cinema. In every moment that Ellie is present — that vital, optimistic female character — the surroundings are steeped in magenta, remaining present even at her own funeral. However, in the scene where Carl, her husband, returns from the funeral alone, this colour gradually fades into a dark environment with no colour at all: Ellie is no longer there.

In the same film, in the scene where the couple climb the hill as young people, vibrant greens and blues convey energy. However, when they climb the hill again as an elderly couple, the colours shift to darker, less saturated tones. Through this we already feel the decline and the sorrow of the fatal ending to come — two very different, fairly opposed moments in the characters' lives that we are able to understand thanks to the psychological dimension of colour.

## How perception turns a sensation into meaning

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L31 · 03:45–06:15

We can define the perception of colour sensation as a human process of integrating the chromatic stimuli received from the environment — a process involving the receiver's progressive engagement, starting from the sensation received, an effort of attention and an effort of intellect. Through perception, human beings mentally separate an element from its context to make it intelligible. In this initial attention, we distinguish the various colours, suppressing possible ambiguity in order to later understand the information: learning and understanding at the same time. Experiences are generated in this process of learning and understanding, and so the chromatic pieces we design generate meaning in the receiver based fundamentally on reflexes and associations with images and experiences retained and integrated into their memory — a memory built up from both personal and collective knowledge.

The brain translates the colour sensation coming from our outer world, but the psychic creation of this world happens within us. Because the psychological meaning generated by a colour resides in people, it is very important that you always keep this in mind: there is no single point of view, either for receiving it or for projecting it.

## Meaning depends on the receiver, the sender's intent and context

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L31 · 06:15–07:34

This meaning of a colour depends on the sender's intention, the graphic designer's intention as mediator, the context, and the receiver's knowledge and expectations. In short, the psychological meaning that can be drawn from a colour is conditioned by the state of society, by the cultural behaviour of those who live within it — in short, by the individual.

If we understand the communication process as an act in which the receiver constructs the meaning, we can understand that the designed graphic element does not make up the whole message: the message stays relatively uncertain until the receiver establishes it through their own intervention, generating various associations of ideas. This is how the blue of BBVA can be interpreted as security, or the blue of Scottex (captions: *escotes*) as hygiene or cleanliness. Because of this capacity to generate associations of ideas in people's minds, psychological colour carries values that go beyond the object represented, beyond denotative meaning. These associations are made with a particular situation or circumstance in mind, because a colour is a sign capable of meaning different things depending on context — the meaning of blue, for example, varies according to the contextual factors that condition it.

This psychological variable of colour must take into account the different effects a single colour provokes in different observers, according to factors as varied as lived experience, sensitivity or intelligence. In general, the emotional values associated with different colours are influenced by era, place, nationality, culture, education, age, sex, fashion and social status, among others.

## Colour as a social sign, and Goethe's foundational study

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L31 · 07:34–08:45

Communication through colour in graphic design is not a one-way relationship between sender and receiver: it is a relationship of situated signs, that is, the creation of visual discourses of a social nature. Colour therefore implies a connotative design decision, one born from a search to generate a visual discourse. If we want to properly control the psychological effects a colour should generate in a specific situation, we designers must know the innate psychological reactions that colour can provoke.

Various authors over time have tried to specify the psychological character attributed to different colours, cataloguing them individually. These characters attributed to colours should in any case be considered generic data, drawn from certain constants of a specific cultural perception. This matter of universal chromatic sensations was studied in depth, for example, by the writer and intellectual Johann Goethe in his work on the psychology of colour. Goethe's theories laid the foundations of the colour psychology we know today, and highlighted human perception as the aspect other, purely scientific theories of colour had overlooked. The psychological connotations of colour feel familiar to us, even though — Goethe's theories included — they have never been scientifically proven, since this is shifting ground, pushed along by cultural factors at the same time.

## Emotional opposites: warm/cold and other polarities

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L31 · 08:45–09:38

Colours are sometimes associated with emotional opposites, in which we can observe psychological contrasts — for example, associations of ideas built on polar concepts such as heat and cold. On the hot–cold polarity, it seems clear that a large share of people can agree that red can be judged warmer than green or blue. This is the easiest, most classic example usually mentioned when reflecting on the sensations colours produce. The hot–cold opposition is a subjective but widely shared response, one to which a quantitative character can even be given.

Analysing the various known data on intersubjective constants of cultural perception, we notice that this data tends to rest on structural schemes — on organised oppositions capable of taking on meanings that can vary by culture, such as the already-mentioned cold–warm or passive–active, feminine–masculine, natural–artificial, exclusive–popular, and many more.

## Physical values shift meaning; the Shell and Nourish examples

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L31 · 10:41–11:45

The constants of our colour sensations — hue, luminosity and saturation — can completely change how a colour is perceived, along with the meaning it can carry. The physical values of a colour, such as its different intensities or shades, can vary its chromatic meanings: the same red, identical in hue, intensity and brightness, does not mean the same next to a yellow as it does next to a violet.

In short, colour as sensory matter and as signifying matter is interpreted through its physical values, but it is also deeply affected by three decisive variables: the socio-cultural context, the observer's subjective characteristics, and the contrasts of the polychromatic setting in which it appears. In this packaging for Nourish Snacks, we can appreciate the suggestion of the snacks' flavours: the graphic pieces include a modular system with patterns, colours and typography that represent the brand, letting it generate synaesthetic meanings that serve sweet as well as savoury, spicy or sour snacks alike. It is a polychromatic proposal in which the socio-cultural context and the receiver's own characteristics play a very important role.

## The reveal: colour, sensation and product

### Key points (derived)

- Psychological colour projects onto the unconscious, producing individual sensations that become intersubjective — and so communicable — when shared across a society.
- Painters (Bacon, Basquiat, Picasso's Blue Period, Monet) and film (Pixar's *Up*) use colour to act on the viewer's subconscious directly, independent of represented form.
- Meaning is completed by the receiver: the same colour (blue) can denote a hue and connote security (BBVA) or hygiene (Scottex) depending on context, sender intent and the receiver's own experience, sensitivity and intelligence.
- Goethe's studies grounded today's colour psychology by foregrounding human perception, though such meanings remain culturally shifting, not scientifically proven.
- Colours combine: shared surface area and overall chromatic perception (Shell's yellow+red; Nourish Snacks' modular system) can matter as much as each colour's own quality.
- Hue, luminosity and saturation each independently change a colour's meaning, even when the base colour (Shell's red/yellow) stays recognisable.
- The six-package game demonstrates the lesson's core claim experientially: orange reads as vitamins, dark red/brown as chocolate, blue+red as disinfectant, violet as cosmetics, black+yellow (animal-warning colours) as poison, and green+blue as detergent.
