## What symbolic colour is

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 00:00–01:06

This lesson explains the symbolic variable of colour. Symbolic colour (*color simbólico*) has a psychological basis, but it already belongs to a world of codifications.

By symbol (*símbolo*) we mean everything that, by convention or association, can be considered representative of an idea, a concept, a set of values — that which uses suggestion or the association of signs to produce conscious emotions. In this sense, symbolic colour, unlike psychological colour, constitutes a codification that establishes a kind of collective psychology: from being a sensitive, collective and intersubjective phenomenon, it has become a cultural phenomenon. The symbolism of colour is one of the most socially shared and most consciously used resources in our visual context — in art, heraldry, liturgy, alchemy, literature, and a long etcetera. We could say that the origin and social use of colour's symbolic meanings comes from very diverse sources, at very different moments of human cultural development.

## The Arnolfini Portrait as a symbolic scene

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 01:06–02:39

For example, we can see how blue has traditionally been used in art to represent the Virgin Mary. The Arnolfini Portrait (*El retrato del matrimonio Arnolfini*), painted in the 15th century by Jan van Eyck, is an extraordinary example of the creation of a symbolic scenario in which supernatural events are represented. The whole painting is populated with symbolic elements: a lit candle, a signature, a mirror with two figures reflected in it — every last detail, down to the smallest, carries symbolic messages. This mattered greatly to Renaissance European viewers, since images summed up much broader semantic content, often related to morality.

This particular painting recreates an everyday scene in order to convey moralising messages through symbolic objects that allude to transcendental matters. In fact, the objects surrounding the couple, their arrangement, the setting, and of course the colour, are all put to work in a symbology meant to exalt the sacredness of marriage. This work in fact acts as a representative document of a union — something like a traditional wedding portrait-photograph. The chromatic symbolism in the painting is an essential discursive factor of the work. Let's look at it.

## The bride's silhouette: fashion, not pregnancy

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 02:39–03:14

First we should clarify that in the bride's — Giovanna Cenami's — swollen belly, we should not read an anticipated pregnancy. Following the feminine fashion of the era, the bride appears with a bulge in her belly that makes her look pregnant. This is a deliberate aesthetic anomaly: at the time, a very tight bodice narrowed the chest, creating a bulge at the belly and also at the hips. In fact, almost all of this artist's female figures are dressed this way.

## Green and blue: fertility and purity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 03:14–04:07

Green is a colour we still recognise today in many visual-communication media associated with life and fertility. The inner garment Giovanna wears is blue, a colour that refers to her purity and virginity, given its symbolic association with water and cleanliness. As already noted, blue is the colour through which we recognise the figure of the Virgin in a large part of the history of painting's pictorial representations. Symbolic of purity and cleanliness, this is why we can find it prevalent in a large part of water packaging, as we'll see further ahead.

## The groom's brown robe: masculine severity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 05:24–06:18

As for the colour of the groom's clothing — Giovanni Arnolfini — the sober, earthy tone of this dark brown seems intended to reinforce the severe bearing demanded of husbands at the time, reinforced by the gesture of blessing the ceremony and by holding his wife's hand as she gives it to him — thereby demonstrating the moral power of the masculine in the household. This symbolism of the masculine, generated by earth colours — browns, greys, etc. — can be seen in the intent behind products specifically aimed at men, such as perfumes. Symbolic allusions to masculinity through colour, as we can see, still endure in our collective imagination.

## The origins of heraldry

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 06:18–07:39

Another part of the colour symbolism we use today comes, as mentioned earlier, from heraldic symbolism, which arose from the need to distinguish and differentiate knights and particular groups through shields and military blazons during the Middle Ages. We could go so far as to say that symbolic colour and sign colour have their most specific and immediate origin in heraldry. Immediate recognition was a very important, crucial aspect in the hand-to-hand combats of wartime; this need for identification and distinction — friend from enemy — is what gave rise to true heraldry.

This is the case of the coat of arms of Navarre, dating back to the 13th century, its colour recognisable as a symbol of services, corporations and associations of this autonomous community. Before the 12th century, the colour of military ensigns had no particular meaning, until the prosperity of the feudal system, with its insistence on rank and title, made the armament of war more complex. With the new closed helmets, it became impossible to talk to one another, and identification through the use of colour on shields as well as banners became essential. Thus, each of the components making up a shield had a determined meaning and an established communicative function.

## Heraldic enamels (*esmaltes*): the vocabulary of colour

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 07:39–08:19

In heraldry's own terminology, colours are generally called enamels (*esmaltes*), and they comprised two metals and five main colours. The metals are gold (*oro*) and silver (*argén*), and the colours are gules, sinople, purpura, sable and azur — that is, red, green, purple, black and blue. In the Renaissance, a complex symbolism of these heraldic colours was developed in relation to virtues, planets, metals, precious stones, and even codes of conduct. Each enamel carried its own symbolic meanings, and so the different members of the families bearing each colour were characterised by the qualities noted in each case.

## Colour in religion: the Catholic liturgy and beyond

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 09:05–10:00

Just as with art and heraldry, different religions and civilisations have made intentional use of colour. On screen we show you a table with examples of the symbolic coding of colours in Catholic liturgy — here you can certainly appreciate and weigh the psychological variable applied to everyday religious ritual. Across all cultures, the different religions have had an enormous influence when it comes to the symbolic coding of colours: white for purity, red for fire and blood, green for hope and eternal life, violet for penitence, black for mourning, blue for heaven. But in other religious contexts, such as the Anglican Church, the same colours mean different things. This shows that the symbolic code has a conventional character. Sometimes we attribute to colours meanings more related to the innate, and other times to the learned. In any case, symbolic colours have different meanings depending on the specific cultural environment in which they are perceived.

## Colour meanings vary by culture and globalisation

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 10:00–10:52

It's clear that colours have an associative effect, and that their connotations are not the same the world over, as we said in an earlier lesson. In short, colour can point to meanings that, depending on the country and its culture, can differ. Bearing in mind principles of universality and this era of globalisation, it's important to attend to this question of cultures and differences in the meanings attributed to colours. It could be said that culture determines, through social convention, which colour will represent concepts such as feminine or masculine.

## Pink, blue and the HeForShe campaign

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 10:52–11:52

Only in our cultural context, where pink is used to symbolise the feminine and blue to symbolise the masculine, can these graphic products be understood. And only within a culture can the meaning of the pink used in this campaign, driven by the United Nations under the name HeForShe — the "for her" — and developed by the DIA consultancy, be understood. It is a solidarity initiative that aims to mobilise the whole of the male gender against the inequalities suffered by women and girls around the world on gender grounds. The graphic mark represents this movement through the formal coincidence of part of the structure of the male symbol, transgressing it into the colour pink, and the female symbol in the colour black. According to the design consultancy itself, this fusion of symbols aims to reflect the necessary union of men and women working together to make gender equality a reality.

## Sedimented meaning: colour idioms and inherited symbolic values

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L32 · 11:52–13:09

This social convention works in the collective subconscious, detached from the personal area or domain, and so the meanings and associations attributed to colours are the result of sedimentations accumulated through human experience over time. As a consequence, individuals belonging to the same culture seem to react in a similar way to the same colour. In this category we find the symbolic value of colours: red and yellow for heat, blue for technology, green for ecology, violet for contemplation, black for elegance, and gold for luxury. Unquestionably, in this collective cultural field, colours can acquire their own values of symbolic expression, inherited over time. And all of them have a turbulent history reaching back to the mists of time, one that has left its mark even in our vocabulary. We say: "lo vemos negro" (we see it black — things look bleak), "se puso amarillo de envidia" (he turned yellow with envy), "verde de miedo" (green with fear), or "se quedó en blanco" (she went blank — literally, white). In short, a symbolic dimension of colour that is an indispensable resource in the essentially symbolic language of graphic design.

### Key points (derived)

- Symbolic colour is a cultural codification built on psychological colour: it moves from individual/collective sensation to a shared, conventional code (art, heraldry, liturgy, alchemy, literature).
- The Arnolfini Portrait works as a single sustained case study: green (fertility), blue (purity/the Virgin), white ermine (purity), red (blood/passion/marriage) and brown (masculine severity) each carry a meaning still legible in today's branding (water bottles, skincare, wine, men's perfume).
- Heraldry is the direct historical source of symbolic and sign colour: shield "enamels" (gules, sinople, purpura, sable, azur, plus gold and silver) each carried assigned virtues and social obligations, born from the practical need for identification in combat.
- The same colour can carry opposite meanings across contexts: blue/black for peace/war in one poster tradition; white, red, green, violet and black mean different things again between Catholic and Anglican liturgy, and again across Europe, Japan, China and the Arab world.
- Pink/feminine and blue/masculine is a cultural convention, not a universal one — a point the HeForShe mark makes explicit by deliberately swapping the two colours between the male and female symbols.
- Symbolic meaning is sedimented collective experience, not personal: it shows up in ready-made brand associations (heat, technology, ecology, contemplation, elegance, luxury) and in idioms baked into the language itself (seeing things "black", turning "yellow" with envy, "green" with fear, going "blank/white").
