## Color's share of visual information

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 00:23–01:33

The first, unquestionable point: a graphic design process necessarily involves the use of color, and since graphic design means visual communication, this use of color is tied from the outset to communicative intentions and functions. Various studies conclude that around 80% of all information we receive is, generally speaking, visual in nature; and since visual information is bound up with shapes and colors, roughly 40% of that information normally concerns color specifically.

![5642 · U3 · L23 · 00:55](frames/U03L23-F0055.jpg)

On screen: "Informaciones de naturaleza óptica — 80% / Informaciones referidas al color — 40%" (Optical-nature information — 80% / Color-related information — 40%)  
Shows: Two ring-chart eye icons side by side, one plain, one with a rainbow iris, each showing its percentage.

Human beings register their surroundings through sight as a universe of colors — colors perceived a fraction of a second before the shapes that contain them. In this sense, color has an experience external to the message, prior to its meaning.

## Three powers of color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 01:59–02:31

Color exerts a triple action on the person observing it. First, a power of impression (*poder de impresión*): when a pigment is seen, it manifests itself by making an impression, drawing the observer's attention. Second, a power of expression: every color, on appearing, expresses a meaning and provokes an emotion. Third, a power of construction: because color carries its own meaning, it acquires the true value of a symbol capable of constructing, by itself, the communicative language of an idea.

![5642 · U3 · L23 · 02:31](frames/U03L23-F0231.jpg)

On screen: "1. Poder de impresión / 2. Poder de expresión / 3. Poder de construcción" (1. Power of impression / 2. Power of expression / 3. Power of construction), next to the NBC peacock logo.  
Shows: Three paired images illustrating each power: a colored iris and a cross-section of the retina (impression); a prism splitting light and a peacock's tail (expression); a color-bar test card and a news anchor on air (construction).

## Color as an omnipresent factor

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 02:31–03:43

As a visual resource of graphic design, color is an essential means for shaping our artificial environment. It is a highly significant element through its capacity to interact with individuals — the suggestive, indispensable element present in both nature and human-made objects, helping complete our image of reality. Aimed at a communicative goal, it is without doubt an influential factor: no area of activity is untouched by color at any moment of our daily lives. Color constantly proves its potential for enriching life and our perception of it — one more sign of our everyday existence. Color is present in every area of life and in everything around us, and, importantly, it is available to everyone: to those who play with it, those who need to control it, those who express themselves through it, those who find relief in contemplating it, and those who put it to work for visual communication.

## A personal reflection on teaching color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 03:43–04:45

Before continuing, the teachers, Leire and Eduardo, share a personal feeling about color. In their teaching and professional work in graphic design, they have sometimes sensed that color is treated as an unavoidable component of things and, curiously, provokes greater reflection on its value as a visual-communication resource — as if its usefulness were limited to a strict accompaniment of form. They feel obliged to make the effort to show how color, from graphic design, can virtually lead us into many areas of people's social and cultural life — not as a mere accompaniment to form, nor as a purely subjective matter of personal taste. With this course, they want to help educate people about color: recognizing its fundamental value for visual communication and its contribution to maintaining and developing human relationships.

## Color as information

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 04:45–05:32

A founding principle: in our visual-communicative context, color is an essential graphic resource of graphic design for establishing human relationships in community (*comunidad*) — a word that shares its root with communication (*comunicación*). Color is information about our environment: an enormous potential of semantic and cognitive information, generally underestimated in our culture, which also contributes elements of emotional sensitivity. The teachers' goal is to address this specifically within graphic design — something they consider urgent, as university teachers and researchers in the discipline, for training future designers. ## Case study: the color yellow on a book cover

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 06:07–07:01

On the cover of José Saramago's novel *Caín* (Cain), the color yellow is used with a traditional symbolic function: since the Middle Ages it symbolized negative feelings, among them jealousy and envy. Yellow has traditionally marked traitors, outlaws, prostitutes and other social outcasts. The despised apostle Judas has traditionally been depicted in yellow clothing in various paintings. The Inquisition also applied this color as a punishment to those tried in its acts of faith, who had to wear the mark for a period to draw their neighbors' contempt. During the Third Reich in Germany, Jewish people were forced to wear the yellow Star of David to identify and, in turn, stigmatize the Jewish people.

![5642 · U3 · L23 · 07:01](frames/U03L23-F0701.jpg)

On screen: "JOSÉ SARAMAGO — CAÍN" yellow book cover; three paintings of Judas's betrayal of Christ; two illustrated Inquisition-era penitents in yellow-and-red sanbenito garments; a yellow Star of David patch reading "Jude" and a black-and-white photograph of two people wearing it on the street.  
Shows: The book cover beside a historical sequence tracing yellow's use as a mark of condemnation: Judas painted in yellow robes, Inquisition penitents in yellow sanbenitos, and the yellow star imposed on Jewish people under the Third Reich.

## Color signs as cultural convention

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 07:01–08:02

Thanks to the human faculty of communication, people can exchange messages; it is the interpretation of these messages through signs that lets us gain knowledge, become aware of things and take the actions — social, cultural, affecting our coexistence — that we need to take as individuals. Communication through chromatic signs can be understood as a cultural phenomenon, where the cultures of communication are the rules of the culture. As this poster for preventing alcohol-related traffic accidents shows, one precondition for building messages through color is that its constituent elements draw on norms, knowledge, traditions, beliefs, experiences or sensations that sender and receiver can share. The use of yellow, red and black here rests on experiences, sensations and coded social norms associated with prevention, danger and death.

## The etymology of design: signs and intentions

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 08:33–09:34

Morphologically, "diseño" is formed, first, by the prefix "de," from which a generic meaning of determining or resolving something can be drawn; and second, by the noun "signo" (sign). The concept of design is directly tied to the idea of intention (*designio*): to designate is to determine signs through the act of designing. Design deals with signs and intentions, understanding signs as what human beings exchange and hold in common — that is, put into communication.

![5642 · U3 · L23 · 09:02](frames/U03L23-F0902.jpg)

On screen: "DISEÑO / DISEGNO / DESIGN" and "DE + SIGNUM — Determinación o resolución de algo + Signo — DESIGNIO / SIGNO" (DE + SIGNUM — determination or resolution of something + sign — intention / sign)  
Shows: Word-origin diagram splitting "design" into its Latin roots.

Designing a graphic sign is not a matter of having a sign-related idea on a whim, but of building a laborious web of effective relationships of meaning for sharing ideas. Graphic designers, by this principle, are controllers of meaning: their work lies in the visual organization of content through the choice and combination of iconographic, typographic and chromatic discourses (*discursos*).

## Reading a brand as four discourses

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 09:34–10:14

Designing a graphic sign means creating a structured organization of discourses, among which color is an essential foundation for generating the desired meanings in the receiver. In graphic design, color forms a significant visual experience of meaning — meanings that are universally shared. In this sense, we can speak of a language of color and establish that, in a visual-communicative context, color is sign.

## What comes next: harmony and contrast

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L23 · 10:14–10:52

To close, the teachers note that colors very rarely appear before us as isolated, particular stimuli. As the following lessons will show, the principles of harmony and contrast are factors that shape every system of iconic production through color. Having laid out these reflections on the discursive possibilities of color in graphic communication, the next lesson hands over to Leire, who will address some reflections on the aesthetic value of color.

### Key points (derived)

- Color carries a measurable share of visual information (roughly 40% of the ~80% of information that is visual) and is perceived fractionally before the shapes that carry it — demonstrated by the blurred-to-sharp shelf and the Avis/Visa color-swap test.
- Color acts on viewers in three ways: impression (draws attention), expression (carries meaning and emotion), and construction (builds the communicative language of an idea by itself).
- Color is treated here as a sign (*signo*), on par with iconic and typographic signs, and design itself is etymologically about signs and intentions (*designio*) — a discipline of controlling meaning, not decoration.
- The same color can carry very different, culturally coded meanings depending on convention: yellow marks condemnation from Judas's robes to the Nazi-era star; yellow, red and black together code danger and death on a road-safety poster.
- Analyzing a logo (Shell, Tuning, Harley-Davidson) as four parallel discourses — verbal, iconographic, typographic, chromatic — shows what color specifically contributes to a brand's meaning, separate from its wordmark or icon.
