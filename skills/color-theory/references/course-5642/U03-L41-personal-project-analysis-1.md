## Researching how illusions of movement work

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L41 · 02:43–03:52

They began by gathering visual documentation for the first part of the commission: nine large-format pieces with movement illusions for the building's windows. This type of illusion causes some images, though static, to produce a sensation of movement in us — the main culprit is thought to be peripheral vision, which evolved over time to detect dangers and predators around us. When an image contains strong jumps in luminosity, our peripheral vision tends to interpret this as movement, and the effect is intensified further when that jump is combined with curved shapes. Through this essential research phase, they came to know and understand the different types of resource for generating apparent movement — among them, static figures that arise from a complex combination of white or lit-up spaces alternating with darkened black spaces. It was on this resource that they chose to focus.

## Testing color combinations: which one reads as movement?

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L41 · 05:27–06:35

Once the shapes for the graphic system had been created, they began evaluating different combinations to check which color tones and pairings produced the greatest sensation of movement. They experimented, on one hand, with combinations in a contiguous-analogy relationship — this is a selection from all the sketches developed, showing different color trials in figure and ground. The color applied has its own effect on how the movement is perceived: in the first combination, the cool harmony on the left, the movement that emerges follows a clockwise direction; in the combination on the right, this particular pairing of a cool and a warm color, the suggested movement runs the opposite way; while in the example in the center, the movement effect is a little slowed down — much slower, in fact, and for some people no sensation of movement arises at all.

## Color-perception illusions: circles that only look colored

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L41 · 11:00–11:41

This is one of the panels on illusions of color perception. The brain processes and interprets the information that reaches it through the eyes and draws its own conclusions, which is why illusions sometimes occur. As was shown earlier in this course, the colors surrounding an object can condition and change how that object's color is perceived. These circles that make up the hearts are white, even though you perceive them as pink or blue. The same goes for these other circles, also white — the reason you can perceive them as other colors is that the optical effect created by the colors around them makes you see them as a different color.

### Key points (derived)

- The project runs the full design process taught earlier in the course — commission, documentation/research, creation, color selection/validation, development — applied to a real brief: nine large-format window pieces for a public building, plus the full set of graphic supports for a science-outreach event.
- Movement illusions of this kind rely on peripheral vision misreading strong luminosity jumps (especially combined with curved shapes) as motion; the design team isolated one specific resource — alternating light/dark module shapes arranged in a radial or grid pattern — and iterated it through sketches, scale prototypes and two candidate base shapes before settling on one.
- Color is a secondary but decisive variable on top of the black/white shape: the same module read as moving clockwise, counter-clockwise or barely at all depending on whether the palette was cool, warm, mixed, analogous or complementary — and the adjacency of neighboring windows further constrained which combination could be used where.
- The finished palette (red-magenta on green, blue on yellow-green, yellow on violet) was chosen for overall harmony across all nine windows, then reused as the background of the event's identity system (poster, leaflet, billboard) for the sake of recognition and because a simple background suits the graphic system better.
- The same movement pieces were re-purposed for a second, independent exhibition inside Metro Bilbao, which required new color decisions (purple/yellow became blue/yellow-green) once judged against the other panels in the set, and was expanded with three further illusion types: impossible figures, parallelism/hidden shapes, and color perception (the hearts and striped-circle panels, where every "colored" element is actually plain white).
- The same design vocabulary carried into a physical intervention — a café-wall illusion applied to escalator-adjacent stair risers — and into simple merchandise (T-shirts), showing color used, in the teachers' words, "concentrated to the essential" so it can function as a sign rather than as decoration.
