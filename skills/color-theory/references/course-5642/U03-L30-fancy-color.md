## What is fantasy color?

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L30 · 00:08–01:03

This lesson covers the last variable of denotative color: fantasy color (*color fantasioso*). It distinguishes two kinds: on one hand imaginary color, on the other arbitrary color.

In the fantasy color variant, color is no longer iconic but abstract — a color that lets itself be carried away by fantasy and imagination. Fantastical chromaticism contradicts the color that things actually have in the image. Through this radical modification of color, the aim is to change the perception not of reality, as happened with expressionist color, but of the iconic image itself. Fantasy color seeks more imaginative chromatic representations, an unreal effect with a fantastic air. Even while keeping a relative degree of iconicity, it no longer reproduces the color of visible reality.

## Imaginary and arbitrary: two kinds of fantasy color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L30 · 02:39–03:30

In this context, fantasy color has two fundamental aspects: the imaginary on one hand, the arbitrary on the other. The imaginary refers to the unreal, found in imagined fantastical scenes such as science fiction, or the unreal character of dreams. Imaginary color is distinguished from arbitrary color on one essential point: the former corresponds to a collective imaginary that comes from myths and literature. The arbitrary, by contrast, refers to the unreal through the graphic palette's own value. Arbitrary color isn't motivated by a chromatic vision of reality, nor does it aim to exalt the chromaticism of things or dramatize color for greater expressiveness; nor is it tied to myths, legends or stories.

## Green demons and white ghosts

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L30 · 04:49–05:49

The devil or demon can also be imagined in greenish colors, as has traditionally been depicted for centuries. Green, because of its ambiguity, can stir unease; hence the custom of showing evil spirits, demons, dragons, serpents and other malevolent creatures in a greenish tone.

![5642 · U3 · L30 · 05:10](frames/U03L30-F0510.jpg)

On screen: "DARREN SHAN — Demon Thief", "Weird Tales", "APOCALIPSIS Z — Los días oscuros" (The Dark Days), "El demonio de la perversidad" (The Imp of the Perverse — Poe's original English title), "La Semilla del Diablo" (the Spanish title for Rosemary's Baby, literally "The Devil's Seed"), "THE DEVIL" [rest omitted]  
Shows: A grid of nine demon-themed book covers in green and red tones, beside a medieval devil tarot card, a winged demon illustration, and a manuscript painting of devils tempting figures.

White, attributed to ghosts or specters, is likewise imaginary. The figure of a ghost, like that of an avatar, a superhero, an extraterrestrial or the devil himself, is iconic not with respect to a human being's actual figure, but with respect to the cultural prototype we've built of it. We imagine them organic, indefinite, floating. And if this iconicity of form relative to the imaginary model has its own rules, so does color: whites, pale, indefinite, almost transparent — because they would be less like ghosts if they imitated the colors of living humans more closely, wouldn't they?

## The psychedelic poster: Art Nouveau and Op Art

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L30 · 07:07–08:07

The psychedelic poster, for example, drew on fantasy color as a visual response to this spirit of social protest. The main visual influences displayed in this graphic work were, first, Art Nouveau, visible in the sinuous, fluid curves of its images; and also the painting movement called Op Art, or optical art, in which artists sought, through the arrangement of blocks of color, a kind of optical illusion of movement and vibration.

## The Copenhagen children's library identity

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L30 · 10:02–10:56

In this visual-identity project for the children's department of the Copenhagen Library, designed by Hasba Aníbal, this use of arbitrary color can be seen very clearly: arbitrary colors applied to designated shapes. A heart can be purple, a book green, a hat red, glasses blue — and these can in turn keep changing color. It's a use of color for purely graphic ends. The project's goal is to create a modular system of shapes in basic colors so children can interact by combining these shapes while creating their own stories and characters, generating greater interest and involvement. As you can see, the use of simple iconic shapes combined with arbitrary colors determines the visual character of the possible results.

## Ambiguity through color: "La dama del lago"

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L30 · 11:46–12:14

This refusal of color to reinforce, and instead contradict, the effect of reality is in some cases a discursive strategy based on ambiguity. For example, on the cover of the book *La dama del lago* (The Lady in the Lake), the fantastical blue of the hair creates an image that flips the figure's interpretation into a new one: a lake. The fantastical color of the hair is, at the same time, the iconic color of water, making this image ambiguous. The use of fantasy color helps this image support several readings at once.

## Closing: the aesthetic pleasure of arbitrary color

Anchor: 5642 · U3 · L30 · 12:47–14:11

We can sum up by saying a graphic piece can either faithfully reproduce natural colors, or modify and invent them, as with arbitrary color. The possibilities for working with color are infinite, but not all of them generate the same effect and meaning for the viewer. Arbitrary color, when used by valuing its graphic and plastic possibilities, can generate significant aesthetic pleasure, as with these projects shown on screen — their chromatic proposal is entirely diverted from the colors these figures have in real life.

![5642 · U3 · L30 · 13:22](frames/U03L30-F1322.jpg)

On screen: "UENO ZOO", "POWIĘKSZENIE" (Enlargement — Polish poster title for Antonioni's film "Blow-Up"), "CAMPER" [rest omitted]  
Shows: A red, spiky-maned lion-like creature with human eyes on a blue ground for a zoo poster, beside four more posters using arbitrary flat color for a dot pattern, a cow, a constellation diagram, and a running dinosaur skeleton.

And to finish: fantasy color, in its arbitrary facet, comes close — through its abstraction and independence — to schematic color with a sign function. On the other hand, by contradicting what we perceive as real, color starts to generate meanings that go beyond the literal, beyond the faithful representation of things. In short, it opens images to more readings.

With fantasy color, we've now seen the last variable of the denotative function. And with fantasy color we move closer to the next function, the connotative one, where we'll look at psychological evocations on one hand and symbolic ones on the other. ### Key points (derived)

- Fantasy color is non-iconic: it contradicts the real color of what it depicts, unlike naturalistic, exalted or expressionist color, which all stay tied to a thing's actual color in some way.
- Imaginary color draws on a collective cultural imaginary (myths, literature, genre convention): blue avatars, green monsters/aliens/demons, white ghosts, psychedelic hippie-era color.
- Arbitrary color has no motivation at all in the depicted form's real color; its value is purely graphic and plastic (Fukuda's posters, the Copenhagen library icons, Saramago's covers, Pierre Mendel's green face).
- Fantasy color can create deliberate ambiguity, letting one image support two readings at once, as in the "La dama del lago" cover where blue hair reads equally as lake water.
- Arbitrary color, by its abstraction and independence from reality, is described as a bridge toward the next function covered in the course: schematic (sign) color.
