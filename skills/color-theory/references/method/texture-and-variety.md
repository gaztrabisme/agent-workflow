# Texture and variety: color richness that leaves the structure alone

Reader: an agent adding life to flat color areas without breaking the value plan. Sources: [L12](../course/U03-L12-textures-what-they-are-for-and-how-to-generate-them.md), [L13](../course/U03-L13-history-the-perfect-pretext-to-add-color.md), [L14](../course/U04-L14-the-power-of-simplicity.md), [L18](../course/U04-L18-bonus-solving-common-color-problems.md).

## What texture is

- Texture is not Photoshop brushes. It is **high-frequency detail**: the constant small changes of color in reality. Pick any color in a photo and the color around it changes all the time. (1140 · U3 · L12 · 00:25–01:23)
- Texture is also **pattern**: roof tiles, tree stripes, water rings, a lily pad's lines. It is "everything that would be painted on top of an object". (1140 · U3 · L12 · 01:23–03:33)
- Texture is **density of detail**. At the scale of the whole image, small stones, birds and specks on the sea work as texture. Remove them and the image goes flat. (1140 · U3 · L12 · 06:35–08:08)

## The style scale

- Styles run from a photograph, through concept art and textured illustration, to flat graphic work with only dots and lines. Texture is present in some form at every step. (1140 · U3 · L12 · 01:23–03:33)
- **The simpler and more graphic the style, the more deliberately texture must be used** to create **color accidents**: small, local hue changes. In grainy illustration, grain flecks make the greens that bridge yellows and blues. (1140 · U3 · L12 · 01:23–03:33, 1140 · U3 · L12 · 03:33–04:22)

## The key word: variety

- Variety is "what you want most in your image". Inside one established color group, add tones close to the group color to create "the illusion of life". Real grass, for example, is partly greener and partly drier and more yellow. (1140 · U3 · L12 · 04:22–05:59)
- Order matters:
  1. establish the large color groups and the bridges;
  2. only then vary inside them.
- **Change color, not value.** Texture in a purple rock adds blue on top of the purple, but in grayscale the textured areas barely change. "Generate color variety without directly affecting the structure of the image." (1140 · U3 · L12 · 05:59–06:35)
- **Photoshop recipe for a same-value hue shift.** In the color picker, compare the new color against the original, and move the hue without moving up or down in value. (1140 · U3 · L12 · 08:54–11:04)

## Optical gray

- **What it is:** two complementary colors of the **same value**, juxtaposed in spaced patches. (1140 · U3 · L12 · 16:03–18:21)
  - Up close they vibrate.
  - From a distance they read as one tone.
  - The grayscale shows no change.
- **Used in the teacher's "fun" version:** a purple ground is streaked with a greener purple and a pinker purple. (1140 · U4 · L14 · 10:58–13:00)
- **Used in a warm image:** a warm monster image uses it in its browns and oranges. (1140 · U4 · L17 · 04:32–05:51)
- **Recipe:**
  1. Put the complement down roughly.
  2. Adjust it with Hue/Saturation (Ctrl+U) until the values blend. It usually needs lightening.
  3. Lower the opacity if it shows too much.
  4. Add the opposite complement beside it so the two balance.

  (1140 · U3 · L12 · 16:03–18:21)

## Automatic variation

- Photoshop's **Brush > Color Dynamics** randomizes each stroke between the foreground and background colors. Use a grass or foliage brush clipped to a shadow shape. (1140 · U3 · L12 · 13:40–16:03)
- A little **hue jitter** adds stray pinks inside a blue shadow. Too much "puts in any color at all". (1140 · U3 · L12 · 13:40–16:03)

## Story and detail as pretexts for color

- **Added elements carry color.** Patterns, butterflies, a nest, an egg: each should also add color. (1140 · U3 · L13 · 03:03–04:50, 1140 · U3 · L13 · 05:34–06:51)
- **The focal area gets the most color contrast.** The flower's opening goes from blue through pink to yellow: three extremes concentrated where attention should land. (1140 · U3 · L13 · 03:03–04:50)
- **Quiet areas get variation, not contrast.** A foreground butterfly turns yellow to orange, adding variation to an empty area without adding contrast. (1140 · U3 · L13 · 05:34–06:51)

## Failure: lack of color variety

This is Problem 3.

**The failure:** a poster in olive and pale yellow varies only in value, so it reads as monochromatic. (1140 · U4 · L18 · 03:19–05:09)

**The fix, in steps:**
1. two color groups;
2. variation in saturation and hue within each group;
3. temperature contrast: a cold group, a warm group, a cold group.

**The test:** the Hue/Saturation check in [mistakes-checklist.md](mistakes-checklist.md). (1140 · U4 · L18 · 05:09–07:27)

Practice the teacher sets: in the PSD, change colors without changing values, for example turning yellows pink at the same value. He calls it "the most difficult exercise". See [U04-unit.md](../course/U04-unit.md).
