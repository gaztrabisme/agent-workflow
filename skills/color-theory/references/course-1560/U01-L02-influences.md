## Hayao Miyazaki

Anchor: 1560 · U1 · L02 · 00:26–00:58

The biggest influence on my work is definitely the legendary Japanese animation director Hayao Miyazaki. If you've seen his films, then you know why he's the best. If you haven't seen them, I strongly recommend that you watch all of them. He made a lot of wonderful movies, but also mangas, which is a bit less known. And I'm very inspired not only by the movies and mangas he created, but also by his mindset and his work ethic in general. He's a man who really aspires to show the beauty in the world, and he wants to make movies for young people to give them hope and show them that the world can be a good place. He really wants to spread a message of hope and fascination for nature, and this is something that I would really like to show in my own work as well.

## Kazuo Oga and the Ghibli background artists

Anchor: 1560 · U1 · L02 · 00:58–02:09

Next I'd like to talk about Kazuo Oga (captions: *Kasuo*), who was the art director on many of Miyazaki's movies, including Totoro and Mononoke. Their work, and the work of Studio Ghibli background artists in general, has been very influential on my work. One of the main reasons I got so much into painting landscapes and backgrounds in general was because I watched these movies as a kid, and the impression they made on me — I think their work speaks for itself. I particularly like the way that they represent the beauty of nature in general; these backgrounds really take you into the world of the movie, and I think that's really important. The themes in general as well — this comes more from Miyazaki — but the way they're executed in these backgrounds is always stunning.

## Dice Tsutsumi and Robert Kondo (Tonko House)

Anchor: 1560 · U1 · L02 · 02:09–03:25

Next is the work of Dice Tsutsumi (captions: *Dais*) and Robert Kondo. These two artists have a very similar style, and they formed a studio together called Tonko House (captions: *Donker House*). They made an amazing short movie called The Dam Keeper, which I highly encourage you to watch. These two artists have been very, very influential in the way I paint, because they have a very naturalistic way of depicting light that is at the same time very simplified, and their work really helped me to understand how all of this works. They also have a wonderful course online that you can look up to learn about how all of this works in detail. I really enjoy the way that they play with light in general.

They were both art directors at Pixar before they left to create their own studio. I'm showing some images from both of them here mixed, and you can see kind of a very similar feeling in general — I think it's very interesting to see two artists who really understand each other that much in the way they like to paint and to create, which allows them to collaborate beautifully.

## Joaquín Sorolla

Anchor: 1560 · U1 · L02 · 03:25–05:10

Joaquín Sorolla is one of my favorite painters. He was a painter active at the end of the 19th and beginning of the 20th century, and he made a lot of his paintings on the beach — this is why a lot of his paintings have a very strong sense of sunlight, and I really enjoyed that. Like here, we can see these rays of sunlight coming through the walls and onto those characters; these very bold brushstrokes indicate a very strong sunlight.

He has an incredible use of color. He was depicting all kinds of variations of hue — you see how these simple white dresses have green, violet, purple, blue, sometimes yellow; it's really fascinating. This is a great example of incredibly bright colors that all fit together, still a realistic picture, but it seems like he was enjoying emphasizing the saturation of colors very much, and it helps to feel that strong sense of sunlight. This is a much more subdued example, but you can see the amount of variation of color that we have on this simple white bed — there's all these different colors reflecting from around the room onto this white sheet.

And he has a fascinating depiction of water also — it's kind of hard to imagine how he was doing this without photographs or anything. He was just standing on the beach, making studies, and then creating these kinds of paintings. I think he's a really great example of vibrant colors that feel natural. If you go to Madrid, if you have a chance to visit his museum, it's absolutely incredible — you can see a lot of these paintings for yourself.

### Key points (derived)

- Four influences, each named for a different quality of light and color: Hayao Miyazaki (hope and thematic beauty); Kazuo Oga and the Ghibli background artists (immersive, stunning backgrounds); Dice Tsutsumi and Robert Kondo of Tonko House (naturalistic yet simplified light, both former Pixar art directors); Joaquín Sorolla (bold, saturated sunlight and color variation within shadow and white fabric).
- Two names are mis-transcribed in the captions and corrected here: "Kasuo Oga" for Kazuo Oga, and "Dais Tsutsumi" / "Donker House" for Dice Tsutsumi / Tonko House.
- The unit's additional resources list further named artists (Zac Retz, Aurélien Predal, Craig Mullins, Ryan Lang), Mike Azevedo's "Color Basics" lecture, and the trailer for The Dam Keeper.
