# Lesson 06 · Exposure, reflections and translucency

## The exposure problem

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L06 · 00:08–01:48

Three important aspects of capturing light: exposure, which you may already know from photography, and translucency and reflections, which are more about the properties of matter and how light interacts with it.

![1560 · U2 · L06 · 00:08](frames/U02L06-F0008.jpg)

On screen: "SAMUEL SMITH" / "Exposure, reflections and translucency"  
Shows: Lesson title card over an illustration of a boy in a cape and cap standing between two tall arched windows.

Imagine a line representing how dark or how light the real world can get. One end is complete darkness - a room with no windows and the light off, or simply closing your eyes. The other end is the brightest thing you could experience, like staring directly into the sun. The problem painters face is that the darkness or light a painting can actually show is only a small portion of that line: the canvas itself only runs from black to white. A painting's darkest area is never as dark as true blackness, and it can never emit light the way the sun does. So a painter has to find a way to describe the contrast seen in real life within these limits. The tool for that is exposure: you can expose for light or expose for shadows. Exposing for shadows compresses the light areas until they become overexposed; exposing for light pushes the dark areas down until they become underexposed.

![1560 · U2 · L06 · 01:26](frames/U02L06-F0126.jpg)

On screen: "EXPOSURE" / handwritten labels "B" and "W" on the range line  
Shows: A horizontal value line running from a dark icon to a sun icon. Two tick marks close to the dark end, labelled "B" and "W", mark the narrow black-to-white range a painting can actually cover, sitting inside the much wider range of real-world light.

## Choosing what to expose for

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L06 · 01:49–04:41

A photo of a living room shows the inside quite clearly, but looking through the window at what's outside is unreadable - the exterior is so much brighter than the interior that the camera can't capture both at once. You could recover the outside information by lowering the exposure, but then you lose the feeling of contrast you get in real life. So, just as in photography, a painter has to choose between showing all the information or depicting the feeling of light. Lowering the exposure reveals what's happening outside perfectly, but the inside then looks very dark - and that trade-off is a choice you make for every scene you paint.

Piet Mondrian's painting of a windmill chose very low exposure. He was there in real life and obviously saw more information than the painting shows, but he chose to render it this way to convey a certain feeling.

![1560 · U2 · L06 · 03:05](frames/U02L06-F0305.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: Two side-by-side views of a windmill silhouetted almost completely black against a glowing, cloud-mottled sky, with the moon or sun showing through a gap low in the clouds.

A painting by Dmitry Belyukin, looking out through an open church door, shows the outside as overexposed - which conveys the feeling of a very bright, sunny day. The inside of the doorway and the walls on the left stay clearly readable, but the information in the overexposed courtyard beyond is reduced because its values are compressed. Again, that doesn't mean this is exactly what the painter saw in real life - it conveys the feeling of what he saw. That's an important point about painting in general: you're not showing reality, because you can't show reality as it is - you can only describe it, since your means are so limited compared to the full value range of the real world. You have to choose what aspects of that range you want to describe.

A painting by Richard Schmid shows a scene with much less contrast - a very cloudy day - so it can show something much closer to the actual value range of real life. It has no overexposed or underexposed areas because the value range in the real scene was genuinely small: the darkest dark and the lightest light weren't far apart.

## Translucency

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L06 · 05:04–06:16

Translucency happens when a material is soft enough for a strong light to pass through it and illuminate it from the inside. It's most noticeable when someone has a light source behind them and their ears turn a shiny, bright red - you've probably seen this yourself, or experienced it with sunlight shining through your own hand. That bright red is light entering the flesh and shining back out through it, because the flesh is thin enough for light to actually penetrate and exit the other side, picking up the color of the flesh on the way - and the high blood content of skin and flesh is why the color comes out so red. The same thing happens through a cat's ear, and it also happens with thin materials such as leaves. Backlit leaves on a tree go very saturated because the sunlight is shining straight through them. It also happens with cloth, liquids, and hair.

## Reflections: how to spot them

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L06 · 06:17–07:59

Reflections happen when a surface is smooth enough that you can see bright things reflecting on it. A toy robot and a mug are both very smooth surfaces: as the viewer moves around them, the reflections move too, according to the viewer's position - and that's actually the easiest way to spot a reflection, because it changes depending on where you're standing. Reflections don't only happen on the materials you'd expect, like metal or porcelain - they also happen on wood. A small highlight on top of a wooden object moves slightly as the viewer moves, shifting from one side of a line to the other. What's being seen there is a lamp itself, reflecting on that smooth surface - not the surface being lit by the lamp, but the lamp itself, because it's bright enough to be seen even in a surface that isn't very reflective. A small plant nearby shows the same thing: the white glare on its surface isn't light affecting the surface, it's the lamp itself being reflected. This happens on skin, too, and on many materials, as long as they're smooth enough.

## Reflections in film, art and photography

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L06 · 08:00–13:27

Some reflections are obvious, like the ones on windows and glass, which we're used to seeing. But the eye also carries reflections, and so does the nose - what looks like light hitting the nose is actually the light source itself being seen reflected there, with the nose acting as a very faint mirror. This happens far more often than you might think: a line on the edge of a lip can be a reflection of a light source in exactly the same way. It's important to think of these as separate from light simply affecting a surface, because they're two different phenomena. A color that's just being lit will not change if you move around the character, but a highlight that's a reflection - on the nose, the eyes, anywhere - will move as your viewpoint changes. Get used to noticing this as you move through the world: any time the light on an object seems to change as you move around it, you're not seeing the light itself, you're seeing a reflection of it.

![1560 · U2 · L06 · 08:30](frames/U02L06-F0830.jpg)

Reflective materials are everywhere once you start looking. A car's reflections come from the traffic lights and street lights around it, and without them the car would look completely different, because its whole surface is really made up of reflections of the world around it. A faint highlight on skin can be a reflection of the sky above - the sky being a light source in its own right, especially between tall buildings that continue that reflection upward. A character lit by green light shows both the effect of that light on the skin and its actual reflection on the skin, lips and nose - and if you turned the character around, those reflections wouldn't stay in the same place, they'd move with the viewpoint.

An extreme example makes the same point in reverse: what you see reflected isn't only light sources, it's anything bright enough. In an eye, a bright area of the surroundings is visible reflected in the iris, while the darker area, like nearby trees, shows only the iris's own color rather than a reflection - because anything bright enough can become a reflection in another surface. Usually the highest value on any given surface - its brightest area - will be a reflection, which makes sense: it isn't the effect of light you're seeing there, it's the light itself being reflected.

## Painting a sphere: exposure, reflection and translucency

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L06 · 13:28–18:26

Before moving on, a quick demonstration on a simple ball pulls exposure, reflection and translucency together. Start a new layer for the ball itself, then a second layer clipped to it and set to Multiply.

![1560 · U2 · L06 · 13:47](frames/U02L06-F1347.jpg)

On screen: "Multiply" [rest omitted]  
Shows: A flat maroon-red circle - the base ball - about to be clipped to a Multiply layer for the ambient light.

First add the feeling of the sky as an ambient light source: pick a very bright blue and paint it on the Multiply layer. The ball goes very dark - that's expected, because this blue light is meant to be coming from everywhere. But assuming the ball is resting on a floor, the light can't reach the area where it touches the ground, so starting from that bright blue, remove the saturation and lower the value there, since that area needs to be darker. As the blue is pulled back, the ball's true red color shows through underneath, giving warmer tones wherever the blue ambient light isn't hitting - a smudge tool can help soften those transitions.

![1560 · U2 · L06 · 14:53](frames/U02L06-F1453.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: The base ball as a flat maroon-red circle on its own Multiply layer, not yet shaded; three hand-drawn arrows above it mark the direction the ambient light is about to come from.

Now add the sun. Painting white on a Multiply layer has no visible effect - it effectively erases whatever is underneath, so toggling the layer on and off makes no difference wherever white has been painted. Because of this, white can be used to remove the shadow in a chosen area, and the result still feels natural, since that area is still lit by the ambient light rather than going flat black. On top of this, assuming a white light source like the sun, a small white highlight - a reflection - can be added. That single reflection instantly changes the read of the ball from a soft material to a very hard, smooth one. Changing that same reflection to a blurry one instead makes the ball read as a different, softer material - maybe rubber.

![1560 · U2 · L06 · 15:35](frames/U02L06-F1535.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: The same ball with a small, sharp white highlight painted near the top - a hard, glossy reflection that reads as a smooth, shiny material.

Duplicating the ball sets up a second material test: skin. The exact same Multiply ambient-light layer and process can be reused on the new ball. If not enough of the local color shows through, reducing the saturation of the ambient light reveals more of the natural color underneath. White again removes the shadow and brings up the light - but because skin is a translucent material, showing that transition properly means bringing in a reddish tone on the same multiply layer, softly mixed in, the way light picks up the colors inside skin as it passes through. Getting the right amount of color takes some practice before it feels natural; picking the color right at the transition and bringing up its saturation slightly helps.

![1560 · U2 · L06 · 16:08](frames/U02L06-F1608.jpg)

On screen: "Hue: 0", "Saturation: -25", "Lightness: 0" [rest omitted]  
Shows: The finished glossy red ball on the left; beside it, a second, duplicated ball being desaturated toward a pale pink flesh tone inside a selection, ready for its own ambient-light pass.

Assuming light is hitting the floor and bouncing back into the ball, that bounced color can be picked, brightened slightly and softly brushed into the shadow area as bounce light. A reflection can then be added to help the ball read as skin, but it needs to be very subtle, because skin isn't especially reflective. As a general rule for skin, it helps to have more warmth in the deep shadows - selecting the deepest shadow and shifting it toward a warmer color brings that warmth in.

![1560 · U2 · L06 · 17:49](frames/U02L06-F1749.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: Both balls side by side - the glossy red one and the flesh-toned one, now shaded with the same ambient-light gradient and softened so the pink ball reads as skin rather than plastic.

Keep practicing these basic exercises until these fundamental principles feel completely comfortable, because they'll help a great deal with more complex paintings later on. The next lesson covers preparing the drawing used for the rest of the course.

### Key points (derived)

- Exposure is a choice, not a fact: a painting can only ever show a narrow slice of real-world contrast, so decide deliberately whether to expose for the light or for the shadows in a given scene.
- Translucency (light passing through a thin or soft material) and reflection (a bright object's image bouncing off a smooth surface) are two distinct phenomena, both easy to confuse with a surface simply being "lit."
- The giveaway for a reflection is that it moves as the viewpoint changes, while a surface's lit local color does not - train yourself to notice this in everyday life.
- Wet and plastic surfaces reflect more because both increase how smooth a surface is; the brightest spot on a surface is usually a reflection rather than the effect of light on it.
- A practical build order for a form: an ambient/environment light on a Multiply layer, desaturated and darkened where light can't reach; a hard white highlight for a glossy reflection (blurred for a softer material); and, for translucent materials like skin, a reddish tone mixed at the light/shadow transition, a subtle reflection, and extra warmth in the deep shadows.
