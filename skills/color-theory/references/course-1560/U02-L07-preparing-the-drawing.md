# Lesson 07 · Preparing the drawing

## The character to draw

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L07 · 00:08–01:46

With the theory done, this lesson sets up the character to paint: a simple character made of basic volumes, posed on a very minimalistic background.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 00:08](frames/U02L07-F0008.jpg)

On screen: "SAMUEL SMITH" / "Preparing the drawing"  
Shows: Lesson title card over a sketch of a train platform with a steam engine and three travellers, one partly cropped at the right edge.

The drawing to prepare for the course, and to paint later on, needs to be a simple character posed on top of a very simple background. The point isn't to make a pretty picture or an interesting composition - it's to make something simple enough to paint over and over, which is why small details should be left out: they only slow you down. The character needs to be made of simple volumes; this matters a lot if you want to learn to paint properly, so don't make complex shapes. Think of the character in terms of spheres and cylinders of different shapes - this really helps with painting the volume correctly later on.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 01:06](frames/U02L07-F0106.jpg)

Once the first version is done, the plan is to paint a second background that's a very simple interior, since it's interesting to see how light behaves indoors as opposed to outdoors. Anyone who just wants to use the course to learn to paint, without drawing their own character, can use the teacher's own drawing, provided along with a second alternative character.

## Local colors and the white test

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L07 · 01:47–03:24

The next step is painting the local colors of the scene: the color an object would take if a plain white light were simply shone on it - so a red shirt just gets painted red, with no volume information yet. That's just the color of the objects; the volume comes later, when light is painted on top, and having local colors in place first makes it much easier to paint different lighting setups afterward. This local-color layer goes underneath the line layer, and there's no need to worry too much if a color isn't exactly right - it can always be adjusted later.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 02:18](frames/U02L07-F0218.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: The finished, clean line drawing of the character standing in front of a rock wall with leaves, with no color yet applied.

It's important to make the different local colors quite saturated, because as much color information as possible is needed - adding lighting later can make colors noticeably less saturated, so saturation here really means how much color information is available to work with.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 03:11](frames/U02L07-F0311.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: The character with a first pass of local color - pale skin tone and a plain white T-shirt - painted on a layer under the line drawing, which is still otherwise a rough sketch.

The white T-shirt is a particularly interesting object, because since light reflects all colors, it will show the effects of different lighting very clearly - it's made pure white as a local color, and because of that, it will always be the brightest object in the scene: the brighter a color is, the more reflective it is, which means it reflects every other color perfectly.

## Loose shapes and skin variation

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L07 · 03:25–05:24

Precise shapes don't need much attention at this stage. It's a common mistake for beginners to try to define everything very precisely, but painting is really an optical illusion that gives the impression that things are there - a vague indication of certain details is enough to make them read, and it's much easier to paint that way. The skin's local color can be made a bit more saturated and pushed slightly toward red for the local color of the nose - the difference shouldn't be too big, but a little color difference helps make a character feel more alive; without it, evenly-colored skin tends to feel like a statue, or like it's made of plastic. The same kind of variation goes on the cheeks and the elbows, and hands tend to have a bit more blood inside them, so they can be a little more red as well.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 04:26](frames/U02L07-F0426.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: The character's face and torso with rosy, more saturated color on the cheeks and nose against the paler base skin tone, and the white T-shirt now filled in; the eyes are still just grey line-drawing pupils, not yet colored.

A useful trick for the face: with the lasso tool, select the eyes from the line drawing, cut them (Command-X) and paste them back in the same place (Command-Shift-V). That way, when the line drawing is later removed, the eyes stay in place - their color is generally black anyway, and it's easy to paint over them afterward, for example to make the iris a greenish color. This saves having to repaint those very precise shapes a second time, since if they're already correct in the drawing, they can simply be reused.

## Hair, hat and finding color by feel

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L07 · 05:25–06:44

Hair is chosen next - dark brown in this case. This isn't meant to be the most original character; its purpose is just to give something to learn painting on. Precise shapes aren't a priority here either: as a personal preference, the character feels more alive painted with deliberate brushstrokes rather than with precise selections made perfectly neat - a slightly more organic look is preferred. For the hat, a greenish color is tried, then reconsidered in favor of red. Finding the exact color and value for local colors can be a little tricky at first, and colors may need adjusting once the lighting is painted in, but judging the right value becomes more instinctive with practice.

## One layer, and local color vs. volume

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L07 · 06:45–08:41

All of these local colors are painted on a single layer. It can be tempting to separate everything into different layers for more control, but in the end that tends to be a waste of time - repainting over things doesn't take long, especially on a character this simple, so it's worth doing all the character's local colors on one layer.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 07:31](frames/U02L07-F0731.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: The character with local colors filled in across skin, hair, cap, white T-shirt and green trousers, all on a single color layer beneath the line drawing; the shoes are still unpainted.

It also matters to think clearly about what counts as local color and what counts as volume. The nostrils, for example, could be painted in as a different color, but they aren't actually a different color from the rest of the nose - they're simply darker because they're a hole that light doesn't reach, so they should appear once volume and lighting are painted in, not as a local-color choice now. It's worth removing the line layer every so often just to check whether the shapes are reading correctly, and adjusting from that view if needed. A square "Color Dynamics" brush can add a little texture and color variation - for instance, shifting a bit more toward yellow and adding saturation on the knees, as if the character had been playing outside in the mud. A slightly lighter version of the trousers' color is used to indicate the inside of the rolled-up cuffs; skin color is reused for the small bit of ankle showing there, and the shoes are a reddish brown with white socks.

## Finishing the character

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L07 · 08:42–10:07

Flipping the canvas (Image > Image Rotation > Flip Canvas Horizontal, or the Command-H / Control-H shortcut) is a useful habit for checking whether shapes look right, since it helps refresh the eye. A small metallic button is added, and the bottom of the cap is given a slightly different color to help separate its shapes.

The character doesn't need perfectly precise shapes at this stage - as long as it generally follows the drawing, the volumes will read correctly once lighting is added, so there's no need to overthink it if the drawing itself is correct; it's just worth checking that both sides of the character are aligned so it reads as one coherent object.

## Background local colors: grass, rocks and leaves

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L07 · 10:08–14:52

With the character's local colors finished, a new layer is started for the background, kept separate from the character's local colors.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 10:22](frames/U02L07-F1022.jpg)

On screen: "bg local colors" [rest omitted]  
Shows: The fully colored character standing in front of the still-uncolored line drawing of the rock wall and grass, with a new, empty "bg local colors" layer ready underneath.

Grass is picked in green, and grass can take on many different shades, so it's worth experimenting with lighter and darker variations - it's an organic material and should feel a little random rather than a perfectly even color, unlike, say, a football field. Since this is meant to be wild countryside grass, a stripey brush is used to give a bit of texture and interest, without painting individual blades.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 10:37](frames/U02L07-F1037.jpg)

On screen: [rest omitted]  
Shows: The first patch of green grass color painted in at the base of the scene, beneath the character, with the rocks and leaves behind still only line drawing.

The rocks behind are given a warm-leaning color, but with very low saturation, since rocks are generally gray - though they can lean a bit more toward brown. There's no single definitive rock color; some can look a bit cooler and some a bit warmer. Bringing the line layer to the top and lowering its opacity makes it easier to see what's being painted underneath. Comparing local colors directly against each other is a good way to judge how light or dark they should be - for instance, checking whether a rock should read as darker than the character's trousers or his skin; converting the view to black and white with the earlier shortcut makes such value comparisons easier. Each rock is given a slightly different color so that the rocks feel like separate stones gathered from around the area, rather than one uniform mass.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 12:03](frames/U02L07-F1203.jpg)

On screen: "bg local colors" [rest omitted]  
Shows: The same scene converted to black and white, making it easier to compare how light or dark the rock, grass and character colors read against each other.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 12:30](frames/U02L07-F1230.jpg)

On screen: [rest omitted]  
Shows: The rock wall now colored in warm, low-saturation grays and browns, each rock a slightly different shade, with green climbing leaves painted in front of part of it and grass filling the foreground.

The transition between the rocks and the grass should be a little fuzzy rather than sharply defined, since grass has soft edges and isn't a solid material - it's made of many small blades that aren't painted individually, so the join is treated as a soft transition instead. Colors are adjusted continually by comparing how they feel next to each other - for example, making the grass a bit darker than a neighboring rock, just enough to read as separate. More saturation is added in some areas, and the Color Dynamics brush again helps add variety. Moss on the rocks is generally darker than the rock it sits on, so its color is picked from the rock, darkened, and textured in with the same variation brush. It's important to make sure every area is fully covered with no holes, since gaps would let the background layer show through and look strange. The leaves are given a more green, fairly dark local color; once again, precise shapes aren't the priority - a rough impression is enough, and if the color, value and lighting are right, it will still read as the right thing (this doesn't rule out a more precise drawing - it's simply the teacher's personal preference for a looser look). Hiding the line drawing at this point reveals a few remaining holes to fix, and neighboring clumps of leaves are differentiated a little more from each other. The result looks quite flat at this stage, which is expected - it's only the base that the different lighting scenarios will be painted onto later.

## The interior version: walls, ceiling and floor

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L07 · 14:53–17:36

For the interior version, a new layer is started and the opacity of the line drawing is lowered.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 15:09](frames/U02L07-F1509.jpg)

On screen: "interior local colors" [rest omitted]  
Shows: The line drawing for the simple interior room - a corner with a window strip, a small table holding a vase, and the colored character standing against the bare walls - with no local color painted in yet.

The wall is given a very saturated color, then lightened a bit; the ceiling is a light gray. The transitions between wall, ceiling and floor aren't painted perfectly straight, even though the line drawing itself was made with a ruler - because the painting itself isn't done holding Shift for straight lines, it's painted a little more freely, which keeps the room from feeling too rigid and helps tie everything together, including the line at the bottom of the wall.

![1560 · U2 · L07 · 15:46](frames/U02L07-F1546.jpg)

On screen: [rest omitted]  
Shows: The interior wall now filled in a saturated teal, with the floor, window frame and furniture still only line drawing.

The floor is a wooden one, in browns between orange and red, painted fairly dark. Wood is a material that shows a lot of natural variation, so the same brush is used to shift a little more toward red in some spots and more toward yellow in others - as long as those hue, saturation and value changes stay small, the floor still reads as the same overall wood color. Any remaining gaps are filled in, and the floor is given a subtle plank-like variation: slightly different color shapes next to each other, textured in the direction of the wood grain. This, again, is only a difference in local color, not volume - a distinction that can feel a little odd to hold in mind while painting. Going back briefly to the character, his shoes turn out to be close to the same color as the new floor and start to get lost against it, so they're darkened to make sure they stand out.

## Interior details, and wrapping up local colors

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L07 · 17:37–20:47

The wall is treated as if it were a little old: thinking about what color might be underneath, a slightly warm gray is used to suggest patches where the paint - or perhaps it's wallpaper - is peeling off in places, adding a bit of visual interest. The ceiling is given a little variation too, with small changes so it doesn't feel too flat and even.

The window reuses that same wooden color for its frame; only a small part of it is visible in this view, and that visible sliver is deliberately erased so the sky can be seen through it later, once lighting is painted in - at this stage nothing is darkened or otherwise treated differently there, since that separation will only appear once volume is added.

A separate layer is used for the table, to make it easier to paint later; it's given a darker wood tone, and painting it needs to follow the room's perspective fairly precisely so that it still reads correctly once the line drawing is removed. The tabletop is painted slightly blue, since it will be treated as a reflective material like porcelain - a way of indicating materials through reflections that comes up later in the course. A vase on the table gets a floral pattern; the exact pattern doesn't matter much, it just needs to carry a bit of visual information. Green leaves are added, and then bright yellow flowers, made bright enough to stand out clearly.

That's local colors finished for the interior - not the most exciting part of the process, but an important step, and one that will be very useful for the next lesson, in which the first lighting scenario, a sunny day, gets painted on top of this base.

### Key points (derived)

- Build the reference drawing from simple volumes (spheres and cylinders) and keep the background minimal - detail slows down repeated practice and isn't the point of the exercise.
- Local color is the object's plain, evenly-lit color with no light or shadow information; keep it fairly saturated, since adding light later will desaturate it.
- A white object is always the scene's brightest local color, because the brighter a color is, the more completely it reflects every other color.
- Small, deliberate color variation (nose, cheeks, hands; individual rocks; wood grain) keeps organic surfaces from reading as flat, uniform plastic.
- Keep local color and volume mentally separate: shading, cast shadows and details like nostrils belong to the lighting pass, not to the local-color layer.
- Practical shortcuts worth reusing: cut-and-paste eyes onto their own layer so they survive removing the line drawing; flip the canvas to catch shape errors; hide the line layer periodically to check the color shapes alone; convert to black and white to compare values.
