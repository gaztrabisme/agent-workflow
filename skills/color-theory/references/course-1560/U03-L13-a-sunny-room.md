## Intro and the warm base layer

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L13 · 00:00–01:19

This lesson brightens things up, painting the same room under a nice sunny atmosphere. The approach here is to expose for shadow, really emphasizing how bright and vivid the light is.

As usual, the first step is a multiply layer for each element of the scene, using a warm color, because the light coming in through the window is going to bounce around the room and create this warm atmosphere. On the character's right side, he gets the influence from that window — the color of the sky — so the search is for whichever warm color feels most natural; the first attempt isn't bright enough, since that side should read brighter than the other.

## The strong direct light

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L13 · 01:19–02:31

The strong light source needs to go in right away, since it will be needed to work out how light bounces around the rest of the room. A linear dodge layer is created, just as was done for the sunlight outside, with the light coming from this direction. Some of it is erased to preserve the volume: the arm casts a shadow, and everything else nearby is in full light, except where it falls away from the light; folds are added to the clothing as well.

## Bounce light onto the face

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L13 · 02:31–03:06

Because there's a very strong light in an otherwise quite dark environment, a strong bounce light comes off it — the light bounces on the t-shirt and comes back up into the character's chin. Back on the multiply layer, the same influence shows up on the bottom of the nose and even in the brow area, since these are surfaces that face somewhat downward.

In the background, some of the window's light illuminates the wall too, painted with a very, very soft brush, because even though it's coming from the right, it is very diffused.

## The sunbeam through the window

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L13 · 04:16–04:56

This bright light also has to hit the wall behind, so a new linear dodge layer is added, with the light coming in roughly like this. The shape of the light doesn't need to be exact — it can be quite unpredictable how light comes through a window — and as long as it doesn't look completely out of place, being inexact is fine. The light is then removed wherever the character casts a shadow.

## Bounce light on the floor and reflective surfaces

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L13 · 04:57–06:30

The strong light on the floor is going to bounce around the same way it does on the character, so the bounce-light color is sampled, and the lasso tool is used to make a selection that keeps the brightening confined to the right surface, really showing that the light is bouncing on the floor. A bit of that bounce could show elsewhere too, very subtly — the further from the light, the darker it gets.

The character's face is quite reflective, so it picks up that sky color as well, and the roses on the table get the same treatment. This still needs reflections to look fully proper, which will be covered later; the guiding rule throughout is simply to think about how much light a particular surface is receiving, whether through direct light, bounce light, or diffuse light — that's really all there is to know. A color that looks a bit dirty can be made to look more natural by increasing its saturation.

## Reading the volumes from afar; the hierarchy of light on the face

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L13 · 06:30–07:57

Looking at the painting from a distance, one area looks a bit odd and needs to be blended better; the mouth shape needs attention too — the cheek just above the mouth faces slightly downward, so it catches a touch of that light. Painting faces calls for a lot of subtlety, but if the hierarchy of light is respected, it will always work: knowing which surface you're painting, which way it's facing, and how much light it's receiving is enough to figure out how it should look.

## Diffused shadows: making the character feel present

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L13 · 07:57–08:33

The character is still blocking the light from reaching a certain area behind him, so a diffused shadow is painted there; shadows like this really help sell the idea that he is present in the room, influencing the space around him. The same treatment goes under the table: there's a clear direction to the shadow, but it stays very soft. The airbrush can be a little tricky to control this way and takes some getting used to, but it's very useful for this kind of soft shadow.

This type of lighting is very satisfying to paint, and the scene could even be pushed brighter still, with a bit more bounce light added here and there. The character gets more bounce light from the left than from the bottom, because the light hits his shirt and bounces back into his arm; the volume of his belly is followed closely to keep this reading correctly.

## Key takeaway: bounce light sells the realism

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L13 · 11:06–11:46

When painting this kind of lighting situation, adding the hard, direct light is the easy part. What actually sells the realism and believability of the scene is how the light bouncing around the room is painted. Bear in mind that bounce light is always strongest on the surfaces right next to the one that's being hit directly by the sun. The next lesson returns to the very first sunny scene to add a few details that push its realism and believability further.

### Key points (derived)

- Exposing for shadow (rather than for the light) keeps a sunlit scene reading as bright and vivid.
- The strong direct light source should be placed early, since every other light decision in the room is worked out relative to it.
- Bounce light from a bright surface (the lit shirt) shows up on nearby downward-facing surfaces: chin, nose, brow.
- Surfaces facing away from all light sources, and the wall the light itself comes from, read darker — this is what builds the room's volume.
- The exact shape of light through a window doesn't need to be accurate; only the impression of natural, unpredictable light matters.
- Bounce light should be confined to the correct surface with a selection (lasso), sampled from the strong light's own color.
- Reflective surfaces (skin, flowers) pick up the color of the light around them.
- Diffused, soft shadows under a character or object help sell that it is physically present in the space.
- The single guiding question for any surface is: how much light is it receiving, and through which kind (direct, bounce, or diffuse)?
- Bounce light is strongest on the surfaces immediately adjacent to the one receiving direct light — this detail, more than the hard light itself, is what makes a lit scene believable.
