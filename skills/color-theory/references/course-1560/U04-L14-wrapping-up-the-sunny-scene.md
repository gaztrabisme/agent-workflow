## Why add reflections and translucency now

Anchor: 1560 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–00:36

The lighting for the sunny scene has already been set up by defining the different light sources, but the picture still isn't complete. Going back to that scene, this lesson adds the effect of light reflecting off surfaces and light passing through surfaces, so that the different materials in the scene read as distinct from one another.

## Painting translucency in skin and cloth

Anchor: 1560 · U4 · L14 · 00:37–02:55

Translucency happens when a strong light source shines through a translucent material such as skin or cloth. In skin, what is illuminated is understood to be the blood inside the body, so the brightest possible reddish color is used with an airbrush on a Multiply layer, worked in around the places where the light hits. Inside the ear, the skin is thin enough that light shines straight through it, so that area can be pushed to almost completely red. The same treatment goes further down the arm, and especially on the fingers, where the translucency is strongest; darkening the base tone slightly first, then adding the red on top, helps it read more clearly. The same effect happens again without color, where sunlight passes through the t‑shirt and lands on the skin underneath.

## Setting up reflections and adding them to skin and hair

Anchor: 1560 · U4 · L14 · 02:56–04:15

Reflections, unlike translucency, happen on very hard, very smooth surfaces. A new layer is added on top of everything, with the eyedropper set to sample all layers so color can be picked from anything already painted. The color of the sky is sampled and pushed a little further, then applied very lightly to surfaces that could plausibly be reflective: the tip of the nose, and, extremely subtly, the skin in general — subtle enough that the character doesn't start to feel like it's made of metal or plastic. Where a reflection comes from the sun itself, the color used is warm and close to white; in the hair, for example, it is painted on and then partly erased back until the amount feels right. Getting the balance right is tricky, but it matters, because it is what makes the different materials read clearly.

## Reflections in the eyes, face edge and ear

Anchor: 1560 · U4 · L14 · 04:16–05:07

A highlight from the sun can go into the eye — not because the sun would necessarily reflect there, but because it helps the eyes feel more alive. The same sky-colored highlight goes on the edge of the face and on the ear, applied sparingly and without overdoing it, since reflections get out of hand quickly if pushed too far. Each small highlight helps describe the material a little better.

## Reflective materials: shoes, grass and leaves

Anchor: 1560 · U4 · L14 · 05:08–05:58

A darker blue is used for the shoes, which read as a harder, more reflective material; they get darker still toward the sole, where the grass the character is standing in reflects a touch of green into them. Metal surfaces can be taken much further than this, because they are more complicated and pick up reflections from everywhere. The leaves on the wall also pick up some reflection from the sky, but darker than the sky itself, since the leaves are a darker material to begin with.

## Highlight technique, final touches and clouds

Anchor: 1560 · U4 · L14 · 05:59–07:38

To paint a highlight on an object, it helps to first paint the full shape solidly and then erase back out of it; this tends to give the reflection a more believable form than painting the highlight directly. That technique is used to adjust the highlight in the eyes, easing it back where it starts to look wrong, leaving just enough to indicate the volume. A few clouds are added by taking the color of the sky and desaturating it. Pure white is usually best avoided for clouds, but because this scene is exposed for the shadows, everything is already bright, so pure white still reads acceptably here. As a last touch, a slight reflection is added on an edge. Overall, the reflections don't add very much, but what they do add gives the materials more credibility, and getting better at judging the right amount comes mainly from observing how different real materials react to light.

### Key points (derived)

- Translucency reads as a warm, reddish glow in thin, light-permeable areas (ear, fingers) and can appear colorless where light passes through cloth onto skin.
- Reflections belong on hard, smooth surfaces; sample their color from the surrounding environment (sky, sun) and keep them extremely subtle to avoid a metallic or plastic look.
- Painting a highlight's full shape first and then erasing back gives a more believable result than drawing the highlight directly.
- Different materials (skin, hair, shoes, metal, leaves) take reflection differently; judging the right amount comes mainly from observing real materials.
