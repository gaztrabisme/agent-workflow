# Lesson 10 · A Cloudy Day

## Why a cloudy day is tricky

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L10 · 00:00–00:26

This lesson covers painting a cloudy day. It does not look like much on the surface, but it is one of the hardest lighting situations to paint, because there is no direct light and therefore no hard shadows to describe volume with.

## Removing direct light and turning the sky gray

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L10 · 00:26–01:53

Starting from the sunny-day file, save it as a new version, then delete the direct-light layer. Go back into the ambient layer and remove every trace of bounce light and warmth, erasing it back to the normal sky color. A little more shadow overall helps too, since the general level of light in the scene is going to be lower. Replace the background with a real gray.

With the warm bounce light gone, only the light from the sky remains, so desaturate it a little: on a cloudy day the light is not coming from a blue sky, it is just generally white light, so it stays cold enough that the shadows still read as warm, but nowhere near as blue as a sunny day's skylight. Remove some of the saturation from the shadow color the same way.

## Painting clouds and softening the shadows

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L10 · 01:53–03:39

Paint in a few dark clouds — cloud transitions are useful for this — and, to push the feeling further, add a layer on top with a big, soft airbrush and a touch of fog, so the scene feels properly overcast.

![1560 · U3 · L10 · 02:26](frames/U03L10-F0226.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: Soft gray clouds painted into the now-overcast sky, with the character's colors still unchanged.

Add slightly stronger shadows, still very soft, to emphasize that there simply is not much light in the scene — that is the question to keep asking: how much light is actually in this scene? Here it is quite low, since there is no sunlight and so no direct light source, which means the shadows in general get darker. That is the cloudy day, essentially: a touch of atmospheric perspective on the mountains, and remembering to shape them too.

## Levels and the soft contact shadow

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L10 · 03:39–04:17

Opening Levels (Image > Adjustments > Levels) lets the shadows be pushed stronger without repainting them. The character still needs a very soft shadow underneath him, since he is still blocking some light from reaching the ground — but it has to stay very soft, because anything stronger would read as a direct light source, which a cloudy day does not have.

## Key takeaways

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L10 · 04:17–05:05

Painting diffused light like this can feel overwhelming at first, since it goes against most of the usual assumptions about light: there is no defined light side or shadow side, and volume has to be described with only very small amounts of darkness. It takes practice, and it helps to actually go out and observe a cloudy day, watching how it behaves, to build an intuition for it. The next lesson moves to painting the character at night, lit by a lantern and moonlight.

### Key points (derived)

- A cloudy day has no direct light source, so there are no hard shadows — volume comes from very small, very soft amounts of darkness instead.
- Removing the sunlight and bounce-light layers and desaturating the ambient shadow turns a sunny-day file into an overcast one without repainting from scratch.
- Overcast light is close to white rather than blue, so the shadows stay only mildly warm, not as blue-shadowed as under a clear sky.
- The core question for this lighting situation is simply "how much light is in the scene" — on a cloudy day, the answer is "not much," so shadows should be sparse, soft and generally dark rather than sharply defined.
- Even a diffused scene needs a soft contact shadow under the character to ground it, as long as it stays soft enough not to read as a direct light source.
