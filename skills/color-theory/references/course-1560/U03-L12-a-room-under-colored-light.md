## Painting the green ambient light

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L12 · 01:09–02:23

Imagine there's a green light somewhere off-screen illuminating the whole scene, bouncing around the room and tinting everything with a green tint. The technique is the same multiply approach as before, one layer for each element, using a green that isn't too saturated. The lamp itself is erased from this tint, because it's a light source and isn't receiving light — its own light is already stronger than the ambient green. That said, it looks better a bit more saturated. Getting darker and a touch warmer in places, the direction of this light is assumed to come from roughly where the viewer is, heading straight at the character.

## Shadow direction and blending technique

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L12 · 02:23–04:00

Because the light comes from roughly the viewer's position, surfaces facing away from it get darker — which also means the character casts a very soft shadow behind him, painted with the airbrush. Blending with the smudge tool needs care, because it easily gets too soft and the volumes become unreadable. The table also casts a shadow, and things get noticeably darker underneath it, although some areas nearby are still getting full light; the working assumption is a fairly large light source — maybe a screen, like a giant TV showing a green image, casting light the way a screen does.

## Adding the red light source

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L12 · 04:00–05:54

Once the general green ambient lighting is in place, it's time to add the second light source: the color is taken directly from the lamp itself and tried on the character. It initially looks too powerful, so it's made a bit darker and less saturated. Because this is a small light source, it falls off quickly — strong close to the lamp, and much weaker further away.

![1560 · U3 · L12 · 04:28](frames/U03L12-F0428.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: The character's arm and side lit with a strong red-orange glow sampled directly from the lamp, close beside the lit lava lamp.

The red hat reacts quite strongly to this light, since its color is close to the lamp's own color; the nose picks up a bit of it too. Wherever the light is painted in, the shadow underneath is erased at the same time, because that area is no longer in shadow — it's simply receiving a different-colored light. This shows that the same logic applies no matter what the lighting situation is: the table could catch a bit of the red light too, and even the eye might pick up a touch of that color. On the arm, farthest from the lamp, only a very faint hint of the light is needed.

## Refining details and the hierarchy of light

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L12 · 05:54–07:25

A few ambient-light details still need fixing: the nostrils (always forgotten) help define the shape of the nose, and the cheek needs its shape reinforced too. Everywhere that doesn't directly face the light source needs to be a little darker.

![1560 · U3 · L12 · 06:41](frames/U03L12-F0641.jpg)

On screen: [none]  
Shows: A close-up of the character's face, refining the nostril and cheek shading where the red light and green ambient light meet.

All of this shows how simple it really is to color any kind of lighting situation, as long as the logic and hierarchy of light are respected: the strongest light source always wins. Here, wherever the lamp's red light reaches the character, it overpowers the green ambient light. A bit more shadow is added at this point, because this is a low-light environment — the only sources are the green ambient light and the small red lamp — shadows can get extremely dark, almost black, since there isn't enough light bouncing around the scene to fully illuminate them.

## Finishing touches and the takeaway

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L12 · 07:25–08:34

The shadow under the character's hand is strengthened so it stands out, since it was starting to disappear against a background of a very similar value — and that finishes this lighting scenario.

On screen: [none]

The colored lights in this scene were exaggerated on purpose, to show that this method really does work with any type of light, even if it's a completely green or completely red light. The principles stay the same, and following the logic will always get you there. The next lesson goes back to this same room, this time with sunshine coming in through the window and bouncing around.

### Key points (derived)

- With no outside light, every light in the scene is artificial and can be assigned any color.
- A surface that is itself a light source (the lamp) doesn't receive the ambient tint — its own light is already stronger.
- Light direction determines which surfaces darken: facing away from the source means darker, with a soft shadow cast behind the subject.
- The smudge tool can oversoften volumes if used carelessly; blend with care.
- Color sampled straight from a light source, then darkened and desaturated, reads as that light hitting a surface.
- Small light sources fall off quickly with distance; distant surfaces should only hint at the color.
- The strongest light source always overrides a weaker one wherever both would affect the same surface.
- In a low-light scene with few light sources, shadows can go almost black, since little light is left to bounce into them.
