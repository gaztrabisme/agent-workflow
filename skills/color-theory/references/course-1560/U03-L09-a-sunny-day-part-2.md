## Starting point: changing the light's direction

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:40

The previous lesson lit the character from behind, with the sun mostly hidden. This lesson asks what happens if the light comes from the front instead — simply brightening everything would overblow the image and stop making sense, so a new approach is needed. Save the file as a new document, delete the direct sunlight layer, and strip the ambient Multiply layer of all its bounce light and warmth, back to a plain shadow with no light added.

With Command+U (Hue/Saturation), darken everything. This changes the exposure: if the shadows are made this dark, the sky should logically go much darker too, even though it is still a sunny day — the difference is that this time the exposure is set for the light instead of the shadow, so much more information shows in the bright areas. Bring some of that blue influence back into the shadow, since the character is still, technically, in shadow at this stage.

## Painting light and shadow on the same layer

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L09 · 01:40–04:48

The big difference this time is that instead of creating a separate layer for the light, the same Multiply layer is used for both. Bring that layer to a similar level of darkness, then pick a very bright, almost unsaturated yellow: painting with pure white on a Multiply layer removes it entirely, so painting with this near-white yellow effectively erases the shadow and reveals the light beneath it — turning it on and off shows the difference clearly.

This is what exposing for light means: the shadows become very strong and very dark compared to the lights, and because the jump between the two is so sharp, a transition color is needed. For skin, pick a warm color that sits in value between the light and the shadow, and use it as a softer, in-between shadow rather than jumping straight to the deep tone; this gives a natural transition between light and shadow instead of a false hard edge everywhere. The blue influence used elsewhere barely affects red areas, since red sits far from blue on the color wheel.

This lighting scenario is trickier to paint because light and shadow now live on the same layer, so more care is needed with each stroke — in general, learning to control color and brushwork well enough to paint everything on one layer is a useful skill on its own. Shadows carry noticeably more saturation here too, since they are so much darker.

## Applying the technique to the background

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L09 · 04:48–06:49

The same approach carries over to the background. Top-facing surfaces sit completely in light, including the grass, with perhaps a few tiny shadows under the rocks; everywhere else shows only hard shadows, because this is very hard light coming directly from the sun. Switch between the light color and the shadow color as needed — the history panel is a quick way to go back to a previous color — and remember to remove light wherever something is casting a shadow onto another surface.

There is still a little bounce light on downward-facing surfaces, and the mountains follow the same logic as before: take the shadow color, lower its value, add a bit more saturation, and the result reads as a background mountain. That color sits close to the sky color because what is actually being painted is the sky in between the viewer and the mountains — this is called atmospheric perspective.

## Wrap-up

Anchor: 1560 · U3 · L09 · 06:49–07:14

Depending on where the light is hitting the scene, the exposure has to be adjusted each time to show the viewer the most information possible. The next lesson moves to a diffused light on a cloudy day.

### Key points (derived)

- Changing where the light comes from is as much an exposure decision as a color one: expose for shadow when the light source is hidden, expose for light when it is the dominant feature of the scene.
- Painting light and shadow on the same Multiply layer (using near-white to erase shadow and reveal light) works when the light is direct and strong enough to make the shadow the exception rather than the rule.
- A hard light/shadow transition needs an in-between transition color at the boundary, or the jump reads as a flat, unconvincing edge.
- A color's temperature affects how much a colored light influences it: blue light barely shifts red surfaces, since they are far apart on the color wheel.
- Distant background elements (mountains) should be tinted toward the sky color between the viewer and the object — atmospheric perspective.
