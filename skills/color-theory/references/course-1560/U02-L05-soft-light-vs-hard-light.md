## Soft light versus hard light

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L05 · 00:00–00:26

In this lesson I'm going to introduce the first concept of light theory in my opinion: the difference between soft light and hard light. This is a very important distinction that a lot of people don't know about.

## Hard light: direct sunlight makes sharp shadows

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L05 · 00:27–01:03

These are pictures of an apple I took under sunlight. You can see the light source clearly: the sun is just a little point in the sky, and it makes very sharp shadows. This is because the light rays are coming in parallel; when they hit the surface of the apple, they're stopped there, so the light rays that touch the apple can't reach the floor behind it. That's why we get such sharp shadows. This is what most people think of when they think about light: a simple, direct light source making sharp shadows.

## Soft light from the sky

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L05 · 01:04–03:43

But what happens when the sun isn't there — because it's behind a cloud, or you're in the shadow of a building? One of these apple photos was taken in a building's shadow, the other on a cloudy day, and the result is much the same: there's no direct light. The light illuminating the apple comes from sunlight diffracted through clouds, or through the sky in general. The sun lights up the whole sky, and the sky then disperses that light so it comes from everywhere at once.

Because the light comes from everywhere at once, we can't get sharp shadows. The only places that go dark are where the space gets very tight — small, confined areas the light has a harder time reaching.

It's important to understand that when sunlight is present, it isn't replacing the ambient skylight, it's added on top of it. If you select the shape sunlight would make and raise the brightness, you can see the skylight is still hitting the floor everywhere else, but the sunlight overpowers it wherever it falls, because it's a much stronger light source. The stronger light source always wins. The sky is a very weak light source compared to the sun, so a surface will look blue when it's only lit by the sky, but appear a warmer color when it's lit by the sun, because the sun is a warm light and so much stronger than the sky's color. You'll still see a little blue, but it's subtle, and more visible in real life than in photographs, since photos don't have the same light sensitivity as the human eye — so the sun's warmth tends to overpower the blue in a photo.

## A spectrum, not two categories

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L05 · 03:44–04:46

Soft light and hard light aren't separate things — they're more like a spectrum. The softest light you could get becomes ambient light, like the sky, which lights from everywhere at once; the opposite extreme is a direct light, like the sun, producing hard shadows. It's softer in one direction and harder in the other.

In between: if sunlight is going through a thin layer of cloud — thin enough that you still get directional light — the shadows are visible and fairly clear, but a little blurry. On the other side, if you have a big window in a room but the sun isn't shining directly through it, the ambient light from the sky comes in through the window and produces a very soft light with very soft shadows. It's not completely ambient, because it still has a direction — the light is still coming from the window — but it's very soft.

## Example: a perfectly overcast day

Anchor: 1560 · U2 · L05 · 04:47–05:52

Here's an example of a perfectly overcast day — very cloudy, with the light perfectly diffused everywhere. The two visible faces of this building are exactly the same color and value; if it weren't for the bricks and their shape, you wouldn't be able to tell there's a change of volume there at all. The light is generally coming from the top, but really from everywhere, so the only places that get darker are surfaces facing downward, or tight spots the light struggles to reach. The transition is always gradual — never a sharp shadow, always a soft one. It's tricky to paint because you have to be extremely subtle, but it's important to understand how this kind of lighting works.

Most of these images are actually lit quite softly. This is worth keeping in mind, because in illustration we tend to put strong sunlight everywhere, just because it's an easy way to describe things with shadows — but in real life it doesn't actually happen that often. Now that you know about the difference between soft light and hard light, in the next lesson we'll talk about exposure, reflections, and translucency, and how they work.

### Key points (derived)

- Hard light comes from a small, distant, direct source (the sun) whose rays arrive parallel, producing sharp, well-defined shadows.
- Soft/ambient light comes from a large or diffuse source (the sky, an overcast day) whose light arrives from many directions at once, so shadows are gradual and only very confined or downward-facing spots go dark.
- Soft and hard light are two ends of one spectrum, not a binary; direction plus softness can vary independently (e.g. sunlight through thin cloud: directional but blurry; skylight through a big window: soft but still directional).
- Direct light adds on top of ambient light rather than replacing it, and the stronger source always dominates the visible color and shadow.
- A subject only lit by the sky reads cooler/bluer; the same subject in direct sun reads warmer, because the sun overpowers the sky's color.
- Across the painting and film examples, only a minority use strong direct sunlight — most real-world and cinematic lighting is some variation of soft, diffused, ambient light.
