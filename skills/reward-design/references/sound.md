# Sound, voice and silence

Reader: a designer planning an auditory cue alongside the message. A static graphic gains sound only through presentation or interaction. Hand the cue sheet to presentation/motion or UX/frontend for implementation. Tiers follow [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md); IDs resolve in [evidence-map.md](evidence-map.md). All cue recipes are inferences to test, not validated sound settings.

## Expectation and musical payoff

- Use Huron's ITPRA theory to annotate imagination (possible outcome), tension (preparation), prediction (expected arrival), reaction (immediate response) and appraisal (later interpretation). Compare deferred and immediate resolution. V assigns the scholarly theory T3; its cue-sheet translation has no controlled validation. (T3; C1)
- Audition the preceding phrase with the peak and compare it with starting at the peak. Selected emotional music listeners supplied dopamine-sensitive positron emission tomography (PET) evidence; finer anticipation/peak timing came from complementary functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which measures blood-oxygenation changes. No tested lead-in duration or audience dopamine estimate follows. (T1; C2)
- Try a relevant voice entrance, harmonic shift or texture change at the reveal, then ask listeners where emotion changed. Natural-piece associations do not establish universal chills or a causal feature recipe. (T3; C3)
- Test music with listeners of different preferences and keep the answer understandable without a bodily response. Frisson means music-evoked chills; the small diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) study measured brain structure, hence corrected T1, but connectivity was correlational. It establishes no training effect or universal soundtrack response. (T1; C4)
- Compare reflective and more rhythmic beds at matched volume with the same narration. Tempo/activation associations were confounded by musical selections; no ideal beats per minute, liking or learning gain was established. (T3; C5)
- For an audience that values electronic dance music, audition its breakdown, buildup and return as a contrast-based payoff. Visual alignment is a hypothesis; natural excerpts do not isolate each device, and skin conductance measures arousal, meaning activation rather than liking. No universal bar count follows. (T3; C6)
- After stable context, audition a meaningful deviation; after ambiguity, audition resolution. Surprise and prior uncertainty interacted in pleasure ratings and selected brain regions, while the measured reward-related regions reflected uncertainty. This is not a more-surprise rule or dopamine assay. (T1; C25)

## Event cues and functional feedback

- Name the event pair before aligning it: impact/contact, visible speech, narrated reference-to-mark or musical transition-to-reveal. Align a relevant accent to the visible event as an initial target, then inspect normal-speed delivery. Narrated reference/mark alignment is a semantic cue check (R07); perceived audiovisual synchrony is R15. Physical offsets are supporting evidence, not a universal perceptual pass threshold. Synchrony depends on stimulus, task and adaptation. (T2; C7) (T1; B16, B18)
- Check visible mouth movements against replaced speech; use a relevant cutaway or matching rerecording when needed. McGurk syllable conflicts and stable listener differences support speech integration, not the claim that every small lip-sync error changes meaning. (T2; C8)
- Choose one semantically appropriate contact sound for a decisive movement or completion. Chion's synchresis means perceived fusion of coincident sound and image; it is film theory/craft, not an effect size or a reward mechanism. (T4; C9)
- If earcons are useful, teach distinct timbres (sound characters) and rhythms, then test recognition with competing cues. An earcon is a learned abstract sound code. Small studies do not establish it as the best default; later comparative synthesis often favors speech-based alerts. Provide equivalent visible status. (T3; C10)
- Test an everyday action sound as an auditory icon, a sound metaphor for an event. Physical analogies vary culturally and can annoy with repetition; the informal SonicFinder assessment does not establish superiority to earcons or speech. (T4; C11)
- Reserve success sounds for the user's actual net success. Gambling studies show sound can inflate perceived wins, including returns below the wager; do not transfer that into an ordinary-UI diagnosis. The corrected studies recruited 157 and analysed 148 in the later experiment; use the erratum if quoting win estimates. (T2; C12)

## Brand, voice and mix

- Prototype a stable, recognizable signature and test sound-only identification after realistic exposure. Intel's 1994 composition and 1995 debut are history, not proof of recognition uplift; its three seconds and five notes are examples. (T4; C13)
- Write a miniature preparation/arrival cue when it suits brand character, then test repeated listening. Netflix creator interviews describe intended tension/release; the 2024 page reposts a 2020 episode and is not the cue's creation date. Deployment is no causal audience result. (T4; C14)
- Compare comfortable neutral and expressive readings, marking the argument's contrast and answer. Pitch-range correlations in a small second-language speaker sample are not causal voice-training advice; no universal lower-pitch or hertz prescription follows. (T3; C15)
- Keep phrase boundaries and allow time for unfamiliar terms and numbers; test listeners' explanation of the argument. Speech-rate/charisma correlation was weak and nonsignificant, and a two-CEO comparison supplies no universal words-per-minute target. (T3; C16)
- Compare space before an unresolved answer with space after closure. Musical pauses were perceived differently after tonal closure; transferring that finding to narrative is untested. Choose prototype duration by comprehension and delivery context. (T3; C17)
- Compare compression variants at matched perceived loudness and preserve intelligible speech. Heavy compression generally harmed quality; mild compression could be preferred with unequal loudness. This establishes neither a streaming loudness target nor universal superiority of more dynamic range. (T3; C18)
- Match the music to the occasion and compare candidate moods with silence. Greeting-card purchase intentions do not establish real sales or a general happy-music advantage; cultural associations matter. (T3; C19)
- Translate specific brand attributes into a musical brief, then test fit without labels. V assigns T3 proof of concept: preselected music, training, small samples and forced-choice matching constrain Brodsky's four related studies. Do not claim independently replicated composition effectiveness or universal instrument meanings. (T3; C20)
- Test recall of the message separately from liking or recall of its music. Attention-grabbing music helped reception conditionally on congruence, meaning that its associations fitted the message; moving changes away from dense facts is a precaution, not the experiment's tested recipe. (T3; C21)
- Default to silence beneath dense reading; place music where it has an expressive role and compare the actual task with and without it. A meta-analysis found a small reading disadvantage but a null average across mixed outcomes. Transfer to narrated explainers or charts needs testing. (T1; C22)
- Remove competing lyrics beneath names, numbers and definitions, then compare instrumental music with silence. Digit-recall experiments found more vocal interference, while instrumental effects varied. Removing lyrics or lowering volume does not guarantee no interference. (T3; C23)

## Check before handoff

Record prerequisites separately: audio stream present, signal decoded, mix auditioned, stems known and listener task tested. A decoded waveform or LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale) describes the signal; it does not establish speech content, intelligibility or mood. Supplied captions report text windows, not independently heard words. Caption-derived rates are text-window proxies; establish actual spoken pace by listening/transcription when it matters. (T3; C15, C16, C18)

Confirm a sound bed by audition or production evidence before proposing its bed/silence comparison. Unknown bed presence is not judged; absence makes that comparison inapplicable. If stems are unavailable, say whether comparable versions can be made and leave the effect test pending. Use [measure-playback.md](measure-playback.md) for loudness and signal events, and [review-checklist.md](review-checklist.md) for artifact/audience subchecks. (T1; C22) (T3; C23)

Give every cue a purpose, event location, lead-in, end and mute equivalent; listen to the rendered delivery with the actual narration. (T3; C1) (T2; C7) Reject an intelligence claim from selecting Mozart; task-specific temporary effects do not establish a lasting general gain. (T1; C24)
