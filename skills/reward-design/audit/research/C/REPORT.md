## Least certain entries and why

- C1 (ITPRA): a scholarly framework is useful, but the proposed cue-sheet/editing workflow was not directly tested. T4 is deliberately conservative for the design recipe.
- C3 and C6 (chills and EDM drops): small samples and natural music make musical-feature causality uncertain. Arousal and chills do not establish reward value for a new audience.
- C4 (connectivity/frisson): 10 participants per group and postselection exclusions; correlational DTI cannot establish the cause of audience differences or a way to change them.
- C7 (synchrony): the tutorial review summarizes controlled evidence, but stimulus-dependent windows do not translate into one portable millisecond target. T2 refers to the underlying experimental literature, not to a meta-analysis.
- C9, C11, C13, and C14 (synchresis, auditory icons, Intel, Netflix): valuable craft/history, labelled T4. Public case descriptions and creator interviews do not establish effect sizes or causal recall gains.
- C15 and C16 (voice): acoustic correlates of charisma are not experiments establishing a universal pitch or words-per-minute target. The Rosenberg–Hirschberg rate correlation was weak and nonsignificant (r = 0.094, p = 0.0902); a secondary summary had overstated it, and the final entry follows the primary paper. Speaker identity, language, gender stereotypes, and content can confound listener ratings.
- C17 (silence): tonal closure experiments do not directly test a pause before a business reveal. The 0/1/2-beat recipe is an explicit prototype comparison, not a proven optimum.
- C19–C21 (advertising/branding): limited products and laboratory settings; intention, fit, reception, and actual sales are different outcomes. Brodsky is not cited as proving advertising recall.
- C25 (expectation/pleasure): fMRI includes reward regions, so the study meets the supplied T1 definition, but the pleasure interaction was not present in nucleus accumbens/caudate. T1 is a measurement class, not proof of a general surprise formula.

### C2 Preserve the lead-in to a musical payoff
- Claim: In listeners experiencing intense music-evoked emotion, Salimpoor and colleagues measured striatal dopamine release with PET. Complementary fMRI distinguished activity before a peak from activity at the peak.
- Mechanism: Anticipation and music reward processing; PET measured dopamine, while fMRI tracked associated activity over time.
- Evidence tier: T1 — raclopride PET plus fMRI directly examined the striatal reward system.
- Sources: Salimpoor, Valorie N.; Benovoy, Mitchel; Larcher, Kevin; Dagher, Alain; and Zatorre, Robert J. (2011). “Anatomically distinct dopamine release during anticipation and experience of peak emotion to music.” *Nature Neuroscience*, 14(2), 257–262. https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726 ; opened abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21217764/ .
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Audition a promising peak together with its preceding phrase. Place the answer, transformation, or final image at the peak, and retain enough of the phrase to establish expectation. Compare this with starting directly at the peak. This is an editing hypothesis, not a tested timing prescription.
- Caveats: Selected listeners and intensely emotional music limit generalization. PET and fMRI are different measurements; fMRI alone does not measure dopamine. This study does not establish dopamine as pleasure itself or demonstrate that a commercial edit produces the same response.
- Dark pattern risk: low — musical anticipation can inflate the apparent importance of an otherwise weak claim.

### C22 Turn background music off when comprehension is the priority
- Claim: A meta-analysis found a small negative association between concurrent background music and text understanding across its reading studies. Adding pleasant music is not a reliable way to improve learning.
- Mechanism: Competition for limited attention/working-memory resources; the precise mechanism was not established by the meta-analysis.
- Evidence tier: T1 — quantitative meta-analysis; the reading subset contained eight studies and 680 participants.
- Sources: Kämpfe, Juliane; Sedlmeier, Peter; and Renkewitz, Frank (2011). “The impact of background music on adult listeners: A meta-analysis.” *Psychology of Music*, 39(4), 424–448. https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735610376261 ; opened primary paper: https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/2010-kampfe.pdf .
- Applies to: video | audio | slides | infographic | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Default to silence under dense reading, unfamiliar definitions, or a complex chart. Put identity/emotion music in an opening, transition, or ending. If a bed seems necessary, compare comprehension and key-fact recall with and without it; liking alone is an inadequate success measure.
- Caveats: The reading estimate was small (weighted r = −0.11), and the overall aggregation across different outcomes was null. Older adult studies and heterogeneous tasks do not show that every soundtrack harms every learner. Concurrent music differs from listening before a task; transfer to narrated explainers requires testing.
- Dark pattern risk: low — music can make difficult material feel engaging while masking reduced understanding.
