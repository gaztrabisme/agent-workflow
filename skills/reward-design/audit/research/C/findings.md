# Unit C — sound, music, voice, and audiovisual timing

Research run: 2026-10-10-reward-skill, checked on 10 October 2026. These findings support planning and critique of anticipation, surprise, and payoff through sound. Dopamine participates in reward learning, prediction, anticipation, and wanting; it is not a synonym for pleasure. Only C2 directly measured dopamine release. Physiological arousal, reported chills, memory, and brand recognition are different outcomes. A designer cannot infer an audience's dopamine from an edit. Recipes below are design translations to test, not experimentally established optimum settings, unless explicitly identified as a source's procedure or recommendation. Static slides and infographics gain an auditory channel only when presented, narrated, or made interactive.

### C1 Plan musical expectation with Huron's ITPRA framework
- Claim: Huron's ITPRA theory separates imagining an outcome, tension before it, prediction at its arrival, immediate reaction, and later appraisal. It offers a way to plan a musical setup and resolution without treating every surprise as pleasurable.
- Mechanism: Learned musical expectation, anticipatory tension, prediction, defensive reaction, and conscious appraisal; a theoretical framework rather than a dopamine assay.
- Evidence tier: T4 — scholarly synthesis and theory; the proposed editing recipe has no direct controlled validation.
- Sources: Huron, David (2006). *Sweet Anticipation: Music and the Psychology of Expectation*. MIT Press. Publisher description and original-edition date opened: https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262582780/sweet-anticipation/ .
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk | slides
- How to apply: Make a cue sheet with five columns: expected event, preparation, arrival, immediate impression, and meaning after arrival. Establish a repeating rhythmic or melodic pattern, defer its completion once, then resolve it when the visual or spoken answer arrives. Compare the deferred version with an immediate-resolution version; this sequence is a design inference, not a prescribed number of bars.
- Caveats: Musical expectations are learned and depend on genre, familiarity, and culture. ITPRA is not proof that a cadence or surprise causes dopamine release; a startling reaction may be unpleasant.
- Dark pattern risk: low — extended tension can become coercive if it delays information people need to make a decision.

### C2 Preserve the lead-in to a musical payoff
- Claim: In listeners experiencing intense music-evoked emotion, Salimpoor and colleagues measured striatal dopamine release with PET. Complementary fMRI distinguished activity before a peak from activity at the peak.
- Mechanism: Anticipation and music reward processing; PET measured dopamine, while fMRI tracked associated activity over time.
- Evidence tier: T1 — raclopride PET plus fMRI directly examined the striatal reward system.
- Sources: Salimpoor, Valorie N.; Benovoy, Mitchel; Larcher, Kevin; Dagher, Alain; and Zatorre, Robert J. (2011). “Anatomically distinct dopamine release during anticipation and experience of peak emotion to music.” *Nature Neuroscience*, 14(2), 257–262. https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726 ; opened abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21217764/ .
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Audition a promising peak together with its preceding phrase. Place the answer, transformation, or final image at the peak, and retain enough of the phrase to establish expectation. Compare this with starting directly at the peak. This is an editing hypothesis, not a tested timing prescription.
- Caveats: Selected listeners and intensely emotional music limit generalization. PET and fMRI are different measurements; fMRI alone does not measure dopamine. This study does not establish dopamine as pleasure itself or demonstrate that a commercial edit produces the same response.
- Dark pattern risk: low — musical anticipation can inflate the apparent importance of an otherwise weak claim.

### C3 Use textural change as a candidate for chills, not a trigger
- Claim: Grewe and colleagues found that unexpected harmony and dynamic or textural changes could coincide with chills. Responses did not follow a simple, universal stimulus–response rule.
- Mechanism: Auditory novelty, attention, emotional interpretation, and autonomic arousal/chills.
- Evidence tier: T3 — one exploratory study of 38 listeners using natural music and continuous reports/physiology.
- Sources: Grewe, Oliver; Nagel, Frederik; Kopiez, Reinhard; and Altenmüller, Eckart (2007). “Listening to Music as a Re-Creative Process: Physiological, Psychological, and Psychoacoustical Correlates of Chills and Strong Emotions.” *Music Perception*, 24(3), 297–314. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2007.24.3.297 ; opened author-uploaded paper: https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Frederik-Nagel/publication/249979721_Listening_To_Music_As_A_ReCreative_Process_Physiological_Psychological_And_Psychoacoustical_Correlates_Of_Chills_And_Strong_Emotions/links/00b4953199238cf580000000/Listening-To-Music-As-A-ReCreative-Process-Physiological-Psychological-And-Psychoacoustical-Correlates-Of-Chills-And-Strong-Emotions.pdf .
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Mark entrances of a new voice/instrument, a harmonic shift, or a marked texture change. Try one at the meaningful reveal. Preserve the preceding musical context: the study used whole pieces because context might matter. Ask test listeners where emotion intensified, rather than asking whether the cue “gave them dopamine.”
- Caveats: Associations with natural musical features do not isolate their causal effects. Preference and listening situation mattered; a cue that moves one person can leave another cold. No independent replication of this exact editing recipe is established here.
- Dark pattern risk: low — emotionally charged music can make unsupported claims feel convincing.

### C4 Expect substantial individual differences in frisson
- Claim: Sachs and colleagues found differences in auditory–emotional white-matter connectivity between frequent chill responders and matched nonresponders. A lack of chills does not mean an audience failed to understand the work.
- Mechanism: Individual differences in sensory–emotional connectivity and aesthetic response sensitivity.
- Evidence tier: T3 — small, correlational DTI study, not a dopamine measurement or randomized intervention.
- Sources: Sachs, Matthew E.; Ellis, Robert J.; Schlaug, Gottfried; and Loui, Psyche (2016). “Brain connectivity reflects human aesthetic responses to music.” *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience*, 11(6), 884–891. https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsw009 ; opened paper: https://mindlab.research.wesleyan.edu/files/2014/01/Sachs-Ellis-Schlaug-Loui-2016-scan-chills-dti.pdf .
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Test candidate music with listeners drawn from the intended audience, including different musical preferences. Gather separate ratings for relevance, pleasure, arousal, and clarity. Make the payoff understandable through the content even when a listener has no bodily response.
- Caveats: Scanning/behavioural groups contained 10 participants each; five initially selected chill responders were excluded after failing to report chills in testing. Connectivity correlations do not show that music training creates these differences or that a chosen soundtrack will cause chills.
- Dark pattern risk: none — the main implication is to avoid overpromising a universal response.

### C5 Match tempo to the intended level of activation
- Claim: In Bernardi and colleagues' laboratory study, faster tempi and simpler rhythmic structures were associated with increased breathing, blood pressure, and heart rate. Arousal is not evidence of liking, learning, or reward.
- Mechanism: Autonomic activation and respiratory/rhythmic coupling.
- Evidence tier: T3 — controlled exposure but only 24 listeners, with different musical selections rather than one isolated tempo manipulation.
- Sources: Bernardi, Luciano; Porta, Cesare; and Sleight, Peter (2006). “Cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and respiratory changes induced by different types of music in musicians and non-musicians: the importance of silence.” *Heart*, 92(4), 445–452. https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600 ; opened abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16199412/ .
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk | slides
- How to apply: Choose a slower, less rhythmically insistent bed for reflective explanation and a faster, clearer pulse for an action sequence. Compare alternatives at matched volume with the same narration. The source used six styles in 2- and 4-minute exposures; it did not establish an ideal BPM for a presentation.
- Caveats: Preference, musical training, rhythmic structure, and genre complicate transfer. Increased heart rate is not a desirable outcome in every context. Do not claim that fast music necessarily improves attention or comprehension.
- Dark pattern risk: low — pace can create urgency that exceeds the importance of the task.

### C6 Build an EDM drop through contrast and return
- Claim: Solberg and Dibben found that EDM drops were linked to peak reported experiences and higher skin-conductance responses than other parts of the break routine. The study supports a contrast-based musical payoff, not a claim of measured dopamine release.
- Mechanism: Expectation, dynamic contrast, embodied rhythmic engagement, and autonomic arousal.
- Evidence tier: T3 — one laboratory listening study with 24 participants and five excerpts.
- Sources: Solberg, Ragnhild Torvanger, and Dibben, Nicola (2019). “Peak experiences with electronic dance music: Subjective experiences, physiological responses, and musical characteristics of the break routine.” *Music Perception*, 36(4), 371–389. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2019.36.4.371 ; opened university abstract: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/145911/ .
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Establish a beat; remove or thin key rhythmic/bass layers in a breakdown; intensify the buildup; restore the full groove at the reveal. Align the major visual transformation with that return. Use the existing track's phrasing instead of imposing a universal 8- or 16-bar formula.
- Caveats: Participants listened away from a club, without dancing. Natural excerpts do not isolate each production device, and skin conductance indexes arousal rather than positive value. Genre familiarity and audience taste matter; repeated drops can become predictable or tiring.
- Dark pattern risk: low — a dramatic return can overstate a modest result.

### C7 Align audiovisual events, then check the actual playback
- Claim: Perceived audiovisual synchrony tolerates some physical delay, but its tolerance varies with the stimulus, task, and prior exposure. There is no single synchronization window that fits speech, impacts, and all presentation contexts.
- Mechanism: Audiovisual temporal integration, compensation for transmission/processing delays, and temporal recalibration.
- Evidence tier: T2 — tutorial review of converging controlled timing experiments, not a quantitative meta-analysis.
- Sources: Vroomen, Jean, and Keetels, Mirjam (2010). “Perception of intersensory synchrony: A tutorial review.” *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics*, 72(4), 871–884. https://doi.org/10.3758/APP.72.4.871 ; opened publisher review: https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/APP.72.4.871 .
- Applies to: video | UI | audio | live talk | slides | infographic
- How to apply: Align the audible onset with the visible contact, click, or reveal as an initial editing target. Review speech and sharp impacts separately after export, on the delivery device and at normal speed. If an offset is noticed, compare nearby offsets and retain the version listeners judge synchronous; do not use a reported laboratory threshold as permission for sloppy synchronization.
- Caveats: Detection, order judgments, and causal binding are different tasks. Speech can tolerate different lags from brief flashes and tones; adaptation changes judgments. Accurate synchrony can improve coherence without being rewarding.
- Dark pattern risk: low — synchronization can make an unrelated sound appear to belong to an event.

### C8 Keep talking-head sound and mouth movements consistent
- Claim: In the McGurk illusion, conflicting mouth movements can change the syllable a listener reports hearing. Later large-sample work found substantial, stable differences between listeners and between stimuli.
- Mechanism: Audiovisual speech integration and perceptual conflict resolution.
- Evidence tier: T2 — classic controlled illusion plus a 165-person study with a one-year retest subset.
- Sources: McGurk, Harry, and MacDonald, John (1976). “Hearing lips and seeing voices.” *Nature*, 264, 746–748. https://doi.org/10.1038/264746a0 . Basu Mallick, Debshila; Magnotti, John F.; and Beauchamp, Michael S. (2015). “Variability and stability in the McGurk effect: contributions of participants, stimuli, time, and response type.” *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 22, 1299–1307. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0817-4 ; opened primary paper: https://oww-files-public.sfo3.cdn.digitaloceanspaces.com/6/6d/VariabilityStability_McGurk.pdf .
- Applies to: video | live talk
- How to apply: Inspect dubbed/replaced dialogue where the speaker's mouth is visible, especially consonant onsets. When the replacement does not match, use a relevant cutaway or rerecord a matching performance. Evaluate intelligibility with viewers instead of using the illusion as a trick to drive engagement.
- Caveats: The 2015 study found individual response frequencies spanning 0–100%; it does not justify saying everyone fuses conflicting speech. Laboratory syllables are not normal dialogue, and the illusion does not measure reward or prove that any small lip-sync error changes meaning.
- Dark pattern risk: high — deliberately pairing false speech with a person's face can misrepresent what they said.

### C9 Give the reveal a clear sound-picture contact point
- Claim: Chion calls the apparent fusion of a coincident sound and image “synchresis.” Film craft uses this to make a movement, contact, or transformation feel like one event even when its sound was created separately.
- Mechanism: Perceptual audiovisual grouping and attribution of a sound to a visible event; craft terminology, not a demonstrated dopamine mechanism.
- Evidence tier: T4 — film theory and professional examples rather than controlled effect estimates.
- Sources: Chion, Michel (1994; second English edition 2019). *Audio-Vision: Sound on Screen*. Edited and translated by Claudia Gorbman. Columbia University Press. https://cup.columbia.edu/book/audio-vision-sound-on-screen/9780231185882/ . Chion excerpts edited by FilmSound.org, “Synchresis” (undated): https://www.filmsound.org/chion/sync.htm ; both opened.
- Applies to: video | UI | slides | infographic | live talk
- How to apply: Identify the decisive contact point: a bar reaching its target, a logo completing, or an object landing. Place a restrained, semantically appropriate sound onset there. Audition it against the original picture without the added sound; use sound to clarify the event's weight or completion.
- Caveats: Chion's examples are not experiments establishing an optimum offset or a reward response. Coherence, context, and the viewer's expectations constrain fusion. Static graphics need an accompanying presentation or interaction for this technique.
- Dark pattern risk: high — a fabricated impact can make an event or result seem more forceful than the evidence warrants.

### C10 Give UI earcons a learnable sound grammar
- Claim: Brewster and colleagues found that structured earcons could convey interface information better than unstructured sounds. Distinct musical timbres and sufficiently different rhythms improved recognition; subtle pitch changes alone were difficult.
- Mechanism: Learned auditory categories, pattern recognition, and feedback certainty.
- Evidence tier: T3 — two small laboratory experiments with a revised sound set, not a large field validation.
- Sources: Brewster, Stephen A.; Wright, Peter C.; and Edwards, Alistair D. N. (1993). “An evaluation of earcons for use in auditory human-computer interfaces.” *Proceedings of INTERCHI '93*, 222–227. https://doi.org/10.1145/169059.169179 ; opened paper: https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/3257/1/CHI4493.pdf .
- Applies to: UI | audio | video
- How to apply: Assign a timbre to a family of events and a clearly different rhythm to the event within that family. The paper recommends contrasting timbres such as brass and organ rather than near-identical variants. Teach the mapping with visible feedback, then test recognition alone and alongside competing sounds. Keep success, warning, and error distinct.
- Caveats: The participants learned the mappings; earcons are not inherently understood. Combining sounds still created recognition problems. The study measures identification, not pleasure or reward; tiny pitch differences are especially unreliable for diverse hearing abilities.
- Dark pattern risk: low — pleasant confirmation tones become misleading if they sound before the operation actually succeeds.

### C11 Use auditory icons for concrete actions
- Claim: Gaver's SonicFinder used everyday sound analogies to convey interface events and properties alongside graphics. This provides a design vocabulary for feedback that refers to an action rather than an arbitrary musical code.
- Mechanism: Ecological sound recognition and event/source analogy; reduced uncertainty about what happened.
- Evidence tier: T4 — conceptual argument and implemented interface examples, without a controlled superiority estimate in the cited paper.
- Sources: Gaver, William W. (1989). “The SonicFinder: An Interface That Uses Auditory Icons.” *Human–Computer Interaction*, 4(1), 67–94. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327051hci0401_3 ; opened primary text: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/213799409_The_SonicFinder_An_Interface_that_Uses_Auditory_Icons .
- Applies to: UI | video | audio
- How to apply: Start with actions that have understandable physical analogies: an item being dropped into a container, tapping/selecting, or filling/progress. Use a short caricature of that sound, pair it with visible state feedback, and test whether users can name the event. For abstract operations without an intuitive analogue, use a taught earcon or explicit text.
- Caveats: Familiarity with a source sound and the metaphor varies culturally and by experience. A sound that is recognizable may still be annoying with repetition. SonicFinder is an example, not proof that every naturalistic sound is more usable than an earcon.
- Dark pattern risk: low — reassuring physical metaphors can obscure whether data was actually saved, deleted, or transferred.

### C12 Never celebrate a net loss as a win
- Claim: Dixon and colleagues found that winning sounds increased players' overestimation of how often they had won. Related experiments show that sound can help expose outcomes that return less than the wager as losses.
- Mechanism: Reinforcement cues, autonomic arousal, outcome categorization, and biased memory of success.
- Evidence tier: T2 — controlled simulator studies with 96 and 157 participants, with converging results across sound manipulations.
- Sources: Dixon, Mike J.; Harrigan, Kevin A.; Santesso, Diane L.; Graydon, Candice; Fugelsang, Jonathan A.; and Collins, Karen (2014; online 2013). “The Impact of Sound in Modern Multiline Video Slot Machine Play.” *Journal of Gambling Studies*, 30, 913–929. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10899-013-9391-8 ; opened full text: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10899-013-9391-8 . The same authors (2014; online 2013). “Erratum to: The Impact of Sound in Modern Multiline Video Slot Machine Play.” *Journal of Gambling Studies*, 30, 931. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10899-013-9417-2 ; opened correction: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/307982634_Erratum_to_The_Impact_of_Sound_in_Modern_Multiline_Video_Slot_Machine_Play . Dixon, Mike J.; Collins, Karen; Harrigan, Kevin A.; Graydon, Candice; and Fugelsang, Jonathan A. (2015; online 2013). “Using sound to unmask losses disguised as wins in multiline slot machines.” *Journal of Gambling Studies*, 31, 183–196. Opened record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24198088/ .
- Applies to: UI | video | audio
- How to apply: Define success from the user's net outcome before choosing a sound. Reserve celebration for actual success; give partial completion, costs, and failure distinct, truthful feedback. Show “wager 10; return 2; net −8” as a loss, rather than attaching a win fanfare to the return of 2.
- Caveats: Gambling simulator outcomes do not establish an addiction effect in ordinary productivity UI. Physiology was not a dopamine assay. The erratum corrects discussion percentages: with 28 actual wins, estimates averaged 33 without sound (18% overestimation) and 36 with sound (29%). Use those corrected figures, not the original percentages.
- Dark pattern risk: high — disguising losses or costs as rewarding progress can distort decisions and encourage further spending.

### C13 Use a short, stable sonic signature: the Intel case
- Claim: Intel's history documents a three-second, five-note signature, composed by Walter Werzowa in 1994 and placed on its 1995 timeline. It is a concrete sonic-branding example, not controlled evidence of a recognition uplift.
- Mechanism: Repeated sound–brand association and an auditory retrieval cue; proposed explanation of the practice.
- Evidence tier: T4 — brand-owner history and professional deployment, without a controlled comparison.
- Sources: Intel Corporation (undated). “The Intel Bong,” *Explore Intel's History*, 1995 timeline entry. https://timeline.intel.com/1995/the-intel-bong ; opened.
- Applies to: video | audio | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Prototype a brief signature with a distinctive rhythm/timbre and deploy the same core version at consistent brand moments. Intel's three seconds and five notes are an example, not an optimum. Test sound-only brand identification after realistic exposure, and compare mistaken identification of competitors.
- Caveats: Creation year and timeline/debut year should not be collapsed. Brand familiarity and decades of exposure confound any inference about the notes themselves. No dopamine measurement or independent causal brand-recall estimate was found for this case.
- Dark pattern risk: low — excessive repetition can intrude on attention or mask functional feedback.

### C14 Make a sonic logo a miniature narrative: Netflix's ta-dum
- Claim: Netflix's creators describe a brief cue intended to build and release tension, with repetition teaching the brand association. Netflix retained the sound when changing its visual ident in 2019.
- Mechanism: Learned auditory association, expectation, and a short tension–resolution gesture; creator intent rather than measured audience neurochemistry.
- Evidence tier: T4 — first-person production interviews and a company announcement.
- Sources: Taylor, Dallas, host; interviews with Todd Yellin, Lon Bender, Charlie Campagna, and Tanya Kumar (2024 repost of a 2020 episode). “Tudum! It's Netflix.” *Twenty Thousand Hertz*. https://www.20k.org/fave-episodes/netflix-czkar . Netflix (2019, February 1). “Your new Netflix ident animation (cue *Netflix Sound*).” *About Netflix*. https://about.netflix.com/en/news/your-new-netflix-ident-animation-cue-netflix-sound ; both opened.
- Applies to: video | audio | UI
- How to apply: Write a brief specifying brand character and the event the cue introduces. Prototype a short preparation followed by a decisive ending, and synchronize it with the ident. Yellin describes fitting the story-like gesture into about three seconds. Test whether it conveys the intended character and remains tolerable on repeated listening.
- Caveats: A successful, heavily repeated logo cannot establish that tension–resolution alone caused recognition. The podcast page is dated July 12, 2024 and tagged 2020; do not miscite the repost as the cue's creation date. No measured dopamine response was found.
- Dark pattern risk: low — a habitual opening cue can encourage automatic consumption if used to push unwanted continuation.

### C15 Vary vocal pitch within a natural expressive range
- Claim: Niebuhr and colleagues found that a larger pitch range was associated with higher perceived charisma in entrepreneurial speech. Their results do not support a universal instruction to lower every speaker's pitch.
- Mechanism: Prosodic salience and social judgments of engagement, confidence, and charisma.
- Evidence tier: T3 — correlations from 12 second-language English speakers rated by 98 listeners.
- Sources: Niebuhr, Oliver; Skarnitzl, Radek; and Tylečková, Lea (2018). “The acoustic fingerprint of a charismatic voice—Initial evidence from correlations between long-term spectral features and listener ratings.” *Speech Prosody 2018*, 359–363. https://doi.org/10.21437/SpeechProsody.2018-73 ; opened paper record/abstract: https://www.isca-archive.org/speechprosody_2018/niebuhr18_speechprosody.html .
- Applies to: live talk | video | audio | slides
- How to apply: Mark the contrast words and the answer in a script. Record one neutral and one naturally expressive reading; use a pitch change to make the important word perceptually distinct. Ask listeners which reading sounds engaged and which is easier to follow. Stay within the speaker's comfortable voice instead of prescribing a pitch in hertz.
- Caveats: Correlation is not an experiment manipulating pitch. This small speaker sample, second-language context, and reported gender-dependent pitch-level associations limit transfer. Exaggeration can sound theatrical; charisma is not truthfulness or learning, and no reward measurement was made.
- Dark pattern risk: low — delivery can make weak evidence sound more authoritative.

### C16 Keep speech moving, but do not chase a universal rate
- Claim: Rosenberg and Hirschberg's positive rate–charisma correlation did not reach conventional statistical significance (r = 0.094, p = 0.0902). Their study does not establish that speaking faster, or varying pace, reliably increases charisma.
- Mechanism: Social inference from fluency, energy, and hesitation, alongside linguistic content.
- Evidence tier: T3 — listener experiments using selected natural speech and transcripts, with correlational acoustic analyses.
- Sources: Rosenberg, Andrew, and Hirschberg, Julia (2009). “Charisma perception from text and speech.” *Speech Communication*, 51(7), 640–655. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.specom.2008.11.001 ; opened primary text: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220121084_Charisma_perception_from_text_and_speech . Niebuhr, Oliver; Brem, Alexander; Michalsky, Jan; and Neitsch, Jana (2020). “What makes business speakers sound charismatic? A contrastive acoustic-melodic analysis of Steve Jobs and Mark Zuckerberg.” *Cadernos de Linguística*, 1(1). https://doi.org/10.25189/2675-4916.2020.v1.n1.id272 ; opened: https://cadernos.abralin.org/index.php/cadernos/article/view/272 .
- Applies to: live talk | video | audio | slides
- How to apply: Remove avoidable hesitations while retaining phrase boundaries. Try a slightly more energetic take, then check whether listeners can state the argument and repeat key numbers. Slow for unfamiliar terms or dense comparisons as a comprehension-oriented design choice, not a tested charisma formula.
- Caveats: The rate association was weak and nonsignificant; do not present it as an established causal effect. Political clips and the Jobs–Zuckerberg case comparison do not establish optimum words per minute. Topic, recognition, language, and style matter. Faster delivery can outrun understanding; charisma ratings do not measure reward or accuracy.
- Dark pattern risk: low — rapid speech can make caveats difficult to process.

### C17 Place silence before or after closure deliberately
- Claim: Margulis found that musical context changes how silence is perceived: pauses after tonal closure were noticed sooner and felt less tense than pauses without closure. Silence can sustain an unanswered expectation or mark that something has finished.
- Mechanism: Expectation, tonal/syntactic context, and perceived tension.
- Evidence tier: T3 — one exploratory paper with two experiments, not independent validation of a reveal-editing rule.
- Sources: Margulis, Elizabeth Hellmuth (2007). “Silences in Music are Musical Not Silent: An Exploratory Study of Context Effects on the Experience of Musical Pauses.” *Music Perception*, 24(5), 485–506. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2007.24.5.485 ; opened university abstract: https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/silences-in-music-are-musical-not-silent-an-exploratory-study-of-/ .
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk | slides
- How to apply: For suspense, leave a question or musical phrase incomplete and briefly remove the bed before the answer. For absorption, resolve the phrase and leave space after the answer. Compare, for example, zero, one, and two beats of space; at 120 BPM one beat is 0.5 seconds. Those test values are design starting points, not study-established optima.
- Caveats: Musical tonal closure is not identical to narrative closure. Neither this paper nor Bernardi's two-minute silence condition establishes a universal one- or two-second reveal pause. Long pauses can feel like a technical failure or needlessly delay the audience.
- Dark pattern risk: low — suspense can become manipulative when essential information is deliberately withheld.

### C18 Reject the “louder is always better” shortcut
- Claim: The loudness-war belief is that maximizing a recording's loudness improves preference. Croghan and colleagues found that heavy compression generally reduced quality, including when loudness differences were minimized.
- Mechanism: Perceived loudness, dynamic contrast, and audible processing distortion, rather than a demonstrated reward mechanism.
- Evidence tier: T3 — one controlled paired-comparison study of processed rock and classical excerpts.
- Sources: Croghan, Naomi B. H.; Arehart, Kathryn H.; and Kates, James M. (2012). “Quality and loudness judgments for music subjected to compression limiting.” *The Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*, 132(2), 1177–1188. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.4730881 ; opened author-uploaded paper: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230679584_Quality_and_loudness_judgments_for_music_subjected_to_compression_limiting .
- Applies to: audio | video | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Compare lightly and heavily compressed versions at matched perceived playback loudness before selecting one. Preserve enough dynamic contrast for the payoff to stand out, while keeping speech intelligible. Review on the intended playback system at ordinary volume; do not use an unlevelled louder version as evidence of better sound.
- Caveats: A small amount of compression was preferred when loudness varied; the paper does not establish that all compression is harmful. Genre, listener, and playback conditions affect preference. This study does not prescribe a streaming loudness target or prove that increased dynamic range always improves emotional response.
- Dark pattern risk: low — sudden loudness can force attention and make ordinary feedback feel disproportionately urgent.

### C19 Match music's mood to the occasion, not just positivity
- Claim: Alpert and Alpert found that music could alter mood and purchase intentions in a simulated advertising setting. This does not support a general rule that happy music sells every product better.
- Mechanism: Mood induction and affect influencing judgment; not a demonstrated dopamine response.
- Evidence tier: T3 — one controlled laboratory study with unfamiliar music and greeting-card stimuli.
- Sources: Alpert, Judy I., and Alpert, Mark I. (1990). “Music influences on mood and purchase intentions.” *Psychology & Marketing*, 7(2), 109–133. https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.4220070204 ; opened author-uploaded primary paper: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227807345_Music_Influences_on_Mood_and_Purchase_Intention .
- Applies to: video | audio | slides | live talk
- How to apply: Specify the occasion and intended emotional reading before choosing music: celebration, reassurance, reflection, or concern. Compare two plausible moods with the same visuals and wording, plus a silent version. Ask separately about mood, message interpretation, and intended action. The study used 30-second music/stimulus exposures; that is a procedure, not an ideal ad duration.
- Caveats: Purchase intention is not an actual purchase, and greeting cards are unusually emotion-related products. The music's cultural associations and personal liking can change responses. Do not generalize the direction of one mood effect across products or audiences.
- Dark pattern risk: high — mood manipulation can nudge costly choices without improving the decision's informational basis.

### C20 Translate brand attributes into a musical brief
- Claim: Brodsky's four studies found that listeners could associate music composed from a product's design language with the intended automobile brand. The evidence concerns brand fit and differentiation, not a universal recall increase from adding music.
- Mechanism: Cross-domain semantic association and perceived music–brand congruence.
- Evidence tier: T2 — four behavioural studies across two countries and several stakeholder groups; independent replication is not established here.
- Sources: Brodsky, Warren (2011). “Developing a functional method to apply music in branding: Design language-generated music.” *Psychology of Music*, 39(2), 261–283. https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735610387778 ; opened publisher abstract: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735610387778 .
- Applies to: video | audio | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Write three specific brand/product attributes, then have a composer express them through instrument choice, rhythm, texture, and phrasing. Present candidate music without its label and ask intended users which product it fits and why. Keep recognizability, emotional fit, and recall as separate test outcomes.
- Caveats: Two GM automobile brands do not represent all categories. Some images/preferences were culturally specific. Abstract access supports the broad findings, not a portable mapping such as “brass always means strength.” Do not treat Brodsky's result as an advertising-recall experiment.
- Dark pattern risk: low — an appealing soundtrack can imply product qualities that have not been substantiated.

### C21 Make attention-grabbing ad music congruent with the message
- Claim: Kellaris and colleagues found that increasing attention to music improved message reception when the music prompted message-congruent thoughts. A memorable soundtrack can otherwise compete with the advertised information.
- Mechanism: Attention allocation and semantic congruence in memory encoding.
- Evidence tier: T3 — one controlled advertising experiment; no general effect size or independent replication is asserted here.
- Sources: Kellaris, James J.; Cox, Anthony D.; and Cox, Dena (1993). “The Effect of Background Music on Ad Processing: A Contingency Explanation.” *Journal of Marketing*, 57(4), 114–125. https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299305700409 ; opened publisher abstract: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002224299305700409 .
- Applies to: video | audio | slides | live talk
- How to apply: Select music whose associations reinforce the main claim. Keep distinctive musical changes away from dense spoken facts as a design precaution. After exposure, test unaided recall of the brand and one key claim separately from recall/liking of the music; compare with a quieter or silent version.
- Caveats: An attention effect is conditional on fit; it does not mean musical excitement always improves recall. The accessed abstract supports the interaction but not a numeric soundtrack tempo or mix target. Cultural associations and prior familiarity affect what counts as congruent.
- Dark pattern risk: low — a catchy cue can divert attention from qualifications while leaving a favourable impression.

### C22 Turn background music off when comprehension is the priority
- Claim: A meta-analysis found a small negative association between concurrent background music and text understanding across its reading studies. Adding pleasant music is not a reliable way to improve learning.
- Mechanism: Competition for limited attention/working-memory resources; the precise mechanism was not established by the meta-analysis.
- Evidence tier: T1 — quantitative meta-analysis; the reading subset contained eight studies and 680 participants.
- Sources: Kämpfe, Juliane; Sedlmeier, Peter; and Renkewitz, Frank (2011). “The impact of background music on adult listeners: A meta-analysis.” *Psychology of Music*, 39(4), 424–448. https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735610376261 ; opened primary paper: https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/2010-kampfe.pdf .
- Applies to: video | audio | slides | infographic | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Default to silence under dense reading, unfamiliar definitions, or a complex chart. Put identity/emotion music in an opening, transition, or ending. If a bed seems necessary, compare comprehension and key-fact recall with and without it; liking alone is an inadequate success measure.
- Caveats: The reading estimate was small (weighted r = −0.11), and the overall aggregation across different outcomes was null. Older adult studies and heterogeneous tasks do not show that every soundtrack harms every learner. Concurrent music differs from listening before a task; transfer to narrated explainers requires testing.
- Dark pattern risk: low — music can make difficult material feel engaging while masking reduced understanding.

### C23 Avoid competing vocals during verbal memory tasks
- Claim: Salamé and Baddeley found that background vocal music disrupted immediate recall more than instrumental music. Instrumental interference was less consistent, so removing lyrics does not guarantee a harmless background.
- Mechanism: Irrelevant auditory material interfering with serial verbal/phonological memory.
- Evidence tier: T3 — three small controlled experiments, including within-paper replication, on a narrow memory task.
- Sources: Salamé, Pierre, and Baddeley, Alan (1989). “Effects of Background Music on Phonological Short-Term Memory.” *The Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology Section A*, 41(1), 107–122. https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748908402355 ; opened primary paper: https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/1989-salame.pdf .
- Applies to: video | audio | slides | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Remove sung lyrics beneath definitions, instructions, names, or numbers the listener needs to retain. Try a quiet instrumental bed and silence as separate versions. Reserve the vocal hook for a transition without competing verbal information; test actual retention rather than assuming low volume solves the problem.
- Caveats: The experiments used serial recall of nine visually presented digits, not long-term comprehension or an entire lecture. Instrumental music was not significantly worse than silence in the second experiment. Disruption is not a measure of musical dislike, dopamine, or overall engagement.
- Dark pattern risk: low — competing vocals can distract from instructions or disclosures.

### C24 Myth: Mozart makes the audience smarter
- Claim: The popular “Mozart effect” expands a possible short-lived spatial-task effect into a claim that Mozart listening generally raises intelligence. A large meta-analysis found little evidence for a specific Mozart advantage.
- Mechanism: Possible nonspecific arousal/mood or preference effects; not a demonstrated Mozart-specific intelligence mechanism.
- Evidence tier: T1 — meta-analysis of nearly 40 studies and over 3,000 participants, including unpublished work.
- Sources: Pietschnig, Jakob; Voracek, Martin; and Formann, Anton K. (2010). “Mozart effect–Shmozart effect: A meta-analysis.” *Intelligence*, 38(3), 314–323. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2010.03.001 ; opened primary abstract: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/242788144_Mozart_effect-Shmozart_effect_A_meta-analysis ; author-institution record: https://ucrisportal.univie.ac.at/en/publications/mozart-effect-shmozart-effect-a-meta-analysis/ .
- Applies to: video | audio | slides | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Select music for relevance, intelligibility, and audience preference. If offering music before a task, compare it with other liked music and silence, then measure the specific task. Do not market a Mozart soundtrack as an IQ, comprehension, or durable-learning enhancer.
- Caveats: Mozart versus no/nonmusical stimulation yielded d = 0.37; other music versus no/nonmusical stimulation was similar (d = 0.38). Mozart versus other music was much smaller (d = 0.15), with publication-bias and laboratory-affiliation concerns. These estimates concern spatial tasks, not a lasting intelligence gain or the separate question of music training.
- Dark pattern risk: high — an unsupported brain-enhancement promise can sell products or mislead educators.

### C25 Reject “more musical surprise always means more pleasure”
- Claim: Cheung and colleagues found that musical pleasure depended jointly on prior uncertainty and the surprise of the next chord. High pleasure could accompany a surprising chord in a predictable context or a predictable chord in an uncertain context.
- Mechanism: Learned musical prediction, uncertainty reduction, and context-dependent surprise; not simply surprise magnitude.
- Evidence tier: T1 — behavioural ratings plus fMRI, including measurement of nucleus accumbens/caudate responses; no dopamine assay.
- Sources: Cheung, Vincent K. M.; Harrison, Peter M. C.; Meyer, Lars; Pearce, Marcus T.; Haynes, John-Dylan; and Koelsch, Stefan (2019). “Uncertainty and Surprise Jointly Predict Musical Pleasure and Amygdala, Hippocampus, and Auditory Cortex Activity.” *Current Biology*, 29(23), 4084–4092.e4. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067 ; opened author-hosted primary paper: https://www.marcus-pearce.com/assets/papers/CheungEtAl2019.pdf .
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: After a stable musical setup, audition one meaningful deviation. After an ambiguous passage, audition a clear resolution. Compare both against an unchanged version and ask about pleasure and coherence separately. These are editing translations of the interaction, not guaranteed payoff formulas.
- Caveats: The stimuli/model concerned Western tonal harmony and pop-song chords; they do not establish a rule for all sound design. The uncertainty–surprise interaction was not found in nucleus accumbens/caudate: those regions reflected uncertainty instead. Unexpected events are not automatically positive reward prediction errors, and fMRI cannot establish dopamine release.
- Dark pattern risk: low — repeated manufactured uncertainty can demand attention without providing useful payoff.
