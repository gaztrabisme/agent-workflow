# Unit A: reward mechanisms in media

Evidence gathered through web search on 2026-10-10. Dopamine contributes to learning from reward predictions and to incentive motivation; it is not a synonym for pleasure. T1 identifies a measurement method or a meta-analysis, not a guarantee of replication, causal inference, or transfer to media design. fMRI measures a blood-oxygenation signal, not dopamine release. Unless explicitly described as a study procedure, each design recipe below is an inference to test with the intended audience; its numbers are prototype choices, not established biological timings.

### A1 Reward depends on what was expected
- Claim: Many midbrain dopamine neurons signal outcomes better than predicted, respond little to fully predicted rewards, and reduce firing at expected reward omission. With learning, responses can shift to predictive cues.
- Mechanism: Reward prediction error: updating expectations about future value.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Primate dopamine-neuron recordings interpreted alongside computational learning models.
- Sources: Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward. Science, 275(5306), 1593–1599. DOI: 10.1126/science.275.5306.1593. [Opened paper](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~sswang/fundamental-readings-for-Wang-lab-members/schultz_montague97_science.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: establish one understandable expectation, then deliver a useful outcome that exceeds it—for example, an explanation that also solves a second practical problem. Ask viewers what they expected before judging the reveal.
- Caveats: Animal conditioning is not media design. Surprise need not have positive value, and expected rewards can remain enjoyable. No media effect size or optimal reveal delay follows.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Inflated promises and deliberately withheld rewards can turn expectation management into clickbait or compulsive checking.

### A2 Wanting and liking can separate
- Claim: Seeking a reward and enjoying it are separable components of reward. Dopamine depletion in rats greatly impaired motivated behaviour while leaving measured positive taste reactions substantially intact.
- Mechanism: Incentive salience, or cue-triggered wanting, versus hedonic liking.
- Evidence tier: T1 — The paper combines a review with dopamine-depletion experiments and affective taste-reaction measures.
- Sources: Berridge, K. C., & Robinson, T. E. (1998). What is the role of dopamine in reward: hedonic impact, reward learning, or incentive salience? Brain Research Reviews, 28(3), 309–369. DOI: 10.1016/S0165-0173(98)00019-8. [Opened paper](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/BerridgeRobinsonBrResRev1998.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: evaluate approach and satisfaction separately. Pair clicks, continuation, or requests for more with a post-experience usefulness/enjoyment rating and a delayed recall question. Improve the delivered benefit as well as the invitation.
- Caveats: Rat taste reactions are not subjective reports from media audiences. The 1998 argument against dopamine-based learning is historically broader than current consensus; use the wanting/liking distinction alongside prediction-error evidence, not against it. Music pharmacology in A10 adds nuance.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Optimising continued seeking while satisfaction deteriorates is a plausible route to exploitative engagement.

### A3 Anticipation and uncertainty are different signals
- Claim: In conditioned monkeys, brief dopamine responses tracked reward probability and prediction error, while activity during the delay was greatest under uncertain reward delivery. Maximum uncertainty in that binary task occurred at a reward probability of 0.5.
- Mechanism: Anticipation of expected value; uncertainty about reward occurrence.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Direct electrophysiological recording of dopamine neurons during controlled reward-probability trials.
- Sources: Fiorillo, C. D., Tobler, P. N., & Schultz, W. (2003). Discrete coding of reward probability and uncertainty by dopamine neurons. Science, 299(5614), 1898–1902. DOI: 10.1126/science.1077349. [Opened paper](https://www.hms.harvard.edu/bss/neuro/bornlab/nb204/final_exam/FiorilloSchultz2003.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | audio | live talk | UI
- How to apply: Design inference: make the forthcoming benefit clear while leaving one bounded question unresolved, such as which of two plausible explanations wins. Resolve it in the same segment. Give essential UI outcomes reliable feedback.
- Caveats: A 50% juice-delivery schedule is not an optimal suspense formula for media. Sustained uncertainty activity is distinct from the brief prediction-error response. Uncertainty can generate stress, and these recordings do not establish pleasure or audience retention.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Random rewards and uncertain access can encourage repeated checking; do not turn the laboratory probability into a product schedule.

### A4 Make the missing information identifiable
- Claim: Information-gap theory proposes that curiosity arises when people notice a gap between what they know and what they want to know. A small amount of relevant knowledge can make a gap visible; total unfamiliarity may give someone little reason to care.
- Mechanism: Curiosity through a perceived information gap.
- Evidence tier: T3 — A narrative theoretical review; the theory itself is not a controlled media intervention or meta-analysis.
- Sources: Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation. Psychological Bulletin, 116(1), 75–98. DOI: 10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75. [Opened paper](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/PsychofCuriosity.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: show one familiar fact, identify one specific unresolved question, and explain why its answer matters. For example: “Both teams used the same model; why did only one save time?” Supply enough context for an informed guess.
- Caveats: This is an influential account, not the only explanation of curiosity. Trivia and information-seeking findings do not validate every teaser. The perceived value and size of the gap vary with prior knowledge; resolution can disappoint.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Concealing basic information or repeatedly promising an answer without delivering it exploits the gap.

### A5 Curiosity can give information motivational value
- Claim: In a trivia task, higher reported curiosity was associated with activity in caudate regions implicated in anticipated reward. Participants also spent limited resources to obtain answers, and higher curiosity predicted better later answer memory.
- Mechanism: Information seeking as anticipated reward; curiosity-linked learning.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Human fMRI plus behavioural willingness-to-spend and memory tasks; neural associations do not measure dopamine release.
- Sources: Kang, M. J., Hsu, M., Krajbich, I. M., Loewenstein, G., McClure, S. M., Wang, J. T., & Camerer, C. F. (2009). The wick in the candle of learning: Epistemic curiosity activates reward circuitry and enhances memory. Psychological Science, 20(8), 963–973. DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x. [Opened paper](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: offer a question that viewers can partially reason about, invite a guess, and reveal the answer with an explanation. Pilot several questions and select ones with high curiosity ratings rather than merely low answer accuracy.
- Caveats: Curiosity was rated, not randomly assigned independently of question content. These tasks involved trivia, not complex client decisions. Caudate BOLD activity and willingness to pay establish neither dopamine concentration nor enjoyment of the eventual answer.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Useful, promptly answered questions are benign; misleading previews or paid access to trivial answers raise the risk.

### A6 Curiosity can help nearby information enter memory
- Claim: People remembered answers better when curiosity was high and also showed a memory benefit for unrelated faces presented while awaiting those answers. Benefits appeared in immediate and one-day-delayed tests.
- Mechanism: Curiosity-associated memory encoding; anticipatory midbrain–hippocampal activity and connectivity.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Human fMRI with memory testing and a separate behavioural experiment; dopamine was inferred from circuit anatomy, not measured.
- Sources: Gruber, M. J., Gelman, B. D., & Ranganath, C. (2014). States of curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit. Neuron, 84(2), 486–496. DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060. [Opened author manuscript](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2zd605r7/qt2zd605r7_noSplash_442c2e267829848246b0ffc2e9beded1.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: raise a meaningful question immediately before presenting its explanatory evidence. Keep that evidence relevant and readable, then resolve the question. Test next-day understanding as well as immediate interest.
- Caveats: Incidental faces are not equivalent to a dense slide or an unrelated sponsor message. The design benefit has no verified universal delay or dosage. Higher curiosity and memory covaried; the fMRI evidence does not prove a dopamine-mediated causal chain.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Relevant learning is beneficial; inserting advertising during an unresolved question would exploit a speculative transfer.

### A7 Novelty can support learning, without being pleasure
- Claim: Human SN/VTA activity responded more to novel images than to several other forms of salience, and novelty also benefited learning of familiar items in the same context. A hippocampal–VTA loop is a proposed account linking novelty detection to memory persistence.
- Mechanism: Novelty detection; hippocampal–midbrain interactions and plasticity.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Bunzeck and Düzel report human fMRI and learning experiments; the loop paper supplies a mechanistic review, not a direct test of the entire human loop.
- Sources: Bunzeck, N., & Düzel, E. (2006). Absolute coding of stimulus novelty in the human substantia nigra/VTA. Neuron, 51(3), 369–379. DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2006.06.021. [Opened institutional abstract](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/de/publications/absolute-coding-of-stimulus-novelty-in-the-human-substantia-nigra/). Lisman, J. E., & Grace, A. A. (2005). The hippocampal-VTA loop: Controlling the entry of information into long-term memory. Neuron, 46(5), 703–713. DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2005.05.002. [Opened paper](https://meds371s.uchc.edu/lisman%20%26%20grace%202005.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: introduce one genuinely new example, perspective, or explanatory visual at a conceptual transition. Retain familiar navigation and labels so viewers can connect the novelty to the lesson.
- Caveats: Novelty is not the same as rarity, surprise, liking, or positive prediction error. BOLD is not dopamine release. Neither source establishes that more animation improves learning; frequent irrelevant changes may compete with comprehension.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Meaningful novelty is useful; endlessly rotating stimuli to sustain browsing increases the risk.

### A8 Give the ending substantive value
- Claim: Retrospective evaluations of some experiences disproportionately reflect their most intense moments and endings. In the original cold-water experiment, a less painful final 30 seconds made a longer painful trial more likely to be chosen again.
- Mechanism: Peak-end weighting in remembered experience; duration neglect.
- Evidence tier: T1 — A meta-analysis supplements the original controlled behavioural experiment; this is memory evidence, not dopamine evidence.
- Sources: Kahneman, D., Fredrickson, B. L., Schreiber, C. A., & Redelmeier, D. A. (1993). When more pain is preferred to less: Adding a better end. Psychological Science, 4(6), 401–405. DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x. [Opened society abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x/). Alaybek, B., Dalal, R. S., Fyffe, S., Aitken, J. A., Zhou, Y., Qu, X., Roman, A., & Baines, J. I. (2022). All’s well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 170, 104149. DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149. [Opened deposited abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html). The same authors’ 2024 corrigendum has DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2023.104278; [publisher search preview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597823000547).
- Applies to: slides | video | audio | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: end with the answer, a useful next action, or a clear sense of completion. Move logistics before that final resolution when feasible. Compare overall experience ratings immediately and after a delay.
- Caveats: Pain procedures are not slide decks. The meta-analysis’s coding was corrected and remains disputed in the editor’s note; no corrected numerical effect is quoted here. Peaks and endings do not erase the rest of the experience; see A25.
- Dark pattern risk: high — A pleasant ending can bias memory of an otherwise poor experience. Fix the experience as well as the finish.

### A9 Music has unusually direct dopamine evidence
- Claim: PET detected striatal dopamine release during intensely pleasurable music listening. Complementary fMRI distinguished caudate activity during anticipation from nucleus-accumbens activity near peak emotional responses.
- Mechanism: Musical anticipation and peak emotional arousal involving reward circuitry.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Dopamine-sensitive raclopride PET, autonomic measures, and complementary fMRI.
- Sources: Salimpoor, V. N., Benovoy, M., Larcher, K., Dagher, A., & Zatorre, R. J. (2011). Anatomically distinct dopamine release during anticipation and experience of peak emotion to music. Nature Neuroscience, 14(2), 257–262. DOI: 10.1038/nn.2726. [Opened primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21217764/).
- Applies to: audio | video | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: ask intended listeners which musical passages feel rewarding, then preserve the buildup and resolution of a chosen passage. If aligning a visual reveal, pilot the alignment against listener ratings rather than assuming a universal “drop.”
- Caveats: The listeners and music were selected for strong emotional responses. PET has limited temporal resolution; the finer anticipation/peak distinction used fMRI. These findings do not establish that any soundtrack, chill, cut, or crescendo produces dopamine release in a new audience.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Appropriate scoring supports expression; overpowering sound or emotional pressure can undermine viewers’ choice.

### A10 Dopamine manipulation changed musical reward
- Claim: Levodopa increased, and risperidone decreased, reported musical pleasure and motivation compared with placebo. This supports dopamine’s causal participation in music reward, without equating dopamine with pleasure.
- Mechanism: Dopaminergic modulation of abstract reward experience and motivation.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Double-blind, within-person pharmacological manipulation; 29 participants completed the study.
- Sources: Ferreri, L., Mas-Herrero, E., Zatorre, R. J., Ripollés, P., Gomez-Andres, A., Alicart, H., Olivé, G., Marco-Pallarés, J., Antonijoan, R. M., Valle, M., Riba, J., & Rodriguez-Fornells, A. (2019). Dopamine modulates the reward experiences elicited by music. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 116(9), 3793–3798. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1811878116. [Opened paper](https://www.ub.edu/brainvitge/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Ferreri_PNAS_2019-1.pdf).
- Applies to: audio | video | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: pilot a soundtrack using separate measures of enjoyment and desire to hear it again. Choose music with the intended listeners, and let their feedback determine the final cue.
- Caveats: Systemic drugs affect multiple processes, so the experiment does not isolate a single pleasure mechanism. It used a small laboratory sample and tested no media-editing recipe.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Listener-informed music choice is benign; the experiment is evidence, not a recommendation to manipulate physiology.

### A11 Surprise works in relation to uncertainty
- Claim: In studied pop-music chord sequences, pleasure depended on an interaction: high surprise could be pleasant when prior uncertainty was low, while a less surprising chord could be pleasant when uncertainty was high. Maximising surprise alone was not the rule.
- Mechanism: Learned musical expectations, uncertainty, and expectancy violation.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Behavioural ratings plus fMRI; the interaction appeared in auditory cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus, while nucleus accumbens reflected uncertainty.
- Sources: Cheung, V. K. M., Harrison, P. M. C., Meyer, L., Pearce, M. T., Haynes, J.-D., & Koelsch, S. (2019). Uncertainty and surprise jointly predict musical pleasure and amygdala, hippocampus, and auditory cortex activity. Current Biology, 29(23), 4084–4092.e4. DOI: 10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067. [Opened paper](https://www.stefan-koelsch.de/papers/cheung_harrison_meyer_pearce_haynes_koelsch_2019_uncertainty_and_surprise_jointly_predict_musical_pleasure_and_amygdala_hippocampus_and_auditory_cortex_activity_current_biology_29.pdf).
- Applies to: audio | video | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: establish a recognisable musical pattern before a deviation; after a confusing passage, try a reassuring resolution. Compare both with intended listeners. The experiment used 2.4-second chords; that is a stimulus setting, not a pacing prescription.
- Caveats: Model-derived probabilities came from Western commercial pop harmony. They need not match another culture’s or individual’s expectations. Neither pleasantness ratings nor BOLD reveal dopamine release; transfer from chords to narrative twists remains an analogy.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Expressive surprise is usually benign; deliberately startling listeners or denying resolution can create distress.

### A12 Aesthetic reward is highly personal
- Claim: Viewers differed substantially in which artworks moved them. Striatal activity varied with aesthetic ratings, and the most moving artworks also recruited parts of the default mode network associated with self-related thought.
- Mechanism: Aesthetic valuation combined with personal relevance and self-referential processing.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Human fMRI and individual artwork ratings; only 15 observers, so T1 does not imply high precision.
- Sources: Vessel, E. A., Starr, G. G., & Rubin, N. (2012). The brain on art: Intense aesthetic experience activates the default mode network. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 6, 66. DOI: 10.3389/fnhum.2012.00066. [Opened paper abstract and figures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22529785/).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: test two or three visual directions with the intended audience and ask what feels meaningful or moving, not only what looks polished. Use subject matter and imagery connected to their actual concerns.
- Caveats: Art viewing is not task-oriented UI use. Default-mode activity is not a dopamine measurement or a unique marker of beauty. This small study supplies no universal palette, composition, or aesthetic conversion advantage.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Audience-relevant visuals support communication; identity-based flattery or appropriation can exploit personal significance.

### A13 Ease of processing can contribute to liking
- Claim: Making neutral visual stimuli easier to process increased positive aesthetic judgments across experiments using matching primes, figure–ground contrast, and exposure duration. Processing fluency is one contributor to liking, not a complete definition of beauty.
- Mechanism: Processing fluency and affective attribution of ease.
- Evidence tier: T2 — Converging controlled behavioural manipulations, with a later review of related experiments; neither paper is a meta-analysis.
- Sources: Reber, R., Winkielman, P., & Schwarz, N. (1998). Effects of perceptual fluency on affective judgments. Psychological Science, 9(1), 45–48. DOI: 10.1111/1467-9280.00008. [Opened paper](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkiel/reber-winkielman-schwarz-Fluency-PS-1998.pdf). Reber, R., Schwarz, N., & Winkielman, P. (2004). Processing fluency and aesthetic pleasure: Is beauty in the perceiver’s processing experience? Personality and Social Psychology Review, 8(4), 364–382. DOI: 10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3. [Opened paper](https://dornsife.usc.edu/norbert-schwarz/wp-content/uploads/sites/231/2023/11/04_pspr_reber_et_al_beauty.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: improve figure–ground separation, keep labels consistent, and allow enough viewing time to identify the intended structure. Test ease of understanding and liking separately. Introduce expressive complexity after the basic organisation is legible.
- Caveats: The 1998 exposures were brief and used limited stimuli; unconstrained viewing was an acknowledged limitation. Expertise, familiarity, meaning, and deliberate artistic difficulty matter. Fluency can affect perceived truth without improving accuracy; no dopamine claim follows.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Clear-looking false claims may feel credible. Pair fluency with verifiable evidence and visible uncertainty.

### A14 An attractive face is not a universal reward
- Claim: In young heterosexual men, ratings of facial beauty and effort spent extending viewing were dissociable: beautiful male and female faces could be rated attractive, but viewing effort preferentially favoured attractive female faces. Reward-circuit responses likewise depended on the face category.
- Mechanism: Social/sexual incentive value distinguished from aesthetic appraisal.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Human fMRI combined with face ratings and a keypress task measuring viewing effort.
- Sources: Aharon, I., Etcoff, N., Ariely, D., Chabris, C. F., O’Connor, E., & Breiter, H. C. (2001). Beautiful faces have variable reward value: fMRI and behavioral evidence. Neuron, 32(3), 537–551. DOI: 10.1016/S0896-6273(01)00491-3. [Opened paper](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersOther/Beautiful%20Faces%20fMRI.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic
- How to apply: Design inference: choose a person’s face because their role or expression aids the message. Test relevance and trust with the audience; do not assume that a generic attractive model will improve understanding or motivation.
- Caveats: Restricted participant demographics and sexual orientation severely limit generalisation. Looking longer does not prove trust, learning, or ethical persuasion. The study measured BOLD, not dopamine release, and does not validate attractiveness-based conversion rules.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Sexualised or idealised faces can redirect attention, exploit social comparison, or conceal weak substantive value.

### A15 Eye contact changes the meaning of a face
- Claim: Direct versus averted gaze changed how facial attractiveness related to activity in reward-associated regions. This is evidence that gaze can alter social valuation, not that every pair of eyes automatically rewards a viewer.
- Mechanism: Social approach cues and context-dependent reward valuation.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Human fMRI of face attractiveness and gaze direction; dopamine was not measured.
- Sources: Kampe, K. K. W., Frith, C. D., Dolan, R. J., & Frith, U. (2001). Reward value of attractiveness and gaze. Nature, 413, 589. DOI: 10.1038/35098149. [Opened paper abstract](https://www.nature.com/articles/35098149). The paper has a 2002 correction, DOI: 10.1038/416602b; [correction search preview](https://www.nature.com/articles/416602b).
- Applies to: video | slides | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: try a presenter looking toward the camera while welcoming viewers or addressing them directly. Use gaze toward an object when directing attention to that object. Compare comfort and comprehension in audience testing.
- Caveats: The correction clarified localisation: activation extended into pallidum and anterior thalamus; nucleus accumbens proper was not activated. Static unfamiliar faces differ from live mutual gaze. Eye contact varies with culture, task, and personal comfort; staring can feel threatening. No universal number of seconds is established.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Appropriate address can help communication; forced intimacy or an implied watcher raises the risk.

### A16 Recognition by other people can carry reward value
- Claim: Receiving favourable information about one’s reputation activated striatal regions that overlapped with those activated by monetary reward. This supports social approval as a valued outcome under the study’s conditions.
- Mechanism: Social reward from favourable evaluation and reputation.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Human fMRI comparing social and monetary rewards in 19 participants.
- Sources: Izuma, K., Saito, D. N., & Sadato, N. (2008). Processing of social and monetary rewards in the human striatum. Neuron, 58(2), 284–294. DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2008.03.020. [Opened paper](https://www.nips.ac.jp/fmritms/publications/2008/2008_IzumaK_Neuron.pdf).
- Applies to: UI | live talk | video | slides
- How to apply: Design inference: acknowledge a real contribution specifically—“Your example exposed the missing assumption”—and make any public credit optional. Distinguish acknowledgment of effort from an objective claim about performance.
- Caveats: Reputation feedback in a scanner does not validate badges, public leaderboards, parasocial relationships, or the mere presence of a face. Neural overlap does not mean social approval is identical to money or that dopamine release occurred.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Public comparisons, approval counts, or withholding recognition can create pressure to return or perform for status.

### A17 An insight can feel rewarding
- Claim: Solving verbal association problems with a stronger reported aha experience was associated with activity in reward-related subcortical regions, including nucleus accumbens. The result supports an association between insight and reward processing.
- Mechanism: Insight, sudden restructuring, and affective relief/valuation.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Ultra-high-field 7-T fMRI during problem solving; final sample of 29 participants.
- Sources: Tik, M., Sladky, R., Di Bernardi Luft, C., Willinger, D., Hoffmann, A., Banissy, M. J., Bhattacharya, J., & Windischberger, C. (2018). Ultra-high-field fMRI insights on insight: Neural correlates of the Aha!-moment. Human Brain Mapping, 39(8), 3241–3252. DOI: 10.1002/hbm.24073. [Opened paper](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/29458/1/FullText.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: expose a puzzling relationship, let viewers try an explanation, then reveal the organising principle visually. For a chart, show the apparent exception before adding the variable that explains it. Verify comprehension after the reveal.
- Caveats: Small verbal-task study; ratings of insight and impasse and limited trials complicate interpretation, as discussed by Oh et al. in A18. fMRI does not measure dopamine release. An aha feeling does not certify that a proposed explanation is true.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Explanatory reveals help learning; false causal stories can create convincing but misleading aha experiences.

### A18 Insight reward is a hypothesis with individual differences
- Claim: During anagram solving, insight solutions showed a distinct frontal EEG gamma response related to participants’ trait reward sensitivity. Source modelling suggested a contribution from orbitofrontal cortex, consistent with—but not uniquely proving—reward processing.
- Mechanism: Insight-linked affective valuation; individual sensitivity to reward.
- Evidence tier: T3 — One EEG study with correlational trait analysis and indirect source reconstruction; it did not directly measure a deep reward circuit or dopamine.
- Sources: Oh, Y., Chesebrough, C., Erickson, B., Zhang, F., & Kounios, J. (2020). An insight-related neural reward signal. NeuroImage, 214, 116757. DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2020.116757. [Opened author-uploaded paper text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339968398_An_insight-related_neural_reward_signal).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: offer a solvable puzzle with an available explanation rather than requiring everyone to discover the answer. Ask separately whether the reveal was surprising, understandable, enjoyable, and correct.
- Caveats: Scalp EEG cannot directly identify dopamine firing or release; reconstructed sources are uncertain. The reported approximately 100-ms separation concerns neural responses relative to solving, not an editing-delay recommendation. Do not assume everyone experiences an aha as equally rewarding.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Optional puzzles are benign; repeatedly inducing confusion or treating confidence as evidence raises the risk.

### A19 Repeated cues can lose their response strength
- Claim: Habituation is a decrease in response after repeated stimulation that cannot be explained simply by sensory adaptation or fatigue. A response may partly recover after a break, and habituation can be stimulus-specific.
- Mechanism: Habituation: learning that repeated stimulation no longer requires the same response.
- Evidence tier: T2 — The consensus review synthesises repeatedly demonstrated behavioural characteristics across experimental systems; it is not a quantitative meta-analysis.
- Sources: Rankin, C. H., Abrams, T., Barry, R. J., Bhatnagar, S., Clayton, D. F., Colombo, J., Coppola, G., Geyer, M. A., Glanzman, D. L., Marsland, S., McSweeney, F. K., Wilson, D. A., Wu, C.-F., & Thompson, R. F. (2009). Habituation revisited: An updated and revised description of the behavioral characteristics of habituation. Neurobiology of Learning and Memory, 92(2), 135–138. DOI: 10.1016/j.nlm.2008.09.012. [Opened paper](https://homepages.ecs.vuw.ac.nz/~marslast/PUBS/Neurobiology2.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: reserve a conspicuous sound or animation for meaningful events; remove it from trivial repeated actions. Use quieter, familiar feedback for routine success. Test reactions over repeated use and after a break.
- Caveats: This is a general response-learning mechanism, not direct evidence that all repeated rewards lose pleasure. Learned predictions, sensory fatigue, satiation, and boredom are different processes. The review gives no universal repetition count; mere exposure can initially increase liking (A20).
- Dark pattern risk: high — Escalating stimulus intensity or rotating rewards to defeat habituation can create pressure and excessive checking.

### A20 Familiarity can increase liking before overexposure
- Claim: A meta-analysis found that repeated exposure’s relationship with liking often rose and then fell rather than increasing indefinitely. Familiarity and a diminishing orienting response can coexist, so repetition is not automatically bad.
- Mechanism: Mere exposure, familiarity, processing fluency, and overexposure.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Meta-analysis of 268 curve estimates from 81 articles; no dopamine measurement.
- Sources: Montoya, R. M., Horton, R. S., Vevea, J. L., Citkowicz, M., & Lauber, E. A. (2017). A re-examination of the mere exposure effect: The influence of repeated exposure on recognition, familiarity, and liking. Psychological Bulletin, 143(5), 459–498. DOI: 10.1037/bul0000085. [Opened primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28263645/).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: repeat a useful visual or musical motif so viewers can recognise it, while changing the information it carries. Track liking and annoyance across exposures rather than fixing an assumed “optimal” repeat count.
- Caveats: The inverted-U pattern held for visual but not auditory stimuli in this analysis and varied with exposure duration and measurement conditions. Applying a visual average curve to a soundtrack would overreach. These results do not establish one universal exposure threshold or media engagement effect.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Repetition can normalise a message or make it feel credible regardless of its accuracy.

### A21 Myth: humans have an eight-second attention span
- Claim: A popular claim says humans now have an eight-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish’s. It is used to justify constant cuts and extremely short explanations.
- Mechanism: Misclassification of attention, browsing behaviour, and memory as one fixed capacity.
- Evidence tier: T3 — A scholarly source audit/editorial, not a controlled test establishing a population-wide attention limit.
- Sources: Bradbury, N. A. (2016). Attention span during lectures: 8 seconds, 10 minutes, or more? Advances in Physiology Education, 40(4), 509–513. DOI: 10.1152/advan.00109.2016. [Publisher page found in search](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/10.1152/advan.00109.2016); [opened reproduction of the primary paper](https://www.scribd.com/document/369398281/Attention-Span-During-Lectures).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Instead, define the task and measure an observable outcome: understanding, successful completion, recall, or where people stop. Choose segment length from that task and audience testing; do not insert a cut every eight seconds on biological grounds.
- Caveats: The cited audit rejects the goldfish comparison and distinguishes web-page behaviour from sustained task attention. It does not supply a replacement universal duration or prove attention is unchanged over time. Task, interest, distraction, and measurement all matter.
- Dark pattern risk: high — The myth can rationalise exhausting edits and repeated interruption that serve retention metrics over understanding.

### A22 Myth: every lecture loses attention after ten minutes
- Claim: A widespread rule says audience attention reliably collapses after about 10–15 minutes, requiring a reset at that interval. Reviews found little adequate evidence for a universal threshold.
- Mechanism: Attention and mind wandering, moderated by people, content, teaching, and context.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Critical review of note-taking, observer, self-report, and physiological studies; not a quantitative meta-analysis.
- Sources: Wilson, K., & Korn, J. H. (2007). Attention during lectures: Beyond ten minutes. Teaching of Psychology, 34(2), 85–89. DOI: 10.1080/00986280701291291. [Opened paper](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Karen-Wilson-5/publication/234649194_Attention_During_Lectures_Beyond_Ten_Minutes/links/57644c9108ae421c44830d91/Attention-During-Lectures-Beyond-Ten-Minutes.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Instead, place a question, example, pause, or activity at a meaningful conceptual boundary. Pilot comprehension and attention at those boundaries. A ten-minute planning block can be a convenience, but label it as such.
- Caveats: Weak support for a fixed limit does not mean uninterrupted lectures are always effective. Note taking or visible movement is an imperfect proxy for attention; individual differences and instructor effects prevent a single stopwatch rule.
- Dark pattern risk: low — A convenient timing heuristic is benign; presenting it as neuroscience creates misleading authority.

### A23 Myth: people remember fixed percentages by medium
- Claim: The familiar retention pyramid claims that people remember 10% of reading, 20% of hearing, and progressively more from seeing or doing. These percentages are often falsely attached to Dale’s Cone of Experience.
- Mechanism: A fabricated measurement claim, often used to imply automatic multisensory learning gains.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Historical and bibliographic audit of the chart’s provenance; not a memory experiment or meta-analysis.
- Sources: Subramony, D. P., Molenda, M., Betrus, A. K., & Thalheimer, W. (2014). The mythical retention chart and the corruption of Dale’s Cone of Experience. Educational Technology, 54(6), 6–16. [Journal issue and article record found in search](https://www.jstor.org/stable/i40186225). Thalheimer, W. (2015). Mythical retention data & the corrupted cone. Work-Learning Research. [Opened coauthor explanation with the four original citations](https://www.worklearning.com/2015/01/05/mythical-retention-data-the-corrupted-cone/).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Instead, choose sight, sound, or interaction for what it communicates, and test the actual learning outcome after an explicitly stated delay. Compare formats with equivalent content and time; report observed results rather than pyramid percentages.
- Caveats: Dale’s cone was not a measured ranking of retention. Without specifying learners, material, exposure, test, and delay, these percentages are meaningless. This audit does not establish that all media are equally effective.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Unsupported numbers can sell unnecessary video, training, or interaction under scientific-looking authority.

### A24 Myth: reward-region activation proves a dopamine hit
- Claim: A common design claim treats activity in a reward-associated brain region as proof that a stimulus released dopamine or caused pleasure. That inference is not justified by an ordinary fMRI result alone.
- Mechanism: Reverse inference; distinguishing neural activity, neurochemistry, motivation, and subjective experience.
- Evidence tier: T3 — A methodological analysis of neuroimaging inference, with database-based illustration; not a direct media reward experiment.
- Sources: Poldrack, R. A. (2006). Can cognitive processes be inferred from neuroimaging data? Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 10(2), 59–63. DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2005.12.004. [Opened paper](https://brainmapping.org/NITP/PNA/Readings/Poldrack2006.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Instead, write “associated with activity in reward-related regions” for suitable fMRI evidence; reserve dopamine-release language for an actual neurochemical measure. For design evaluation, collect enjoyment, curiosity, usefulness, recall, or behaviour and name the outcome measured.
- Caveats: Reverse inference can be informative when selectivity and prior probabilities are accounted for; it is not always worthless. Reward regions serve multiple functions. Even genuine dopamine evidence in a study does not let a designer measure or infer individual audience dopamine.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Neurochemical claims can lend false certainty to engagement tricks and obscure their real outcomes.

### A25 Myth: only the peak and ending matter
- Claim: An overextended version of the peak-end rule says the middle of an experience is irrelevant and a good final flourish can rescue anything. Research on remembered experience does not justify that design rule.
- Mechanism: Retrospective evaluation, peak/end weighting, average experience, and question framing.
- Evidence tier: T1 — A meta-analysis supports comparison with average experience; a later large observational study tests everyday-life boundaries.
- Sources: Alaybek, B., Dalal, R. S., Fyffe, S., Aitken, J. A., Zhou, Y., Qu, X., Roman, A., & Baines, J. I. (2022). All’s well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 170, 104149. DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149. [Opened deposited primary abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html). Scharbert, J., Utesch, K., Reiter, T., ter Horst, J., van Zalk, M., Back, M. D., & Rau, R. (2025; first published online 2024). If you were happy and you know it, clap your hands! Testing the peak-end rule for retrospective judgments of well-being in everyday life. European Journal of Personality, 39(1), 55–69. DOI: 10.1177/08902070241235969. [Opened author-hosted paper](https://richrau.github.io/publication/2025_01_17_scharbert_et_al-2025-peakendrule/2025_01_17_Scharbert_et_al-2025-peakendrule.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Instead, review the whole sequence for value and frustration, then improve its important moments and ending. Measure task success alongside overall and delayed evaluations. State the evaluation question consistently across versions.
- Caveats: The meta-analysis found peak-end prediction comparable to the overall mean, not evidence that the mean is irrelevant; its coding correction is flagged in A8. Four everyday-life samples (N = 1,889) supported peak/end effects with boundaries depending on question wording and timescale. This is qualified generalisation, not a failed replication or a media trial.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Using a pleasant finish to distract from accumulated friction manipulates remembered quality rather than improving it.
