## Grounding answer: what can the skill build on?

**A1 and A2 should govern its vocabulary.** Separate expectation updating, incentive motivation, and pleasure. Define “better than expected” against a viewer’s actual expectation and the value of the outcome, rather than treating novelty or shock as reward. These mechanisms have direct neural and intervention evidence, but their use in a presentation remains an inference. The 1998 Berridge paper’s broader historical argument about reward learning should not be adopted wholesale. [Schultz et al.](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~sswang/fundamental-readings-for-Wang-lab-members/schultz_montague97_science.pdf), [Berridge and Robinson](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/BerridgeRobinsonBrResRev1998.pdf).

**A5–A6 support a curiosity–answer–learning structure.** Their human task evidence is strong enough to motivate asking a meaningful question and testing memory of the resolution. Neural associations do not establish a dopamine-mediated causal chain, and incidental-face memory does not license inserting irrelevant information into a teaser. A4’s information-gap theory is a useful organising account with weaker evidence status as sourced here. [Kang et al.](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf), [Gruber et al.](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2zd605r7/qt2zd605r7_noSplash_442c2e267829848246b0ffc2e9beded1.pdf).

**A9–A10 are the strongest directly relevant human media evidence.** They support music as a possible source of anticipation and reward and show why listener selection matters. PET and pharmacology make the dopamine link more substantial than a generic fMRI association; neither validates a universal soundtrack formula. The design skill should test musical enjoyment and desire to continue separately. [Salimpoor et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21217764/), [Ferreri et al.](https://www.ub.edu/brainvitge/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Ferreri_PNAS_2019-1.pdf).

**A13, A19, and A20 support a readable, recognisable experience with selective variation.** Fluency has convergent behavioural support; habituation is a replicated response-learning phenomenon; mere exposure has meta-analytic support. Their combined lesson is to measure both response strength and liking over repetition, rather than assuming that novelty always wins. A19’s T2 classification reflects the underlying replicated behavioural literature summarised by a consensus review, not a newly inspected large media trial. [Reber et al.](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkiel/reber-winkielman-schwarz-Fluency-PS-1998.pdf), [Rankin et al.](https://homepages.ecs.vuw.ac.nz/~marslast/PUBS/Neurobiology2.pdf), [Montoya et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28263645/).

**A8 is a qualified memory principle.** Give the ending actual value, but do not claim the middle is irrelevant or promise that a flourish rescues a bad experience. The meta-analysis has a coding correction and an explicit editorial dispute; the original abstract also reports peak-end prediction comparable to prediction from the overall mean. A25 provides a large everyday-life study showing dependence on framing and timescale. Do not quote an unverified corrected effect size. [Kahneman et al.](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x/), [Alaybek et al., deposited abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html), [Scharbert et al.](https://richrau.github.io/publication/2025_01_17_scharbert_et_al-2025-peakendrule/2025_01_17_Scharbert_et_al-2025-peakendrule.pdf).

**Use A3, A7, A11–A12, and A14–A18 as bounded options, not universal recipes.** Their T1 labels often mean a small fMRI experiment, not replicated media-design efficacy. Reward uncertainty does not establish a 50% optimal reward schedule; the hippocampal–VTA loop is a proposed integrative model; chord probabilities depend on musical culture; art and face responses are personal and demographically constrained. The eye-contact localisation was corrected. EEG source modelling in A18 is specifically T3. See the full sources and limits in the corresponding findings entries.

**The most common overclaims to reject:** dopamine equals pleasure; every surprise is positive reward; more uncertainty is always better; every novel animation improves learning; attractive faces or eye contact reliably increase trust; an aha certifies truth; brain activation proves dopamine release; everyone loses attention after eight seconds or ten minutes; retention percentages are fixed by medium; only peaks and endings matter. A21–A25 make the last five beliefs explicit myth entries. The measurement guardrail in A24 should be a core constraint even though its methodological source is classified T3. [Poldrack](https://brainmapping.org/NITP/PNA/Readings/Poldrack2006.pdf).

### A1 Reward depends on what was expected
- Claim: Many midbrain dopamine neurons signal outcomes better than predicted, respond little to fully predicted rewards, and reduce firing at expected reward omission. With learning, responses can shift to predictive cues.
- Mechanism: Reward prediction error: updating expectations about future value.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Primate dopamine-neuron recordings interpreted alongside computational learning models.
- Sources: Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward. Science, 275(5306), 1593–1599. DOI: 10.1126/science.275.5306.1593. [Opened paper](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~sswang/fundamental-readings-for-Wang-lab-members/schultz_montague97_science.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: establish one understandable expectation, then deliver a useful outcome that exceeds it—for example, an explanation that also solves a second practical problem. Ask viewers what they expected before judging the reveal.
- Caveats: Animal conditioning is not media design. Surprise need not have positive value, and expected rewards can remain enjoyable. No media effect size or optimal reveal delay follows.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Inflated promises and deliberately withheld rewards can turn expectation management into clickbait or compulsive checking.

### A10 Dopamine manipulation changed musical reward
- Claim: Levodopa increased, and risperidone decreased, reported musical pleasure and motivation compared with placebo. This supports dopamine’s causal participation in music reward, without equating dopamine with pleasure.
- Mechanism: Dopaminergic modulation of abstract reward experience and motivation.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Double-blind, within-person pharmacological manipulation; 29 participants completed the study.
- Sources: Ferreri, L., Mas-Herrero, E., Zatorre, R. J., Ripollés, P., Gomez-Andres, A., Alicart, H., Olivé, G., Marco-Pallarés, J., Antonijoan, R. M., Valle, M., Riba, J., & Rodriguez-Fornells, A. (2019). Dopamine modulates the reward experiences elicited by music. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 116(9), 3793–3798. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1811878116. [Opened paper](https://www.ub.edu/brainvitge/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Ferreri_PNAS_2019-1.pdf).
- Applies to: audio | video | live talk
- How to apply: Design inference: pilot a soundtrack using separate measures of enjoyment and desire to hear it again. Choose music with the intended listeners, and let their feedback determine the final cue.
- Caveats: Systemic drugs affect multiple processes, so the experiment does not isolate a single pleasure mechanism. It used a small laboratory sample and tested no media-editing recipe.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Listener-informed music choice is benign; the experiment is evidence, not a recommendation to manipulate physiology.
