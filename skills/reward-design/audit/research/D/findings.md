Unit D gathers public industry practice for the 2026-10-10 reward-design research run. Most entries are craft frameworks or product examples, not controlled evidence that a technique improves every audience's experience. Mechanism labels are design interpretations unless the cited research tests them; the application recipes are adaptations unless a source-specific number is identified. Dopamine concerns reward prediction error, anticipation and wanting, not pleasure itself. None of these industry examples measures audience dopamine. T1 below identifies research syntheses, not direct dopamine measurements. Each entry links to a source found and opened in this session; blocked publisher pages and other access limitations are recorded in REPORT.md.

### D1 YouTube: fulfil the opening promise
- Claim: YouTube recommends making the opening match the title and thumbnail, then testing different introductions. Its intro metric records the audience remaining after 30 seconds; this is an analytics checkpoint, not a biological attention limit.
- Mechanism: Expectation matching, relevance and anticipation of a promised answer; these mechanisms are inferred from the guidance.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Official platform practice and analytics documentation, without a controlled experiment establishing an optimal opening length.
- Sources: YouTube (n.d.; accessed 2026-10-10). “Measure key moments for audience retention.” YouTube Help. https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415
- Applies to: video | live talk | slides
- How to apply: For a prototype, show the promised subject and state the useful outcome within the first 5 seconds, then demonstrate progress before 30 seconds. The 5-second target is an adaptation. Compare intro retention across similar-length videos and revise the opening or packaging when they disagree.
- Caveats: YouTube specifies 30 seconds for this metric, not a required hook duration or guaranteed retention percentage. Audience, traffic source and video length affect comparisons. In talks and slides, test comprehension directly rather than transferring YouTube metrics.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Becomes misleading clickbait if the promised answer is exaggerated or never delivered.

### D2 Myth: every retention spike is a successful payoff
- Claim: Creators often treat replay spikes as proof that a moment delighted viewers. YouTube explicitly offers another explanation: unclear content can make viewers replay a section.
- Mechanism: Rewatching can reflect interest or a comprehension repair, which are different processes.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Official interpretation of platform metrics; no experiment separating the causes of each spike.
- Sources: YouTube (n.d.; accessed 2026-10-10). “Measure key moments for audience retention.” YouTube Help, sections “Spikes,” “Dips” and “Top moments.” https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415
- Applies to: video | infographic
- How to apply: Rewatch the segment behind a spike and ask viewers what they were trying to see. For a confusing chart, try clearer labels or a longer hold. For a valued demonstration, bring that material earlier. Check understanding alongside retention before copying the edit.
- Caveats: A spike can mix rewatches and shares; a dip can mix skipping and departures. Neither identifies emotion or dopamine. This is a metric interpretation error, not a failed replication of a scientific theory.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Optimizing replay counts alone can reward deliberately obscure or misleading edits.

### D3 MrBeast memo: progress, re-hooks and a promised ending
- Claim: A publicly circulated memo attributed to Jimmy Donaldson describes an opening that meets expectations, rapid progress in minutes 1–3, and re-engagement around minutes 3 and 6. It reserves anticipation for a final prize or payoff.
- Mechanism: Curiosity/information gap, investment in an unfolding outcome, novelty and renewed attention; no neural mechanism was measured.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Public production memo and practitioner testimony; attribution and performance claims were not independently authenticated here.
- Sources: Donaldson, Jimmy, attributed (n.d.; publicly circulated 2024). “How to succeed in MrBeast production.” Production onboarding memo, PDF pp. 5–8, mirrored at https://cdn.alexanderjarvis.com/2024/09/How-To-Succeed-At-MrBeast-Production.pdf ; Willison, Simon (2024, September 15). “How to succeed in MrBeast production (leaked PDF).” Simon Willison’s Weblog. https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/15/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/
- Applies to: video | live talk
- How to apply: State one unresolved outcome, show meaningful progress, and close that loop on screen. For a comparable long video, prototype story-relevant re-hooks near 3 and 6 minutes. Use a new obstacle, demonstration or scene as a pattern interrupt; keep it connected to the current question.
- Caveats: Those times belong to this operation's format, not all videos. The memo does not validate “open loops” as a general causal law or establish a fixed cut interval. Its extraordinary budgets and established audience limit transfer.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Withholding an answer, repeatedly moving the payoff, or hiding that the substantive content is over can exploit viewers' time.

### D4 TikTok: hook, body, close with purposeful sound
- Claim: TikTok advises an early hook followed by value and a clear close. Its Creative Codes page reports that 90% of ad-recall impact is captured within six seconds; a separate official guide recommends grabbing attention in the first three seconds.
- Mechanism: Early relevance, surprise, arousal and audiovisual attention; platform claims concern recall, not dopamine.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Official creative guidance with commissioned research summaries; the six-second analysis is not publicly detailed enough here to judge causal validity.
- Sources: TikTok for Business (n.d.; accessed 2026-10-10). “Creative Codes: 6 principles for creating on TikTok.” Official blog; cites TikTok “Value of View” Analysis (2020). https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en/blog/creative-best-practices-top-performing-ads?redirected=1 ; TikTok for Business (n.d.; accessed 2026-10-10). “Why video advertising belongs at the heart of your marketing.” Official guide. https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en/guides/video-advertising-guide?redirected=1
- Applies to: video | audio
- How to apply: Begin with the problem, result or demonstration in seconds 0–3. Communicate value by six seconds, then supply evidence and an appropriate call to action. Pair voice, music or an impact sound with the relevant visual beat, and provide captions. Compare opening variants using both recall and conversion.
- Caveats: “90%” is the platform's reported ad-recall result, not 90% of attention or sales. It does not prove three seconds is universally optimal. Fast cuts and sound can obstruct understanding; no validated cut frequency is supplied.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High if an alarming hook or disguised promotion misrepresents the eventual content.

### D5 Meta: make the first three seconds legible without sound
- Claim: Facebook's 2016 guidance reports up to 47% of campaign value in the first three seconds and up to 74% in the first ten. It recommends immediate visual communication and sound-independent meaning.
- Mechanism: Rapid recognition, processing fluency and accessibility; the reported outcome is advertising value, not a fixed attention capacity.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Official commissioned/industry research summary; methods, uncertainty and campaign selection are not fully exposed on the opened page.
- Sources: Facebook (2016, February 10). “Capta la atención con nuevas funcionalidades para anuncios de vídeo.” Meta Newsroom, Spanish edition; reports Facebook/Nielsen findings and internal tests. https://about.fb.com/es/news/2016/02/capta-la-atencion-con-nuevas-funcionalidades-para-anuncios-de-video/
- Applies to: video | UI
- How to apply: Show the subject, product or benefit immediately and caption essential speech. Test whether the first three seconds make sense muted. Preserve optional sound for emphasis. The source also reports an average 12% increase in viewing time for captioned ads in internal tests.
- Caveats: Retain “up to”; 47% is not an average guaranteed lift, a retention rate or a neuroscience finding. This is historical Facebook-feed guidance, not verified contemporary Reels performance. The page itself says there is no universal creative solution.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Unexpected loud autoplay can be intrusive; the same source reports negative reactions to unexpectedly loud mobile-feed ads.

### D6 Game juice and Game Feel: amplify an action's consequence
- Claim: Jonasson and Purho demonstrate how feedback layers can make a simple game feel more satisfying. Swink treats game feel as control, simulated space and polish together; decoration alone is insufficient.
- Mechanism: Perceived agency, action–outcome feedback and audiovisual emphasis; these are craft explanations rather than measured reward circuitry.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Industry demonstration and design book, with a practitioner counterargument against indiscriminate polish.
- Sources: Jonasson, Martin, and Purho, Petri (2012). “Juice It or Lose It.” GDC Europe, Independent Games Summit. https://gdcvault.com/play/1016487/Juice-It-or-Lose ; Swink, Steve (2009 copyright; released 2008). Game Feel: A Game Designer's Guide to Virtual Sensation. Morgan Kaufmann, ch. 1. Opened copy: https://madwomb.com/tutorials/gamedesign/books/book_GameFeel_SteveSwink.pdf ; Kelly, Folmer (2014). “Don't Juice It or Lose It.” GDC Europe. https://gdcvault.com/play/1020937/Don-t-Juice-It-or
- Applies to: UI | video | audio
- How to apply: Start with a clear action and a dependable result. Add one congruent feedback layer at a time: a sound, a visual response or an impact effect. Compare the plain and layered versions for clarity and perceived control. Keep status information readable and offer reduced motion.
- Caveats: The opened GDC pages provide session descriptions, not a controlled evaluation. Kelly warns that contextless bounce, dust and other eye candy can damage immersion. Swink's real-time-control definition does not transfer intact to a passive slide.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Feedback becomes manipulative when it disguises failure or encourages unnecessary repeat actions.

### D7 MDA: design the desired experience backwards
- Claim: MDA separates rules and components, their behavior during use, and the experience they create. It helps explain why adding a reward mechanic does not automatically produce an engaging payoff.
- Mechanism: Agency, challenge, discovery, fellowship or dramatic tension, depending on the chosen experience; MDA is not a neural theory.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Influential design framework developed through industry workshops; no controlled comparison of framework effectiveness.
- Sources: Hunicke, Robin; LeBlanc, Marc; Zubek, Robert (2004). “MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research.” Proceedings of the AAAI Workshop on Challenges in Game AI. https://www.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf
- Applies to: UI | slides | video | infographic
- How to apply: Write the intended experience first, such as discovery or competence. Identify the interaction that should create it, then the concrete rules and feedback. For a data reveal: discovery → make a prediction and compare → a drawing input plus actual-data overlay. Test whether users experience discovery, not merely whether they clicked.
- Caveats: Applying MDA to presentations or data stories is an adaptation. Its aesthetics vocabulary describes experiences, not universal measures of fun. Emergent behavior requires iteration; a checklist cannot guarantee the result.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Can become high if the intended “experience” is submission or compulsive return rather than a user's chosen goal.

### D8 Flow: tune challenge, goals and control together
- Claim: Challenge matched to skill is associated with flow, but it is not a sufficient recipe by itself. A 28-study meta-analysis also identifies clear goals and sense of control as robust contributors.
- Mechanism: Flow, competence and intrinsic motivation; the synthesis does not measure dopamine.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Meta-analysis of challenge–skill balance and flow across contexts, covering 9,620 participants for the flow analysis.
- Sources: Fong, Carlton J.; Zaleski, Diana J.; Leach, Jennifer K. (2015; online 2014). “The challenge–skill balance and antecedents of flow: A meta-analytic investigation.” The Journal of Positive Psychology, 10(5), 425–446. DOI: 10.1080/17439760.2014.967799. Opened full-text page: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267450034_The_challenge-skill_balance_and_antecedents_of_flow_A_meta-analytic_investigation ; Csikszentmihalyi, Mihaly (1990). Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience. Harper & Row; bibliographic context for the theory.
- Applies to: UI | live talk | infographic
- How to apply: Establish one clear goal and recognizable feedback. Introduce an achievable task, then increase complexity after the person demonstrates competence; offer a hint or easier route when they stall. For an interactive infographic, teach the first comparison before requesting a more difficult prediction.
- Caveats: The relationship is moderate and varies with domain, culture and measurement. The synthesis supports no universal difficulty curve, success percentage or timing interval. Transferring this evidence to passive media is especially uncertain.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High if adaptive challenge is used to keep someone engaged past their intended stopping point.

### D9 Hooked: variable rewards require an agency check
- Claim: Eyal's Hook model cycles through a trigger, action, variable reward and investment. It is an industry framework for habitual return, not proof that an app creates pleasure or a measured dopamine surge.
- Mechanism: Habit learning, anticipation, uncertainty and wanting; proposed incentive-salience interpretation, not measured in the cited product examples.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Practitioner book and author's explanatory page; no controlled validation of the complete model.
- Sources: Eyal, Nir, with Hoover, Ryan (2014). Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products. Portfolio. Author's public explanation: Eyal, Nir (n.d.; accessed 2026-10-10), “Hooked Resources & Top Articles on User Behavior,” NirAndFar. https://www.nirandfar.com/hooked-user-behavior-resources/
- Applies to: UI
- How to apply: Map the user's chosen goal, cue, useful action, actual benefit and optional investment. Prefer dependable value and a clear stopping state. If content varies, explain why it varies; allow reminder controls and exit. Audit whether variability serves discovery or merely repeated checking.
- Caveats: Eyal's page uses unsupported dopamine rhetoric that this entry does not adopt. The framework does not show that every predictable reward fails or every variable one succeeds. Entertainment retention and task success are different goals.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Variable checking, infinite feeds, social approval chasing and investments that make leaving costly can exploit wanting.

### D10 Duolingo: lower the streak barrier and protect recovery
- Claim: Duolingo reports that allowing one lesson to extend a streak, independently of a daily goal, increased Day-14 retention by 3.3% relative. The company also documents repairing streaks after service outages.
- Mechanism: Habit consistency, visible progress and anticipated loss of accumulated progress; the specific causal mechanism was not tested.
- Evidence tier: T3 — A reported product A/B experiment; sample size and uncertainty are not disclosed in the opened post.
- Sources: Duolingo product team (2020). “Improving the streak.” Duolingo Blog. https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/ ; Duolingo engineering team (2021). “Protecting streaks from site issues.” Duolingo Blog. https://blog.duolingo.com/protecting-streaks-from-site-issues/ (corporate authors used because bylines were not exposed in the opened pages).
- Applies to: UI
- How to apply: Separate a small consistency action from a stretch goal. Display both honestly. Add breaks and outage recovery, and test meaningful learning alongside return rates. A missed day should not erase the record of skills already acquired.
- Caveats: The 3.3% is a relative increase, not 3.3 percentage points. Fewer learners reached their daily goals in the experiment. Return rates do not establish better learning or wellbeing; longer-term comparisons are not randomized evidence.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Streak anxiety, guilt notifications and pressure to pay to protect progress can displace the user's actual goal.

### D11 Apple keynotes: stage a visible reveal, with a selective encore
- Claim: Gallo's analysis highlights simple road maps, tangible demonstrations and a carefully staged reveal. He notes that Jobs sometimes used “one more thing,” while omitting it on other occasions to preserve surprise.
- Mechanism: Anticipation, expectation violation, concrete demonstration and processing fluency; no audience dopamine measurements.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Practitioner analysis of selected successful keynotes, not an experimental test.
- Sources: Gallo, Carmine (2009). The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs: How to Be Insanely Great in Front of Any Audience. McGraw-Hill, “Encore: One More Thing.” Opened preview: https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-presentation-secrets/9780071636759/app01.html ; Gallo, Carmine (2009). “The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs.” Presentation with explanatory notes, pp. 20, 37, 47–50. https://www.cedma-europe.org/newsletter%20articles/WorkplaceXpert/The%20Presentation%20Secrets%20of%20Steve%20Jobs%20%28Dec%2009%29.pdf
- Applies to: slides | live talk | video
- How to apply: Group the road map into three meaningful parts as a craft starting point. Establish a concrete problem, then reveal the working solution through a demonstration or prop. Plan the moment's visibility and pause for inspection. Save an encore for a substantial additional benefit rather than using it routinely.
- Caveats: Three is an organizing heuristic, not a strict memory law. Celebrity, brand attachment and press coverage confound keynote impact. The presentation's dopamine and fixed-retention-percentage explanations are not evidence for the staging technique.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High if theatrical staging conceals limitations or overstates a prototype's readiness.

### D12 Pixar story spine: earn the ending through causes
- Claim: Pixar's teaching material uses a story spine to organize meaningful beats and cause-and-effect decisions. It credits improv instructor Kenn Adams rather than treating the device as Pixar's invention.
- Mechanism: Narrative causality, curiosity about consequences and resolution of an established problem.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Professional storytelling instruction; no controlled test of this specific template's audience effect.
- Sources: Pixar Animation Studios and Khan Academy (n.d.; accessed 2026-10-10). “Story spine.” Pixar in a Box, The Art of Storytelling, Story Structure; teaching video/transcript featuring Derek Thompson and colleagues. https://www.khanacademy.org/partner-content/hass-storytelling/storytelling-pixar-in-a-box/ah-piab-story-structure/v/video1a-fine
- Applies to: video | slides | live talk
- How to apply: Outline the normal situation, an interruption, consequences of decisions, the resolution and the changed situation. Write the causal connection between each beat. For an explainer: a team has a recurring problem; a constraint changes; their response creates a consequence; a tested solution resolves it.
- Caveats: The opened page exposed no transcript lines, but search exposed the primary transcript, including attribution and causal-beat guidance. This is a useful organizing scaffold, not a compulsory formula. Non-narrative explanations may need a different structure.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Causal storytelling can mislead if an attractive sequence is presented as evidence of causation.

### D13 Duarte's sparkline: alternate the current and possible worlds
- Claim: Duarte's presentation framework alternates the current situation with a possible improvement, building toward a future the audience can help create. It makes contrast and tension part of the argument's structure.
- Mechanism: Anticipation, contrast, narrative tension and resolution; proposed design interpretation, not neural measurement.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Practitioner analysis and teaching framework, derived from selected speeches and presentations.
- Sources: Duarte, Nancy, and Duarte, Inc. (n.d.; accessed 2026-10-10). “The Storytelling Framework Every Business Leader Should Know.” Duarte resources. https://www.duarte.com/resources/storytelling-framework/ ; Duarte, Nancy (2010). Resonate: Present Visual Stories That Transform Audiences. Wiley; book origin of the framework. Opened author's book description: https://www.duarte.com/resources/books/resonate/
- Applies to: slides | live talk | video
- How to apply: State the present problem, show a credible improved state, then alternate the two through your main arguments. End with an achievable action and its expected benefit. An adaptation for a consulting deck: current delay → feasible target → causes → demonstrated remedy → costs and tradeoffs → decision.
- Caveats: No controlled evidence here establishes an optimal number of alternations or superior persuasion. A rosy future without evidence can create disappointment. Some audiences need the recommendation first; preserve uncertainty and implementation constraints.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High when current pain is inflated or a speculative future is presented as certain.

### D14 TED: make one idea consequential, then measure more than applause
- Claim: TEDx's speaker guide recommends a focused idea, an opening that makes people care, evidence and an ending that explains the implications. A TED audience-comment study found that reception differed between YouTube and TED's own site.
- Mechanism: Relevance, curiosity, explanation and anticipated personal consequence; comments are an audience response, not a reward-circuit measure.
- Evidence tier: T4 — The recipe is official craft guidance; the supporting audience analysis is observational T3, not a causal test of the recipe.
- Sources: TEDx (n.d.; accessed 2026-10-10). Speaker Guide, pp. 2–4. https://storage.ted.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf?lang=es ; Tsou, Andrew; Thelwall, Mike; Mongeon, Philippe; Sugimoto, Cassidy R. (2014). “A Community of Curious Souls: An Analysis of Commenting Behavior on TED Talks Videos.” PLOS ONE, 9(4), e93609. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0093609. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0093609
- Applies to: live talk | slides | video
- How to apply: Write the idea in one or two sentences. Open with a relatable consequence or intriguing observation, explain it, support it and show what changes if the audience accepts it. Ask viewers to explain the idea afterward; inspect comments separately by platform.
- Caveats: Comments are self-selected and reflect platform norms and speaker biases. The paper does not establish a viral-talk formula. TED's under-18-minute format is an editorial constraint, not a proven universal attention span. Emotion serves the idea rather than replacing evidence.
- Dark pattern risk: low — A compelling personal story can lend unwarranted credibility to unsupported claims.

### D15 IPA: emotional brand building works on a different timescale
- Claim: Binet and Field's IPA analysis associates emotional campaigns with stronger long-term effects, while rational messages can generate immediate responses. “Emotional ads always outperform rational ads” overstates the finding.
- Mechanism: Emotional associations, memory and repeated brand learning; the databank does not measure dopamine.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Retrospective industry campaign analysis; its aggregation is called “meta-analysis” in the deck, but is not a synthesis of randomized trials.
- Sources: Binet, Les, and Field, Peter (2013). The Long and the Short of It: Balancing Short and Long-Term Marketing Strategies. Institute of Practitioners in Advertising. Official summary presentation, “10 key principles of success,” pp. 2, 12–16 and 31. https://ipa.co.uk/media/14463/long_and_short_of_it_presentation_final_030424.pdf ; publication page: https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/documents/the-long-and-the-short-of-it-presentation
- Applies to: video | audio | infographic
- How to apply: Choose the business timescale before the emotional arc. For brand building, associate a relevant human outcome with recognizable brand cues and repeated exposure. For immediate action, include the useful offer and a clear response. Measure immediate behavior and longer-term brand outcomes separately.
- Caveats: The deck covers 996 campaigns, 700 brands and 83 categories. Selected effectiveness cases, campaign budgets and other confounds limit causal inference. It does not establish that a single moving ad creates long-term profit, nor that a 60:40 budget split applies universally.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High if emotion masks terms, costs, evidence or who is advertising.

### D16 Super Bowl ads: compress a complete dramatic arc
- Claim: Quesenberry and Coolsen found higher audience ratings for Super Bowl commercials with fuller dramatic story structures. The result concerns liking in a particular advertising setting, not proof of sales or reward chemistry.
- Mechanism: Narrative tension, involvement with a character, emotional resolution and payoff.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Observational content analysis of 108 commercials over two years.
- Sources: Quesenberry, Keith A., and Coolsen, Michael K. (2014). “What Makes a Super Bowl Ad Super? Five-Act Dramatic Form Affects Consumer Super Bowl Advertising Ratings.” Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice, 22(4), 437–454. DOI: 10.2753/MTP1069-6679220406. Publisher abstract located/open attempted: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2753/MTP1069-6679220406 ; opened institutional account: Rosen, Jill (2014, January 31), “Super Bowl ads: Stories beat sex and humor, Johns Hopkins researcher finds,” Johns Hopkins Hub. https://hub.jhu.edu/2014/01/31/super-bowl-ads/
- Applies to: video | audio
- How to apply: For a 30–60-second prototype, establish a person and goal, introduce a difficulty, show a turning point, then a consequence and resolution. Give the brand a truthful role in the outcome. Those durations are production starting points, not a measured optimum.
- Caveats: Story structure was not randomly assigned. Ad ratings are not purchases; celebrity, production quality and brand familiarity may matter. US event viewers and self-selected raters limit cultural transfer. The publisher page was blocked; the institutional account supplied the sample and finding.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High when emotional resolution implies product benefits the product cannot deliver.

### D17 Trailer editing: three acts, accents and a final button
- Claim: Trailer editors use setup, escalation and climax as an organizing arc, often adding a cold open and a final “button” such as a joke or action beat. Lieu recommends smaller peaks inside the arc rather than uninterrupted acceleration.
- Mechanism: Anticipation, contrast, novelty, tension and release; no dopamine measurement or validated rhythm formula.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Professional editor's analysis and production advice.
- Sources: Lieu, Derek (2018, September 16). “The Trailer Line of Rising Action.” Derek Lieu Creative. https://www.derek-lieu.com/blog/2018/9/16/the-trailer-line-of-rising-action ; Elwyn, Jonny (2019). “How to Edit A Movie Trailer.” Film-editor resource linking Lieu's three-act breakdown. https://jonnyelwyn.co.uk/film-and-video-editing/how-to-edit-a-movie-trailer/
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Outline hook → premise → complications/twist → climax → final button. Rank shots by interest, then interleave clearer, calmer explanation with short relevant audiovisual accents. Lieu discusses 90–150-second trailers; use that as his example range. Let sound or silence mark changes in energy, and hold long enough to understand the shot.
- Caveats: No source gives a scientifically optimal cut interval. Lieu warns that unrelated accents are confusing and constant high energy becomes monotonous. A trailer promises a later experience; an explainer must also deliver its answer within the piece.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High when editing invents plot or gameplay that the advertised work does not contain.

### D18 Microinteractions: define the feedback contract
- Claim: Saffer organizes a microinteraction into trigger, rules, feedback and loops/modes. A satisfying detail begins with a clear, dependable response to an action.
- Mechanism: Perceived control, processing fluency, recognition of state and action–outcome feedback.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Practitioner design framework and workshop; no controlled evidence for the complete model.
- Sources: Saffer, Dan (2013). Microinteractions: Designing with Details. O'Reilly Media. Opened book page: https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/microinteractions/9781449342760/ch02.html ; Saffer, Dan (2013). “How to design microinteractions.” UX Australia workshop. https://uxaustralia.com.au/conferences/ux-australia-2013/presentation/microinteractions/
- Applies to: UI | audio
- How to apply: For one action, write what starts it, what the system permits, how success or failure appears, and what changes after repetition. Example: upload → validate → visible progress → confirmed completion or actionable error. Add celebratory motion only after the status is unambiguous; include mute and reduced-motion behavior.
- Caveats: A delightful animation cannot compensate for latency, failure or confusing rules. Loops and modes describe changing behavior, not necessarily variable rewards. The sources do not prescribe a universal animation duration or feedback intensity.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High if reassuring feedback falsely confirms success, conceals processing or nudges accidental repetition.

### D19 Asana: occasional, optional celebrations
- Claim: Asana documents optional creatures that sometimes appear after task completion, rather than after every task. They are cosmetic celebrations of completed work.
- Mechanism: Surprise/novelty, progress acknowledgement and friendly social symbolism; psychological interpretation, not measured circuitry.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Official product example and promotional account; no published controlled retention or productivity experiment in the opened source.
- Sources: Asana (2016, March 21). “Celebrations revamped: Meet the unicorn's new team.” The Asana Blog. https://asana.com/inside-asana/new-celebrations
- Applies to: UI
- How to apply: Keep ordinary completion feedback dependable. Place any surprise celebration after meaningful completion, make it optional and let the person continue working immediately. Vary a harmless visual motif rather than the availability of essential benefits. Test whether it distracts during repeated work.
- Caveats: The source gives no appearance probability, timing optimum or causal productivity improvement. Its user-love claims are promotional. Workplace audiences may find creatures juvenile or distracting; do not interpret cosmetic variability as evidence for paid random rewards.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Becomes high if people are encouraged to create or complete trivial tasks just to chase the occasional animation.

### D20 Mailchimp: land the high-five after the real milestone
- Claim: Mailchimp describes Freddie's nervous pre-send hand and celebratory high-five after an email is sent. Its Facebook-ad animation was different because submission leads to a waiting period rather than immediate delivery.
- Mechanism: Anticipation, relief, task closure and anthropomorphic acknowledgement; no controlled dopamine or retention evidence.
- Evidence tier: T4 — First-party design process account, with anecdotes rather than an experiment.
- Sources: Chase [byline as displayed] (2017, April 19). “Designing the ‘Freddie High Five’ Animation for Facebook Ads.” Mailchimp Marketing Library. https://mailchimp.com/resources/designing-the-freddie-high-five-animation-for-facebook-ads/
- Applies to: UI | video
- How to apply: Locate the milestone the user cares about and distinguish submission, processing and completion. Give accurate status first, then a short optional acknowledgement. For an asynchronous operation, celebrate successful submission while keeping the pending state explicit. Match the metaphor to the operation, as Mailchimp did with its launch motif.
- Caveats: The source does not disclose causal engagement gains or an optimal duration. Pre-send nervousness can amplify anxiety. A high-five must not imply that an email was delivered to every recipient or that an ad was approved when only submission succeeded.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High if a celebration rewards an irreversible action before the person understands its consequences.

### D21 Rosling: reveal change against a stable visual baseline
- Claim: Rosling's Gapminder example uses animated historical data to show how countries' health and income changed over two centuries. The payoff is a changed understanding supported by the displayed data.
- Mechanism: Expectation violation, information gain and narrative progression; dopamine was not measured.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Demonstrated professional data storytelling; no controlled evaluation of this specific presentation.
- Sources: Rosling, Hans, and Gapminder Foundation (2009). “200 years that changed the world.” Gapminder video and documentation page. https://www.gapminder.org/videos/200-years-that-changed-the-world/ ; BBC and Rosling, Hans (2010). “Hans Rosling's 200 Countries, 200 Years, 4 Minutes — The Joy of Stats.” Official BBC video listing. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jbkSRLYSojo
- Applies to: infographic | slides | video | live talk
- How to apply: Establish what the axes and marks mean, show a historical baseline, and advance time with the mapping stable. Pause at a meaningful change and explain it before proceeding. End with the evidence that revises the opening assumption. Provide a static comparison and data notes for readers who need to inspect values.
- Caveats: Animation, charisma and data quality are inseparable in this example. Movement can obscure exact comparisons. The Gapminder example starts in 1809; the BBC listing describes a separate 1810-based example. Do not confuse either with evidence that all countries or groups improved equally.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Selective endpoints, hidden uncertainty and aggregate progress can conceal inequality or reversals.

### D22 NYT and The Pudding: prediction before a reader-paced reveal
- Claim: The New York Times' “You Draw It” asked readers to sketch a relationship before seeing the real data. The Pudding documents staged chart changes driven by scrolling, while explicitly questioning whether scrollytelling improves comprehension.
- Mechanism: Self-generated expectations, curiosity, comparison with evidence and reader control; no reward-circuit measurement.
- Evidence tier: T4 — First-party descriptions of published interactive journalism; no controlled outcome study in these sources.
- Sources: Aisch, Gregor, with Cox, Amanda, and Quealy, Kevin (2015, May 28). “You Draw It: How Family Income Affects Children's College Chances.” The New York Times; opened author's production account: https://driven-by-data.net/2015/05/28/college-chart-game/ ; Samora, Russell (2017, April). “Responsive scrollytelling best practices.” The Pudding. https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/
- Applies to: infographic | UI | slides | live talk
- How to apply: Invite a prediction, allow skipping it, then overlay the actual data on the same scale and explain the difference. For scrollytelling, give each step one meaningful change and a visible explanation. Use a few purposeful steps and provide stacked/static charts when motion adds little. Support reverse and rapid scrolling.
- Caveats: This combines two craft approaches rather than claiming NYT's drawing interaction is itself scrollytelling. Prediction can embarrass readers; offer a private, optional response. NYT pages were blocked, so the creator's account is cited. Animation quality, mobile performance and comprehension require separate checks.
- Dark pattern risk: low — High if essential information is gated behind participation or scrolling is hijacked to prevent leaving.

### D23 Myths: the eight-second goldfish and ten-minute attention clocks
- Claim: Popular advice says humans have an eight-second attention span and lectures lose attention after ten minutes. Neither should determine a media designer's timing as a universal biological rule.
- Mechanism: Task-dependent attention, relevance and changing cognitive demand; a platform drop-off metric is not a general attention span.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Scholarly critical literature review plus a journalistic source audit; no meta-analysis or new controlled attention experiment.
- Sources: Bradbury, Neil A. (2016). “Attention span during lectures: 8 seconds, 10 minutes, or more?” Advances in Physiology Education, 40(4), 509–513. DOI: 10.1152/advan.00109.2016. Opened paper: https://niplav.site/doc/psychology/attention_span/attention_span_during_lectures_8_seconds_10_minutes_or_more_bradbury_2016.pdf ; Maybin, Simon (2017, March 10). “Busting the attention span myth.” BBC World Service, More or Less / BBC News. Opened archived copy: https://static1.squarespace.com/static/60eb7f14f46b2d2b9ab95b82/t/642f424ea02fd800d85fe8eb/1680818791567/Busting%2Bthe%2Battention%2Bspan%2Bmyth%2B-%2BBBC%2BNews.pdf ; Microsoft Canada (2015). Attention Spans, consumer insights report, PDF p. 6. https://loupiosity.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/microsoft-attention-spans-research-report.pdf
- Applies to: slides | video | live talk | infographic
- How to apply: Pace around the audience's task: introduce a question, supply useful evidence, check understanding and vary the activity at meaningful boundaries. Use actual retention or comprehension data. Treat a ten-minute change of activity as a rehearsal hypothesis rather than a mandatory neurological reset.
- Caveats: Bradbury finds insufficient primary evidence for a fixed 10–15-minute limit. Maybin traced the eight-second numbers to Statistic Brain rather than Microsoft's experiment and could not substantiate the cited research; the report itself labels that source. These are sourcing failures, not failed replications. They do not imply attention never lapses.
- Dark pattern risk: low — The myths can rationalize excessive cutting, sensational hooks and needless urgency.

### D24 Dark patterns: endless continuation and invented urgency
- Claim: Infinite scroll and autoplay can make continuation the default, while false scarcity and fake countdowns pressure a decision without a genuine deadline. These are patterns to audit, not payoff techniques to copy uncritically.
- Mechanism: Reduced stopping cues, low-friction habit continuation, anticipated loss and urgency; the cited artifacts do not measure dopamine.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Regulatory documentation and a public research prototype establish the patterns' design, without a causal effect estimate used here.
- Sources: Federal Trade Commission (2022, September). Bringing Dark Patterns to Light. Staff report, appendix pp. 21 and 28. https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P214800%20Dark%20Patterns%20Report%209.14.2022%20-%20FINAL.pdf?date=2024-09-10 ; Alin, Juho (2026 repository documentation; prototype developed 2024). “TikTok Research Prototype.” GitHub; documents disabled-autoplay and increased-scroll-friction conditions and an infinite feed. https://github.com/juhoalin/tiktok-acdp-research-prototype
- Applies to: UI | video
- How to apply: Supply visible stopping points, easy pause/exit and explicit control of autoplay. For a finite explainer, show progress and an actual end. Remove invented stock counts and resettable timers; display only truthful availability and deadlines. Measure regret and task completion alongside continued viewing.
- Caveats: Infinite scroll and autoplay are not inherently deceptive; utility and user control matter. A real deadline differs from artificial scarcity. The associated CHI 2026 paper could not be fetched, so no claims about its experiment's results are made. FTC examples are not universal effect sizes or a jurisdiction-wide legal opinion.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Endless consumption and fabricated urgency can override a person's intent to stop or deliberate.

### D25 Loot boxes: paid uncertainty is a harm boundary
- Claim: Paid random rewards are associated with problem-gambling symptoms. A 2021 meta-analysis reports r = .26 across 15 studies; this is a harm association, not evidence that loot boxes produce pleasure or a measured dopamine hit.
- Mechanism: Anticipation under uncertainty and gambling-like reinforcement; dopamine was not measured by this synthesis.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Meta-analysis of associations, not a causal or neural experiment.
- Sources: Garea, Shaun Stephen; Drummond, Aaron; Sauer, James D.; Hall, Lauren C.; Williams, Matthew Neil (2021). “Meta-analysis of the relationship between problem gambling, excessive gaming and loot box spending.” International Gambling Studies, 21(3), 460–479. DOI: 10.1080/14459795.2021.1914705. Opened abstract and citation record: https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/intgms/v21y2021i3p460-479.html ; opened authors' laboratory publication list: https://grailgamingresearch.wordpress.com/published-research/
- Applies to: UI
- How to apply: Give core benefits deterministically. If offering playful surprises, make them free and optional, with no pressure to repeat a purchase. Audit paid randomness, escalating purchase invitations and obscured real-money costs as high-risk design choices.
- Caveats: Correlation does not establish causal direction. The abstract also reports r = .25 for excessive gaming across seven studies and r = .37 for gambling after a Trim-and-Fill adjustment. The full paper's bias analysis was not reviewed here; these estimates do not describe one product or a cosmetic celebration.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Paid repeated chances can exploit uncertainty and expose vulnerable users to financial harm.
