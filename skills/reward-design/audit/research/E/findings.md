# Books for reward-design — Unit E

These 29 books are ranked for a consultant making slides, explainer videos, UI and infographics; rank reflects relevance, reliability and practical value, not sales or author fame. Tiers follow this unit's book rubric: T1 researcher-led academic work, T2 rigorous science synthesis, T3 evidence-citing practitioner work, T4 craft or critical theory without controlled validation. Researcher status refers to the work at publication; a tier does not certify every claim. Publisher descriptions, contents, bibliographic records and selected primary papers were checked, not every page of every book. The suggested exercises below are editorial applications unless explicitly identified as a book's framework. Dopamine participates in reward prediction error and incentive salience or "wanting"; it is not a synonym for pleasure. No audience dopamine level can be inferred from liking, clicks, chills or attention, and fMRI activation alone does not measure dopamine release.

### E1 Sweet Anticipation
- Claim: Explains how learned expectations, uncertainty, surprise and resolution shape musical emotion. It offers the closest scientific foundation here for planning anticipation and payoff.
- Mechanism: Music & sound; expectation, statistical learning, tension, prediction, reaction and appraisal.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Experimental music-cognition researcher, academic MIT Press monograph grounded in research; the complete theory remains a proposal.
- Sources: David Huron (2006), Sweet Anticipation: Music and the Psychology of Expectation, MIT Press, ISBN 9780262083454; paperback (2008), ISBN 9780262582780. [Publisher description and author record](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262083454/sweet-anticipation/).
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk | slides
- How to apply: Use Huron's five-response ITPRA framework to annotate a soundtrack: what listeners imagine, when tension builds, what they predict, their immediate reaction, and later appraisal. As an exercise, compare an expected resolution with a delayed one; transfer to slide reveals only as a hypothesis.  
  Buy priority: first — Best match to the anticipation–surprise–payoff brief.
- Caveats: Musical notation helps. Expectations depend on listening history and culture; music findings do not establish equivalent effects in UI or presentations. Neither surprise nor an accurate prediction guarantees enjoyment.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Suspense can attract attention; avoid distressing startles or indefinitely withholding a promised answer.

### E2 The Pleasure Center
- Claim: Introduces the neuroscience of pleasure, desire and emotion through everyday experience. It is an accessible route into research closely connected to Berridge's distinction between wanting and liking.
- Mechanism: Neuroscience of reward; pleasure, incentive motivation and their interaction with cognition.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Reward neuroscientist writing for Oxford University Press from an academic research base.
- Sources: Morten L. Kringelbach (2008; also catalogued 2009), The Pleasure Center: Trust Your Animal Instincts, Oxford University Press, print ISBN 9780195322859. [Oxford record](https://academic.oup.com/book/52795); [opened Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Pleasure_Center.html?hl=fr&id=4vrnEQDiNXcC). Scientific update: Kent C. Berridge and Morten L. Kringelbach (2015), "Pleasure systems in the brain," Neuron 86(3), 646–664, [DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2015.02.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.02.018).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Build separate critique questions for wanting to continue, enjoying the payoff, and learning what predicts it. Evaluate these through audience reports and behaviour; do not rename clicks or satisfaction scores "dopamine."  
  Buy priority: first — Establishes the scientific distinctions needed before using reward language.
- Caveats: Predates later reward research; read with the cited update. The title's singular "center" should not be interpreted as one pleasure button. Animal mechanisms and brain imaging do not provide a media-design recipe.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Explanatory science; it could be misused to justify manipulating motivation.

### E3 Multimedia Learning
- Claim: Explains how people learn from words and graphics, and how presentation choices help or overload them. Its useful payoff is understanding rather than stimulation.
- Mechanism: Storytelling & presentation; cognitive load, limited processing capacity, selection and integration of information.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Active learning researcher, Cambridge University Press textbook synthesizing more than 200 experimental studies.
- Sources: Richard E. Mayer (2021 copyright, third edition; publisher product record dates release to 2020), Multimedia Learning, Cambridge University Press, paperback ISBN 9781316638088. [Publisher description](https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/books/multimedia-learning/FB7E79A165D24D47CEACEB4D2C426ECD?chapterId=9781316941355%23C2); [publisher frontmatter](https://assets.cambridge.org/97813166/38088/frontmatter/9781316638088_frontmatter.pdf); [DOI 10.1017/9781316941355](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316941355).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Audit an explainer for coherence, signalling, proximity of labels to graphics, and synchrony of spoken explanation with the relevant image. Segment complex instruction and check whether viewers can explain or transfer the idea, as well as whether they enjoyed it.  
  Buy priority: first — Most directly useful scientific guide for the owner's explanatory work.
- Caveats: Principles concern learning outcomes, not dopamine or advertising conversion. Apply them with attention to expertise, pacing and task. Accessibility captions should not be removed by mechanically applying the redundancy principle.
- Dark pattern risk: none — Intended to improve comprehension.

### E4 Visual Thinking for Design
- Claim: Turns research on perception, attention and visual cognition into advice for information displays. It helps make a visual discovery easy to see and reason about.
- Mechanism: Visual attention & aesthetics; active vision, visual search and external support for thinking.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Perception and visualization researcher, research-based academic/technical Morgan Kaufmann book.
- Sources: Colin Ware (2008), Visual Thinking for Design, Morgan Kaufmann, ISBN 9780123708960. [Opened bibliographic record and description](https://books.google.com/books/about/Visual_Thinking_for_Design.html?id=zdpkngEACAAJ). Updated title: Ware (2021), Visual Thinking for Information Design, second edition, Morgan Kaufmann/Elsevier, ISBN 9780128235676. [Publisher record](https://www.educate.elsevier.com/book/details/9780128235676).
- Applies to: slides | infographic | UI | video
- How to apply: Start with the viewer's visual question, such as "which category changed most?" Make its answer perceptually distinguishable, group related marks, and remove competing emphasis. Compare viewers' search errors and explanation quality across versions.  
  Buy priority: first — Gives the owner a defensible foundation for visual attention; favour the updated edition when obtainable.
- Caveats: The original is dated; the 2021 update changes the title. Salience depends on context and task, and the most conspicuous element is not necessarily the most useful. This is not a reward-neuroscience manual.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Visual hierarchy can also hide qualifications; keep consequential information legible.

### E5 Audio-Vision
- Claim: Explains how sound changes the meaning and perceived timing of moving images. Its vocabulary helps critique an audiovisual payoff as one experience.
- Mechanism: Music & sound; audiovisual integration, synchronization, temporal interpretation and sound-image meaning.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Scholarly film theory and close analysis, not controlled reward science; academic publication alone does not confer T1.
- Sources: Michel Chion (2019), Audio-Vision: Sound on Screen, second edition, edited and translated by Claudia Gorbman, foreword by Walter Murch, Columbia University Press, paperback ISBN 9780231185899. [Publisher description and contents](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/audio-vision-sound-on-screen/9780231185882/).
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Review a reveal with the image silent, with the soundtrack alone, and with both together. Describe what the sound adds to perceived action, space and time; then compare synchronized and deliberately offset versions.  
  Buy priority: first — Strongest craft/theory complement to Huron for explainer-video sound.
- Caveats: Originally published in English in 1994, updated in 2019. Film analysis does not establish effect sizes or universal timing rules; terminology can be demanding.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Sound can intensify or falsely imply an event; preserve factual meaning in explanatory media.

### E6 Emotional Design
- Claim: Explains why first impressions, ease of use and personal meaning all influence our relationship with products. It broadens critique beyond surface attractiveness.
- Mechanism: UX & emotional design; visceral response, behavioural experience and reflective meaning.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Cognitive scientist's practical design framework informed by psychological research, published as a trade book.
- Sources: Donald A. Norman (2004), Emotional Design: Why We Love (or Hate) Everyday Things, Basic Books; paperback (2005), ISBN 9780465051366. [Publisher](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/donald-a-norman/emotional-design/9780465051366/?lens=basic-books); [2004 bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Emotional_Design.html?id=z2jvRlqhdlwC).
- Applies to: UI | slides | infographic | video
- How to apply: Critique an interface at three levels: immediate impression, success in completing the task, and the meaning users attach to the result. Pair an attractive completion state with reliable operation and an understandable explanation of what happened.  
  Buy priority: later — Essential bridge from science to humane product critique.
- Caveats: The three levels are a design model, not separate brain modules. "Attractive things work better" should not excuse poor usability; much product imagery predates modern mobile interfaces.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Emotional appeal can disguise friction or poor value if function and meaning are neglected.

### E7 Resonate
- Claim: Teaches presentation structure through audience needs, contrast and a meaningful call to action. It offers practical ways to build towards a memorable moment.
- Mechanism: Storytelling & presentation; anticipation, narrative contrast, audience identification and resolution.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Practitioner method supported mainly by analyses of successful presentations, not controlled validation.
- Sources: Nancy Duarte (2010), Resonate: Present Visual Stories that Transform Audiences, John Wiley & Sons, ISBN 9780470632017. [Publisher description and full contents](https://www.wiley-vch.de/de/fachgebiete/finanzen-wirtschaft-recht/resonate-978-0-470-63201-7).
- Applies to: slides | live talk | video
- How to apply: Map the current situation and a credible alternative, alternate contrasts, anticipate resistance, and finish with an achievable action. Use the book's sparkline and S.T.A.R. moment as planning tools, then rehearse with honest critics.  
  Buy priority: later — First presentation-craft purchase after the scientific foundation.
- Caveats: Famous speeches are selected successes; matching their shape does not establish causation. Adapt the arc to audience, evidence and purpose rather than forcing every talk into one template.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Persuasive framing can suppress alternatives; the book explicitly discusses avoiding harmful persuasion.

### E8 Made to Stick
- Claim: Teaches how to make a central idea clear, concrete, surprising and memorable. It is particularly useful for opening a knowledge gap and then resolving it with substance.
- Mechanism: Curiosity; storytelling & presentation; information gaps, concreteness, memory associations and emotion.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Applied communication framework drawing on behavioural research and illustrative cases; the package is not a validated universal law.
- Sources: Chip Heath and Dan Heath (2007), Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die, Random House, ISBN 9781400064281. [Publisher](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/77687/made-to-stick-by-chip-heath-and-dan-heath/); [publisher teaching guide](https://www.randomhouse.com/catalog/teachers_guides/1-4000-6428-7.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | live talk | UI
- How to apply: Apply the book's six traits—simplicity, unexpectedness, concreteness, credibility, emotion and stories—to one message. Show a plausible expectation, expose a specific unanswered question, and supply a concrete, evidenced answer.  
  Buy priority: later — Useful everyday message-development tool.
- Caveats: Being memorable does not make a claim true; the examples include false stories. Case selection and a catchy framework do not establish the optimal message for every culture or audience.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Easily repurposed for misinformation or clickbait; make the payoff accurate and proportionate.

### E9 Game Feel
- Claim: Describes how controls, motion, sound and visual feedback combine into a sensation of responsive interaction. It makes timing and feedback concrete enough to prototype.
- Mechanism: Game feel; sensorimotor coupling, perceived control and multisensory feedback.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Practitioner model and worked game examples; no controlled validation of the complete feel framework established in this check.
- Sources: Steve Swink (2009 edition), Game Feel: A Game Designer's Guide to Virtual Sensation, Morgan Kaufmann Publishers/Elsevier, ISBN 9780123743282. [Opened edition record and description](https://books.google.com/books/about/Game_Feel.html?id=i9GfunWcB-oC). Current rights-holder lists CRC Press and copyright 2009: [publisher record](https://www.routledge.com/Game-Feel-A-Game-Programmers-Guide-to-Virtual-Sensation/Swink/p/book/9780123743282).
- Applies to: UI | video | audio
- How to apply: Prototype the same action with different movement response, impact sound and visual feedback. Change one property at a time and ask whether users feel in control; translate game examples into functional UI feedback cautiously.  
  Buy priority: later — Best specialist craft book here for tactile-looking motion and responsiveness.
- Caveats: Game mechanics are not ordinary work tasks. Original companion tools may be obsolete; screenshake and exaggerated animation can hurt accessibility. Catalogues differ between 2008 release and 2009 edition/copyright.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Satisfying feedback can support excessive repetition; use it to clarify meaningful actions.

### E10 Microinteractions
- Claim: Teaches how small interactions communicate their purpose, rules and results. It makes a useful completion cue easier to design and critique.
- Mechanism: UX & emotional design; feedback, predictability, perceived control and task completion.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Practitioner architecture and examples rather than controlled psychological validation.
- Sources: Dan Saffer (2013), Microinteractions: Designing with Details, O'Reilly Media, ISBN 9781449342760. [Publisher contents and description](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/microinteractions/9781449342760/).
- Applies to: UI | video | audio
- How to apply: Specify the trigger, rules, feedback, and loops/modes of one action. For a save operation, show what started it, what is happening, and whether it succeeded; provide equivalent useful information when sound or animation is unavailable.  
  Buy priority: later — Most immediately actionable UI book for a clear, earned payoff.
- Caveats: Examples and platforms date to 2013. A feedback loop is a functional interaction concept here, not evidence of dopamine release. Timings need testing on actual devices and tasks.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Loops and defaults can undermine control; make outcomes and stopping points clear.

### E11 Designing for Emotion
- Claim: Shows how personality, empathy and inclusion can shape digital experiences. The second edition extends beyond delight to users under stress and to privacy and safety.
- Mechanism: UX & emotional design; affect, social interpretation, empathy and trust.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Practitioner interpretation of psychological principles with case studies; not an experimental demonstration of a complete method.
- Sources: Aarron Walter (2020), Designing for Emotion, second edition, A Book Apart, ISBN 9781952616495. [Publisher launch and scope](https://abookapart.com/blogs/press/new-designing-for-emotion-second-edition-by-aarron-walter.html); [opened edition record](https://books.google.com/books?id=BKqQ0QEACAAJ).
- Applies to: UI | slides | video
- How to apply: Define a product's personality and intended emotional tone, then review routine, failure and stressful states against it. Choose a reassuring recovery message or quiet completion cue when a celebratory flourish would be inappropriate.  
  Buy priority: later — A short practical complement to Norman; choose the second edition.
- Caveats: Brand personality is not a universal emotional response. Case studies do not isolate which design choice caused an outcome. A Book Apart has ceased selling/distributing books; verify an available author or retail edition.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Friendly character and copy can conceal pressure; empathy should extend to refusal and exit.

### E12 In the Blink of an Eye
- Claim: Explains an editor's decisions about cuts, continuity and emotional meaning. It helps decide when an image change serves the audience's experience.
- Mechanism: Storytelling & presentation; event boundaries, rhythm, emotion and attention in film editing.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Experienced editor's craft essay and personal priorities, not behavioural or neural validation.
- Sources: Walter Murch (2001), In the Blink of an Eye: A Perspective on Film Editing, second edition, Silman-James Press, ISBN 9781879505629. [Publisher description](https://www.silmanjamespress.com/shop/filmmaking-directing/in-the-blink-of-an-eye2nd-edition/); [edition year record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL23241024M/In_the_blink_of_an_eye).
- Applies to: video | slides | live talk
- How to apply: For each cut or slide transition, describe what changes emotionally, narratively and rhythmically. Compare a cut at a shift in thought with one driven only by visual continuity; keep the version that makes the intended meaning clearer.  
  Buy priority: later — Concise, valuable craft reading for timing an earned payoff.
- Caveats: Murch's blink analogy and numerical weighting of editing priorities are heuristics, not measured psychological constants. The digital-editing discussion is dated; continuity demands differ across genres.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Editing can remove context or manufacture reactions; explanatory work needs truthful continuity.

### E13 slide:ology
- Claim: Teaches how to develop visual ideas and turn them into coherent slides. It supports the clarity and hierarchy that make a reveal understandable.
- Mechanism: Storytelling & presentation; visual attention, composition and visual explanation.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Presentation-design craft from professional practice; "science" in the subtitle does not establish experimental validation.
- Sources: Nancy Duarte (2008), slide:ology: The Art and Science of Creating Great Presentations, O'Reilly Media, ISBN 9780596522346. [Publisher release](https://www.oreilly.com/pub/pr/2057); [bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books?id=aSXdz_-aFqYC).
- Applies to: slides | infographic | video | live talk
- How to apply: Sketch the concept before opening slide software, translate abstract statements into diagrams, and use hierarchy to make the main relationship visible. Rehearse the interaction between spoken explanation and the slide.  
  Buy priority: later — Useful visual craft companion to Resonate and Ware.
- Caveats: Some examples and software conventions date to 2008. Decorative simplicity cannot replace appropriate detail; charts must preserve scale, uncertainty and qualifications.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Elegant simplification can omit inconvenient evidence; retain what audiences need to judge the claim.

### E14 Presentation Zen
- Claim: Teaches planning, simplicity and delivery for visually focused presentations. It is a useful way to reduce clutter and leave space for a central idea.
- Mechanism: Storytelling & presentation; hierarchy, coherence, narrative and audience attention.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Practitioner design philosophy and examples; not a controlled theory of attention or reward.
- Sources: Garr Reynolds (2019 release, copyright 2020), Presentation Zen: Simple Ideas on Presentation Design and Delivery, third edition, New Riders, ebook ISBN 9780135783146. [Publisher edition record and contents](https://www.peachpit.com/store/presentation-zen-simple-ideas-on-presentation-design-9780135783146).
- Applies to: slides | live talk | video | infographic
- How to apply: Plan the argument away from the screen, remove competing messages, and make the slide support the spoken explanation. Create a separate detailed reading version when the audience needs evidence without a presenter.  
  Buy priority: later — Good entry-level craft book; lower priority if Duarte's books already cover the owner's needs.
- Caveats: Minimalist aesthetics are not a universal communication solution. A slide used live and a document read alone have different requirements; expressive restraint varies by audience and culture.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Minimalism can hide caveats; keep a clear route to substantive evidence.

### E15 Curious
- Claim: Distinguishes fleeting interest in novelty from sustained curiosity and understanding. It helps designers think about a question worth answering rather than an endless tease.
- Mechanism: Curiosity; information gaps, prior knowledge, exploratory attention and empathic curiosity.
- Evidence tier: T2 — Journalistic synthesis of psychological and educational research; its broader cultural argument is less secure than the research it discusses.
- Sources: Ian Leslie (2014), Curious: The Desire to Know and Why Your Future Depends On It, Basic Books, hardcover ISBN 9780465079964. [Opened book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Curious.html?id=qaAVBQAAQBAJ); [Publishers Weekly review, 2014](https://www2.publishersweekly.com/978-0-465-07996-4). Primary theoretical source: George Loewenstein (1994), "The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation," Psychological Bulletin 116(1), 75–98, [DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Establish enough context for viewers to notice a specific gap, invite a prediction, and give a useful explanation. Check whether the opening leads to learning or merely to another click.  
  Buy priority: later — Accessible curiosity reading; supplement with Loewenstein rather than treating it as the primary science.
- Caveats: Publishers Weekly criticized its superficial science survey and corporate emphasis. Claims of a cultural decline in curiosity require more evidence than anecdotes. An information gap does not guarantee pleasant anticipation.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Curiosity gaps become clickbait when the answer is withheld or disappointing.

### E16 The Aesthetic Brain
- Claim: Surveys neuroscience and evolutionary accounts of beauty, pleasure and art. It helps separate noticing an image, finding it attractive, and assigning it meaning.
- Mechanism: Visual attention & aesthetics; perceptual analysis, aesthetic valuation, wanting and liking.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Researching neurologist and neuroaesthetics scholar, Oxford University Press, with a peer-reviewed research basis.
- Sources: Anjan Chatterjee (2014 print edition; online records begin in 2013), The Aesthetic Brain: How We Evolved to Desire Beauty and Enjoy Art, Oxford University Press, ISBN 9780199811809. [Opened edition record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Aesthetic_Brain.html?id=nZi1AQAAQBAJ); [Oxford record](https://academic.oup.com/book/7066).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | UI
- How to apply: Use the book to frame separate critique questions about perceptual legibility, attraction and interpretation. Compare audience responses to alternative imagery and ask what role familiarity, context and cultural experience played.  
  Buy priority: later — Best academic aesthetics overview among the named books.
- Caveats: Evolutionary explanations can be difficult to test; aesthetic ratings and neural associations do not establish one universal beauty formula. The field has developed since publication, and artistic preference is context-sensitive.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Attractive presentation can lend credibility to weak claims; aesthetics must support accurate explanation.

### E17 This Is Your Brain on Music
- Claim: Introduces musical perception, memory, emotion and the brain in accessible language. It gives non-musicians vocabulary for discussing why a soundtrack feels coherent or expressive.
- Mechanism: Music & sound; rhythm, timbre, pitch, expectation and musical memory.
- Evidence tier: T2 — Researcher's well-sourced trade synthesis, rather than an academic monograph or a validation of design recipes.
- Sources: Daniel J. Levitin (2006), This Is Your Brain on Music: The Science of a Human Obsession, Dutton, hardcover ISBN 9780525949695. [Publisher record, including 2006 ebook](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/298964/this-is-your-brain-on-music-by-daniel-j-levitin/9781101218914/); [original-edition record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7771134M/This_Is_Your_Brain_on_Music).
- Applies to: video | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Make a soundtrack brief that describes rhythm, timbre, melodic expectation and relevant associations. Compare how these choices support the message; use Huron for deeper analysis of anticipation and resolution.  
  Buy priority: later — Easier introduction than Huron, with more overlap and less direct design application.
- Caveats: Predates much later reward and music research. Brain-region explanations are simplified for general readers; musical expertise, familiarity and culture change responses. It supplies no universal tempo or dopamine recipe.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Music can steer affect without clarifying evidence; do not let mood substitute for the explanation.

### E18 The Art of Game Design
- Claim: Provides questions or "lenses" for examining experience, rules, challenge, feedback and player motivation. It teaches iterative critique rather than one fixed recipe.
- Mechanism: Game feel; challenge, motivation, uncertainty, feedback and social play.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Practitioner/educator book with endnotes and bibliography alongside extensive craft examples; the lenses themselves are not a controlled intervention.
- Sources: Jesse Schell (2019 publication; publisher copyright 2020), The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses, third edition, A K Peters/CRC Press, ISBN 9781138632059. [Opened edition record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Art_of_Game_Design.html?id=ihwU0QEACAAJ); [expanded contents and references](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Art_of_Game_Design.html?id=gXumDwAAQBAJ).
- Applies to: UI | video | live talk | slides
- How to apply: Select relevant lenses for a demo or interactive experience: what the audience wants, what uncertainty matters, and how feedback reveals progress. Prototype and observe the experience before adding more rewards.  
  Buy priority: later — Valuable for interactive demos and playful experiences; less central for ordinary explanatory graphics.
- Caveats: Over 100 perspectives can become a checklist burden. Fun, repeat play and comprehension are different outcomes; free-to-play examples need special ethical scrutiny.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Reward, repetition and monetization ideas can enable compulsion if used to maximize engagement at users' expense.

### E19 Storytelling in Design
- Claim: Adapts story structure, character, context and scene planning to products used across devices. It helps coordinate an experience that users enter from different starting points.
- Mechanism: UX & emotional design; storytelling, goal structure, context and anticipation across a journey.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Practitioner translation of film and fiction methods into product design, without controlled validation of the transfer.
- Sources: Anna Dahlström (2019), Storytelling in Design: Defining, Designing, and Selling Multidevice Products, O'Reilly Media, ISBN 9781491959411. [Publisher description and detailed contents](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/storytelling-in-design/9781491959411/).
- Applies to: UI | slides | video | live talk
- How to apply: Storyboard a user's real goal, context, obstacles and outcome across touchpoints. Check that a person entering midway can understand the current state and reach the result without needing the designer's preferred narrative order.  
  Buy priority: later — Good for connected product journeys and presenting design decisions.
- Caveats: Product use is not always linear or theatrical. A "hero's journey" can obstruct a simple task; much of the value concerns planning and stakeholder communication rather than audience reward mechanisms.
- Dark pattern risk: low — A compelling journey can become a coercive funnel; support alternate paths and direct completion.

### E20 Design for Real Life
- Claim: Shows how apparently cheerful or convenient designs can fail people under stress. It teaches compassionate design through real examples and stress-case review.
- Mechanism: UX & emotional design; emotional context, empathy, inclusion and avoiding distress.
- Evidence tier: T4 — Experienced practitioners' case-based design guidance, not controlled neuroscience.
- Sources: Eric A. Meyer and Sara Wachter-Boettcher (2016), Design for Real Life, A Book Apart, current publisher-listed ISBN 9781952616372. [Publisher description, contents and publication date](https://abookapart.com/products/design-for-real-life).
- Applies to: UI | slides | video | infographic
- How to apply: Review an anticipated "delight" or memory feature with scenarios involving bereavement, failure, exclusion and unwanted disclosure. Give users control over celebratory messages, reminders and resurfaced personal material.  
  Buy priority: later — Read before applying habit loops or personalized surprise; a practical ethical counterweight.
- Caveats: Case-based rather than an estimate of how often harm occurs. The 2016 platform examples date; the stress-case method transfers more readily than individual interface details. A Book Apart no longer distributes books.
- Dark pattern risk: none — Its purpose is to expose and reduce harmful emotional design.

### E21 Pleasures of the Brain
- Claim: Collects researcher accounts of the mechanisms of pleasure and reward, including music and sensory experience. It is the most direct Berridge-authored book route in this list.
- Mechanism: Neuroscience of reward; hedonic hotspots, incentive salience, learning and neural valuation.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Researcher-edited Oxford University Press academic collection with experimental, imaging and clinical evidence.
- Sources: Morten L. Kringelbach and Kent C. Berridge, editors (2009 publisher record; commonly cited as 2010), Pleasures of the Brain, Oxford University Press, print ISBN 9780195331028. [Oxford record and contents](https://academic.oup.com/book/53854); [opened Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Pleasures_of_the_Brain.html?id=0F-yAwAAQBAJ).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: Consult relevant reward and music chapters when the skill needs a scientific claim. Record whether the evidence measures behaviour, brain activity or causal manipulation, and distinguish liking from wanting before making a design inference.  
  Buy priority: later — Specialist reference after The Pleasure Center; stronger direct scientific authorship than pop dopamine books.
- Caveats: Denser and older than an introductory trade book. Authors present differing accounts, and animal causal evidence does not translate directly into human media recipes. Consult later papers for current claims.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Mechanistic knowledge can be misused, although the book is research rather than a habit-engineering manual.

### E22 Thinking, Fast and Slow — priming caution
- Claim: Teaches judgment biases, framing and distinctions between experienced and remembered outcomes. It also popularizes the belief that subtle social primes can unconsciously produce substantial changes in complex behaviour.
- Mechanism: Storytelling & presentation; judgment, framing, memory of experience and associative priming.
- Evidence tier: T2 — Researcher's major science synthesis; individual chapters vary greatly in evidential strength.
- Sources: Daniel Kahneman (2011), Thinking, Fast and Slow, Farrar, Straus and Giroux, ISBN 9780374275631. [Publisher and excerpt](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374275631/thinkingfastandslow/?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-text-block). Stéphane Doyen, Olivier Klein, Cora-Lise Pichon and Axel Cleeremans (2012), "Behavioral Priming: It's All in the Mind, but Whose Mind?", PLOS ONE 7(1), e29081, [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0029081](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0029081). Kahneman (2012), "A proposal to deal with questions about priming effects," [open letter hosted by Nature](https://www.nature.com/news/polopoly_fs/7.6716.1349271308%21/suppinfoFile/Kahneman%20Letter.pdf).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | UI | live talk
- How to apply: Use it to critique framing and overconfidence, and to ask whether the ending changes remembered evaluation. Verify the specific supporting study before using a priming or peak-end claim; test real understanding and experienced quality alongside retrospective ratings.  
  Buy priority: later — Valuable broad thinking reference with explicit chapter-level warnings.
- Caveats: The age-word/walking-speed finding was not reproduced under automated timing in Doyen's first experiment; effects in the second depended on experimenter expectations. This does not invalidate all priming or the whole book. Systems 1 and 2 are explanatory shorthand, not two anatomical brains; peak-end effects are not universal media laws.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Biases and remembered evaluation can be exploited; improve the actual experience as well as its ending.

### E23 Irresistible
- Claim: Explains how digital products encourage repeated use and examines the costs of behavioural addiction. It supplies a critical counterweight to engagement-maximizing design advice.
- Mechanism: UX & emotional design; reinforcement, goals, feedback, escalation and difficulty stopping.
- Evidence tier: T2 — Psychology researcher's trade synthesis using research and cases; some prevalence and addiction language require caution.
- Sources: Adam Alter (2017), Irresistible: The Rise of Addictive Technology and the Business of Keeping Us Hooked, Penguin Press, opened edition ISBN 9780735223868. [Edition record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Irresistible.html?id=b34yvgAACAAJ); [publisher](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/318516/irresistible-by-adam-alter/). Boundary critique: Joël Billieux, Adriano Schimmenti, Yasser Khazaal, Pierre Maurage and Alexandre Heeren (2015), "Are we overpathologizing everyday life? A tenable blueprint for behavioral addiction research," Journal of Behavioral Addictions 4(3), 119–123, [DOI 10.1556/2006.4.2015.009](https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.4.2015.009).
- Applies to: UI | video | live talk | slides
- How to apply: Audit repeated-use designs for user-valued goals, understandable completion and an easy stopping point. Compare engagement metrics with regret or loss of control, and read this alongside Hooked before choosing a retention objective.  
  Buy priority: later — Useful ethical and behavioural counterweight.
- Caveats: Heavy use alone does not establish clinical addiction. The cited 2015 critique addresses the research field, not this later book. Digital-use harms and causal pathways need specific evidence; avoid repeating sensational prevalence claims without checking their definitions.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Critiques manipulative design, but descriptions of its mechanics can be repurposed.

### E24 How Brands Grow
- Claim: Uses marketing research to challenge common beliefs about loyalty, targeting and brand growth. It emphasizes broad reach and making a brand easy to notice, remember and buy.
- Mechanism: Advertising effectiveness; mental and physical availability, memory and buying behaviour.
- Evidence tier: T1 — Marketing researcher, Oxford University Press, drawing on a sustained peer-reviewed empirical research programme.
- Sources: Byron Sharp (2010), How Brands Grow: What Marketers Don't Know, Oxford University Press Australia & New Zealand, ISBN 9780195573565. [Opened edition record and research description](https://books.google.com/books/about/How_Brands_Grow.html?id=hXVjQgAACAAJ); [publisher page found in search](https://www.oup.com.au/books/higher-education/business-marketing/9780195573565?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-text-block).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | audio
- How to apply: For campaign work, ask whether audiences can recognize the brand and connect the message to it later. Compare this with merely making an entertaining reveal; a memorable execution should also support useful brand memory.  
  Buy priority: later — Relevant when the owner's media supports marketing, not required for every explainer.
- Caveats: This concerns aggregate marketing regularities, not dopamine or the optimal timing of a reveal. Do not assume a buying pattern proves a particular creative technique caused sales; category, distribution and market context still matter.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Commercial persuasion and repeated exposure can crowd out informed choice, although deception is not intrinsic to the method.

### E25 The Long and the Short of It
- Claim: Separates short-term activation from longer-term brand building and explains why immediate response is an incomplete measure of advertising. It helps judge what an emotional creative execution is meant to accomplish.
- Mechanism: Advertising effectiveness; emotional response, memory, campaign reach and effects over time.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Evidence-based practitioner report analysing industry campaign cases; observational case data are not randomized proof.
- Sources: Les Binet and Peter Field (2013), The Long and the Short of It: Balancing Short and Long-Term Marketing Strategies, Institute of Practitioners in Advertising, ISBN 9780852941348. [Opened edition record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Long_and_the_Short_of_It.html?id=OtL-oAEACAAJ); [IPA's official summary](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/documents/the-long-and-the-short-of-it-presentation).
- Applies to: slides | video | infographic | audio
- How to apply: Define whether a creative asset serves immediate action, longer-term memory, or both, and choose matching evaluation periods. Use the report to resist judging a brand story solely by clicks from its final call to action.  
  Buy priority: later — Useful for commercial briefs and client discussions; specialist relevance.
- Caveats: Award-database cases are selected and retrospective; association is not causation. Treat the often-quoted 60:40 brand/activation allocation as context-dependent guidance, not a universal budget rule or a ratio for every slide or video.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Advertising can manipulate, but the report mainly concerns strategy and evaluation rather than coercive interface techniques.

### E26 Hooked
- Claim: Teaches a cycle for building products people return to: trigger, action, variable reward and investment. Its objective is habit formation, which should be distinguished from a satisfying one-time explanation.
- Mechanism: UX & emotional design; cue-driven habits, uncertainty, reinforcement and increasing investment.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Practitioner framework citing psychology and product cases; the complete Hook Model is not established here as a universally validated intervention.
- Sources: Nir Eyal (2014), Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products, Portfolio, ISBN 9781591847786. [Publisher description and author background](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/317898/hooked-by-nir-eyal/).
- Applies to: UI
- How to apply: Read as an audit tool: identify the trigger, required action, promised value and accumulated investment. Use a loop only when repeated use serves a user-chosen goal; provide control over reminders, predictable core utility and a clear exit.  
  Buy priority: later — Optional for retention work; pair with Irresistible and Design for Real Life.
- Caveats: Successful product cases are not causal tests of the framework. Uncertain outcomes can frustrate as well as motivate; task completion need not become a habit. Engagement is not evidence of liking or dopamine release.
- Dark pattern risk: high — Variable rewards and investment can encourage compulsive checking or make disengagement costly.

### E27 100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People — attention myth
- Claim: Offers concise psychology-based design advice, including the popular belief that sustained attention lasts about ten minutes. That apparent precision is unsuitable as a universal media-planning rule.
- Mechanism: Visual attention & aesthetics; UX & emotional design; memory, attention and motivation.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Practitioner psychology digest citing research, with unevenly supported generalizations.
- Sources: Susan Weinschenk (2020), 100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People, second edition, New Riders/Pearson, paperback ISBN 9780136746911. [Publisher contents, including item 44](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/100-things-every-designer-needs-to-know-about-people/P200000000675/9780136746881). Neil A. Bradbury (2016), "Attention span during lectures: 8 seconds, 10 minutes, or more?", Advances in Physiology Education 40(4), 509–513, [DOI 10.1152/advan.00109.2016](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/10.1152/advan.00109.2016); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/).
- Applies to: UI | slides | video | infographic | live talk
- How to apply: Use individual tips to generate hypotheses and follow their references. Plan changes at meaningful boundaries, then observe comprehension and task performance; do not insert arbitrary stimulation every ten minutes.  
  Buy priority: skip — Convenient digest, but Ware and Mayer offer stronger foundations for this owner.
- Caveats: Bradbury's critical review found little support for a universal 10–15-minute lecture attention limit; it is not a direct test of this book or proof of unlimited attention. The second-edition contents still state the rule. Other crisp numeric tips also need contextual checking.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Motivation tips can be used to pressure people; assess outcomes beyond engagement.

### E28 Talk Like TED — fixed-duration caution
- Claim: Extracts storytelling, novelty and delivery advice from successful TED talks. It also promotes eighteen minutes as an ideal presentation length, a popular claim that should not be treated as a universal biological limit.
- Mechanism: Storytelling & presentation; novelty, emotional delivery, rehearsal and audience attention.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Public-speaking practitioner's advice citing studies and interviews; successful-talk analysis does not establish causal effectiveness.
- Sources: Carmine Gallo (2014), Talk Like TED: The 9 Public-Speaking Secrets of the World's Top Minds, St. Martin's Press, ISBN 9781250041128. [Publisher and excerpt](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250041128/talkliketed/); [Kirkus review discussing the eighteen-minute claim](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/carmine-gallo/talk-like-ted/). Scientific attention boundary: Bradbury (2016), [DOI 10.1152/advan.00109.2016](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/10.1152/advan.00109.2016).
- Applies to: live talk | slides | video
- How to apply: Rehearse a concrete opening, a relevant surprising example and a clear final idea. Choose duration from content, audience and format; use the TED limit when the venue requires it rather than attributing it to audience neurobiology.  
  Buy priority: skip — Rehearsal advice is useful, but substantial overlap with Duarte and Reynolds lowers its buying value.
- Caveats: TED is a selected format with edited, promoted success cases. Bradbury questions fixed lecture attention limits; it does not specifically test eighteen-minute talks. Interviews and cited neuroscience do not establish a universal optimum.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Charisma and novelty can amplify weak claims; anchor the performance in evidence.

### E29 The Molecule of More
- Claim: Presents dopamine as a driver of seeking future rewards and uses that story to explain romance, creativity, addiction and politics. Its broad single-chemical narrative is an appealing but weak foundation for media-design science.
- Mechanism: Neuroscience of reward; popular accounts of wanting, anticipation and reward seeking.
- Evidence tier: T3 — Evidence-citing account by a psychiatrist and writer; practitioner interpretation and broad extrapolation warrant a lower rating than rigorous academic reward science.
- Sources: Daniel Z. Lieberman and Michael E. Long (2018 original; cited paperback 2019), The Molecule of More: How a Single Chemical in Your Brain Drives Love, Sex, and Creativity—and Will Determine the Fate of the Human Race, BenBella Books, paperback ISBN 9781948836586. [Official distributor/publisher record](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Molecule-of-More/Daniel-Z-Lieberman/9781948836586); [2018 publisher catalogue](https://www.benbellabooks.com/wp-content/uploads/Catalog_BenBella2018_Marketing-2.pdf). Scientific checks: Wolfram Schultz (2016), "Dopamine reward prediction error coding," Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience 18(1), 23–32, [DOI 10.31887/DCNS.2016.18.1/wschultz](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4826767/); Berridge and Kringelbach (2015), "Pleasure systems in the brain," Neuron 86(3), 646–664, [DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2015.02.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.02.018).
- Applies to: slides | video | UI | infographic | audio | live talk
- How to apply: If reading it, separate the core reward-learning account from speculative explanations of whole domains of human behaviour. Build the skill's neuroscience vocabulary from Kringelbach, Berridge and Schultz; evaluate media with behavioural and subjective measures.  
  Buy priority: skip — The Pleasure Center is a better first science purchase; this offers little actionable media craft.
- Caveats: The publisher's claims that dopamine explains every urge and predicts broad human behaviour exceed what the cited reward literature establishes. This is an editorial assessment of scope, not evidence that every passage is false. Dopamine is neither pleasure itself nor a quantity designers can infer from an audience response.
- Dark pattern risk: low — Neurochemical simplification can give manipulative engagement tactics an undeserved scientific justification.
