## Why research users

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L07 · 00:00–01:00

In this introductory lesson, I’ll explain why it matters to research our users. We’ll discuss the scientific method and analyze a client brief. Without research, we are not doing user experience; you can read this on the Nielsen Norman Group blog.

Research helps us understand who our user is and what needs, goals, and motivations they have. It helps us learn how they navigate our system, where they encounter problems, and how they feel when interacting with the product.

At work, people often ask why we should spend time on research. The typical manager tells us to start designing the screens as soon as possible. We need to research so that we understand our users much better and make sure we are solving a problem that really exists in people’s lives. Research also gives us an overall view of the project or problem.

## When to research and how to gather data

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L07 · 01:00–01:39

When should we do research? A typical manager or client may tell us to do it at the beginning and then start designing immediately. Research is usually done at the start of a project, when we need to empathize with users. We can also use it while defining the features of what we are going to make, while ideating, while prototyping, and when we test.

Research is a two-step process: we collect a great deal of data, then synthesize it to arrive at a solution. Remember Design Thinking’s double diamond. We need to gather a lot of data so that we can define the problem well.

## The scientific method and validating hypotheses

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L07 · 01:39–02:20

The scientific method has four stages. First is observation. When we encounter an interesting fact or phenomenon, we begin by observing it. The second stage is formulating hypotheses: we propose how and why what happened occurred. The third stage is experimentation. We test the hypotheses through experiments. In the fourth stage, data analysis lets us check whether the hypothesis is correct.

It is important to validate hypotheses instead of unthinkingly doing what a manager or client tells us. A phrase from the Facebook team really opened my eyes: designing without research is like getting into a taxi and telling the driver to “drive.”

## What is missing from a typical client brief

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L07 · 02:20–03:26

Here we can see the typical client brief we might receive at the beginning of a project. It asks for general things without making a specific request. The product’s features are not clearly stated, so its scope could be unknown. The brief also does not explain the project’s objectives or what the company wants to achieve with what we make.

A common mistake is for the client to describe the site architecture: which pages it needs and how they should be structured. The client may also tell us through wireframes (the captions read “Wayfrenz”) what to design without knowing what the user actually needs. Basically, the client wants us to make something look nice based on what they think they need.

As you can see, the brief is incomplete. It says nothing about competitors or references that might interest the client. It does not define the target audience or say what content the website will show, and other sections still need to be completed. Worst of all, it asks for three design proposals for the homepage and a secondary page. The client also says the budget is small without specifying how much they can invest. That will make the project very difficult to carry out.

## Information needed for a complete brief

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L07 · 03:26–04:33

What information do we need for a proper brief? First, identify the roles, functions, and responsibilities of the people involved in the project and business: who the clients are, which departments will take part, and so on. We also need to know what problems the project is meant to solve.

We need all the information available about the clients and users, and we can ask why they need this project. It is also important to know what resources and tools we will have and what limitations the project may face.

Define the objectives and goals clearly. Find out the project budget, delivery times and milestones, and any notes or ideas the company may have. I’ll leave a proposal template in the resources section so you can make the brief as complete as possible.

### Key points (derived)

- Research gives teams evidence about users’ needs and the problem to solve.
- Use the scientific method to examine and validate hypotheses before committing to a design.
- A useful brief records stakeholders, users, problems, objectives, constraints, budget, milestones, and ideas.
