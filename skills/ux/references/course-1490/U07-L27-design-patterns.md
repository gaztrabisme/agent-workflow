## What design patterns do

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L27 · 00:00–01:53

Thank you. Design patterns (*patrones de diseño*) are like words in a vocabulary. In this lesson, I’ll explain how they work and what they are for.

A design pattern is an element or component that we repeatedly see in digital products. Patterns give us recurring solutions to common design problems. They are predictive: they make products more intuitive for every kind of user. They have already proved their effectiveness by solving similar problems in the past, and we can reuse them for different design problems in different circumstances.

In our effort to be creative and very innovative, we often look for new solutions to problems that already have solutions. That forces our users to relearn something they learned in earlier products. This is why it is so important to use design patterns that have been used and are already familiar. Please, let’s not reinvent the wheel. If something already works, we do not have to make it different.

For example, look at the number layout on the phone at left. It follows the familiar order from left to right and top to bottom: one, two, three, four, five, six, and so on. If we ignore that pattern and use a different order—in this case, going from top to bottom—it makes the phone harder to use.

ATMs give us a clear example. The numbers can be arranged in different ways when we withdraw cash, and we often enter our PIN intuitively without thinking about the digits. When the arrangement changes, we generally make mistakes. It happens to me quite a lot, at least.

## Familiarity and the cost of relearning

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L27 · 01:53–02:39

Jakob Nielsen said that users spend most of their time on other websites. This means they prefer our new page to work just like the sites they already know. In the end, that applies the law of least effort (*ley del mínimo esfuerzo*) for the user.

Some time ago, many creatives and designers made websites in Flash and created a different design pattern for each one. Here you can see some fairly old examples of award-winning sites. Each one worked differently, and on average it took us 10 to 20 minutes to learn how to use one well enough to find information. That is a serious mistake. This practice has gradually fallen away, although some designers still insist on inventing new ways to use familiar things.

## Where to find design patterns

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L27 · 02:39–04:33

Where can we look for patterns? There are several websites for learning about and finding design patterns, but I recommend these three. This is the UI-Patterns page, where we can see screenshots and many collections that can inspire us. One warning: although there is a lot of content, the images tend to be very old.

What I want you to look at on this page is its main section of design patterns: patterns for getting information, forms, explaining processes with a wizard or stepper, tabs, hierarchies, and menus. I insist that the examples are old, but they explain well what problem each pattern solves and when to use it. This example is from Flash and is very old, but it is useful as a first introduction at the theoretical level. I find this site very interesting for learning the theory of design patterns.

By contrast, Nice Very Nice is a fairly recent website. It has examples of patterns—though I would call them sections more than patterns—and shows how different modules on websites are solved. They are very well designed and provide good references. I like this website a lot. We can examine the examples in detail and visit the page where each one appears.

If we are looking for navigation references, we go to Navigation and find examples of different kinds of navigation. Let’s open this one. The site shows a screenshot and links to the original page. We can visit Uber and see how its menu behaves when it is implemented, how it works in practice. It is very nice and elegant. Nice Very Nice works well as inspiration when we need to solve modules on websites.

## Using Mobbin to study mobile patterns

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L27 · 04:33–06:29

Finally, my whole studio’s favorite is Mobbin Design. It shows screenshots from some of the best iOS apps on the market right now. A team takes the screenshots and organizes them by app. We can also go to Patterns and filter them. For example, we can look at login pages. There are lots of examples, and the collection keeps loading.

For designing apps, this is the best reference you will find. It is updated each week with new digital products, and it is a pleasure to see the ideas we can find there. Its filters include interface elements, such as navigation bars at the bottom of each screen. We can see how these are usually made and avoid putting six items in a navigation bar, a very common mistake when we are starting out.

I always try to include as few items as possible in navigation bars. The minimum gives an impression of simplicity and ease. The more items we put there, the more access points we offer, and the more complex the app looks.

We can also browse patterns directly and make collections. For example, we can start a collection for a data app and save screens that might inspire us. If we like a screen, we can go directly to the app and browse all its screens. That takes a lot of work, but we all appreciate it. When we make apps, we use Mobbin like a dictionary: we look up how leading product-design teams around the world solve a problem.

## Android and iOS conventions

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L27 · 06:29–08:43

Here you can see the differences between design patterns commonly used in Android, on the left, and iOS, on the right. Both platforms have the same goal, but there are substantial differences. In this example, everything is aligned on the left in one version and centered in the other. In iOS, the Back control always has text beside it; Android does not. The title here is centered. The patterns are very similar, but not the same. When we design for Android or iOS, we have to account for the patterns used on each device.

Here is another example: the typical tabs. These are the Android ones, and this is how iOS tabs are designed. We should follow these patterns because many apps already use them. Our digital product will feel more intuitive if we use interface elements that users already recognize.

Here is another example, with a button’s behavior when it is pressed and when it is disabled. In both cases the buttons are gray, but you can clearly tell the states apart. The primary action button floats at the lower right on Android, whereas on iOS it sits in the center of the navigation bar. Modal dialog windows are left-aligned in Android and centered in iOS. The Android button is always on the right, while the iOS button is positioned at the bottom.

During ideation or sketching, we do not need to focus on these details. Once we make high-fidelity wireframes (*wireframes de alta fidelidad*), however, it is important to check them while designing. You can find many examples and a usage guide in Material Design for Android. For iOS, Apple Design Resources has the Human Interface Guidelines, the style guide for iOS apps.
