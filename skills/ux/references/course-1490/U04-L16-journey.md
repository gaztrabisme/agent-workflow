## What a user journey shows

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L16 · 00:00–01:17

A proto journey (*protojourney*) is a reduced version of a typical user journey. In this lesson, we will use a template and generate many possible features for our project.

A user journey (*journey* or *user journey*) maps the process a user goes through to complete a task in our system in response to a need. It includes the research and alternative considerations along the way. Put more simply, it is a visual interpretation of a process from the user's perspective, showing their relationship with an organization, service, product, or brand over time. We represent the process between our user and our product, and how the user feels as it unfolds.

The process has a number of actions, with a beginning, a plot in the middle, and an ending, like a film or series. First the user needs to do something with our application and tries to accomplish it. It may or may not be difficult; in the end, the user either succeeds or does not.

## Reading opportunities in Sarah's day

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L16 · 01:38–03:37

This example is starting to appeal to me more. At the top there is a user persona, but there is still far too much text and I am not going to read it. As I said, use as little text as possible. Below it is a map of opportunities for Sarah to exercise throughout the day. The graph shows more or fewer opportunities, and her alarm rings at six in the morning.

At six she could exercise. When she starts eating breakfast, she cannot. Then she drives to school to teach, so exercising is impossible there. At lunchtime, she could have an opportunity: many people work out while everyone else is eating. Then she goes back to work, where she cannot exercise. But when she gets home at four thirty, you see the graph rise. She puts down her bag and faces the choice we all know: “Do I go to the gym or sit on the sofa?” That is a major opportunity for her to exercise. When she starts grading her students' exams or assignments, the opportunity falls. Once you turn on Netflix, you are lost; it is already impossible to go to the gym. Then she talks with her mother, takes a bath, and goes to bed.

Imagine we are making this journey for a fitness app to decide when to send this group of people a promotion to subscribe to the premium version of our home-training app. The best moment might be at four or four thirty, so they see the offer when they get home. If I were teaching, or if I were in a class as I am now, and a fitness app promotion arrived, that would not be the right time. We need to know the right moment, one that works best for the user and for us as a company. This example could be used for other purposes as long as it shows the opportunities people have. Even so, it still does not work for us: it has too much content and takes a long time to review.

I recommend five to eight steps. Do not put fifteen steps here, or three; between five and eight is good. ## Turn problems into questions and ideas

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L16 · 05:46–07:24

Now I will move down to the bottom of the exercise. Once we have defined a point to improve, we will try to find a solution using “How Might We?” (*¿Cómo podríamos?*), a tool explained in Google's Design Sprint, a method they use; there is also a book I recommend. We choose the problem and put “How Might We?” in front of it.

In our case, the content is out of date and duplicated between the website and the course landing page. We can ask: How might we synchronize the content between these two kinds of pages, the website and the landing page? And how might we show valuable content to someone interested in the course? Usually there can be one question, as you see here, but there can also be two. Reframing the problem this way helps us think more broadly and diverge into features and solutions. It is important to turn the problem around.

Once the “How Might We?” questions are defined, we start writing features, characteristics, and solutions that could meet them. At this stage, there are no restrictions on features. We will prioritize later and build the ones that contribute most to the project, but right now we need to generate as many ideas as possible. It is interesting to do these exercises with a design team that includes programmers, stakeholders, marketing, the boss, or whoever else. We can also do it alone, but it will add much more value with more people. Here are many features for each problem. If we come up with only one, that is all right too. This is what a completed proto journey looks like.

## Imagine Mila's experience, step by step

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L16 · 08:26–10:08

Imagine that Mila enters our application after seeing an ad on Facebook and downloads the app. She is happy because the ad says it will help her read more, keep track of her reading, read reviews, and so on. When she opens the app, we imagine she sees a home page with recommended books and comments from other users. But she has a problem: she does not have an account and does not know where to enter her details. As you can see, her satisfaction drops.

It is hard for her to find the account creation, but eventually she creates an account. The process is tedious and has many form fields: name, surname, and so on. Then she sees a very large button to add a book and says, “Ah, look, it is here.” As you can see, her satisfaction rises again. When she starts filling in the book details, she has to enter the title, author, year, number of pages, and so on. That is a drag, and her satisfaction drops again. She thinks, “Do I have to do this for every book I have read in my life? It is unworkable.” She could retire before she had entered all the information about her books. But when she shares her first book, she sees likes from other users on the platform, and her satisfaction rises again.

In this process, with its ups and downs, she has managed to do what she wanted, which was to add a book. But perhaps we can make the path one where she does not manage it. You might ask where I got this information: I am imagining it. If you cannot think of the steps, it is because you have not seen enough references. ## Generate solutions and features

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L16 · 10:49–12:51

Now we go to the lower part of the exercise and use “How Might We?” How might we improve the home screen? How might we make it easier for new users to create accounts? And how might we make the process of adding books easier, or even fun?

Based on our ideas and especially on the references, we write features, characteristics, and solutions on separate sticky notes for the first question: How might we improve the home screen? We could show book collections with their covers. We could ask what kinds of books the person likes the first time they open the app; that would be the onboarding process. We could show a home feed with posts from other users and news, so that it feels active. We could show activity by other users or friends after the user has logged in. We could include interviews with authors, or social reading events. We want to set goals for different users of our platform. For example, they might read a book before the end of the month, or everyone could read a volume of *Game of Thrones* during a set period.

For the second design opportunity, making account creation easier, at Astrid Hetz we realize that we could create the account directly when a user first enters during onboarding. We could let people register with Google or Facebook social login, or log in using the platform they are on, either Apple or Android. For the last critical point, making it easier or even fun to add book details, we could reduce the fields to a minimum and keep it as simple as possible. We could use the camera to scan a book's barcode. We could select books by their covers and scan the cover photos, importing the book data from a database just as with a barcode. We could import the books someone is reading on Kindle. We could also autocomplete the details so the title appears as soon as they start typing. These are some interesting features for our product.

If we are doing this in a group, we can vote on the ideas with green dot stickers (captions: *GOMETI*). Then we will know which features the group considers most important.

## The proto-journey process

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L16 · 12:51–13:57

Let us review how to make a proto journey. First, analyze the quantitative and qualitative data from our research. Always use the persona to decide what action the person will carry out in our product. As I said, it can be a product we are improving or one we invent. I recommend that you do not make journeys for trivial actions such as logging in or registering unless they are very important to the project.

As a group, lay out each step the user takes and how they would feel at each one. If you have trouble imagining the interactions, look for inspiration in the reference document. Remember that the ideal number of steps is five to eight. Once the process is complete, select the design opportunities. Three or four points are enough. Use “How Might We?” to define solutions for the opportunities where improvements are possible. Finally, generate several features for each solution. Keep the list unfiltered so that you can prioritize and discard items later. You can vote on the best ideas with green stickers (captions: *GOMEDS*). If you come up with few ideas, you have seen few references.

## What the exercise helps with

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L16 · 14:03–14:21

There are several advantages to using a journey. First, it helps us empathize with the user in the main processes and see how the user feels at each step. It helps us solve the critical points in those processes. We generate many ideas and new features for our product. It also helps us see the context in which products are used.
