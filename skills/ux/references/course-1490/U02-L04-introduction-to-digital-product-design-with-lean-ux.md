## The three foundations of digital product design

Anchor: 1490 · U2 · L04 · 02:24–03:22

*1490 · U02 · L04 · 02:24–03:22*

At Elastic Heads (the captions say “Elastic Hedge”), the teacher calls this the Triforce of product design, named after Nintendo's Zelda game. When designing digital products, we need to consider three basic pillars. User experience (*experiencia de usuario*) should add value for every user who interacts with the product. Business should receive value for the company. Development (*desarrollo*) requires us to know the cost or difficulty of implementing the product.

## Agile and Waterfall project work

Anchor: 1490 · U2 · L04 · 03:22–04:41

*1490 · U02 · L04 · 03:22–04:41*

The teacher compares Agile with the traditional Waterfall (*cascada*) model. In Waterfall, a phase cannot begin until the previous one is finished. For example, a team would first receive the full specifications for a product such as a website. Designers would then complete the visual design, pass it to developers and, with some luck, testing would happen at the end. This model comes from the industrial era. It is rigid and does not let the team learn from the project as it develops.

![1490 · U2 · L04 · 04:31](frames/U02L04-F0431.00.jpg)

On screen: "MODELO EN CASCADA" [Waterfall model] / "Waterfall" / "METODOLOGÍAS ÁGILES" [Agile methods] / "Agile Design" / "ESPECIFICACIONES" [Specifications] / "DISEÑO" [Design] / "DESARROLLO" [Development] / "TEST" [Test] / "VS."  
Shows: Waterfall phases run one after another; the Agile side overlaps specifications, design, testing and development. Thumbs-down and thumbs-up icons mark the comparison.

In an Agile process, the team can define a small part of the specifications and start designing right away, then make a prototype to test. Once users validate the prototype, development can begin. The team uses what it learns to change the specifications and can move forward or backward. It works in smaller blocks of time called sprints (*sprints*), which the teacher recommends. Designers also need to maintain control of their work so that it meets a minimum quality standard and does not deteriorate; this is why project management matters.

On screen: "Como diseñadores 👀 necesitamos mantener el control de nuestro trabajo." [As designers 👀, we need to keep control of our work.] / "Por eso es tan importante la gestión del proyecto." [That is why project management is so important.]

## Lean: value, waste and the Design Thinking transition

Anchor: 1490 · U2 · L04 · 04:41–05:12

*1490 · U02 · L04 · 04:41–05:12*

The Lean philosophy focuses on adding as much value as possible while reducing waste as much as possible. Waste includes designing several homepage screens or making five logo options when that work will not be used. Lean UX does not mean avoiding effort or making a mediocre product. It means working more intelligently, cutting work that adds no value and focusing on what helps users.

## Design Thinking and the double diamond

Anchor: 1490 · U2 · L04 · 05:12–06:48

*1490 · U02 · L04 · 05:12–06:48*

Design Thinking is a method or process for addressing organizational problems while keeping the user at the center and studying their needs.

![1490 · U2 · L04 · 05:19](frames/U02L04-F0519.00.jpg)

On screen: "DESIGN THINKING" / "Es una metodología o proceso para afrontar problemas en las organizaciones situando al usuario en el centro estudiando sus necesidades." [It is a method or process for addressing problems in organizations by placing the user at the center and studying their needs.]  
Shows: The Design Thinking definition highlights the user as central.

The double diamond (*doble diamante*) begins with a problem statement, or the project brief: what the company needs from the team. The discovery phase starts with research into users, competitors and the company. This is the diverging part, where the team gathers more and more information. It then synthesizes the information and finds discoveries, or insights (*hallazgos*): concepts found by asking users or researching. With that evidence, the team redefines the real problem. Companies often ask for things they do not need, like a patient asking a doctor for antibiotics when they have a common cold. The team writes a new brief, then begins ideation. It generates many ideas without rejecting them yet, unlike the old expectation that a team had to get it right first time. Here, the goal is to fail quickly and learn. The team makes prototypes to test design ideas with real users and gather information, then arrives at the specific solution it is looking for. The unit resources include a PDF in which Dan Nesler explains this process.

## Lean UX principles

Anchor: 1490 · U2 · L04 · 06:48–08:20

*1490 · U02 · L04 · 06:48–08:20*

The teacher introduces the main Lean UX principles. Teams should be small and multidisciplinary: people from different departments bring more value than a team of designers alone. The focus should be on outcomes rather than the deliverables that used to be written up in project documentation. Teams should remove waste by noticing and dropping work that does not contribute to the objective. They should deliver work in small packages so the audience can validate it early. The teacher recalls that when he worked in advertising, a fully designed and programmed website was sometimes presented all at once, and there were usually problems; smaller deliveries avoid those shocks.

In Lean UX (the teacher says “Ninja UX” here), continuous learning is important: teams need to know what users do and why. They should make prototypes before starting to analyze and validate as early as possible. The teacher says he has invested a great deal of money in projects only for users not to use the product, so it is important to learn whether it is useful before the company grows. Unlike traditional work, Lean UX permits mistakes; failures help refine digital products over time. Teams should also avoid stars and gurus. No one knows the project better than the team, and expensive outside experts often add little value.

## Managing work with Kanban

Anchor: 1490 · U2 · L04 · 08:20–10:21

*1490 · U02 · L04 · 08:20–10:21*

A Kanban board is a tool for visualizing work and working in an Agile way, focused on what needs to be done. The teacher first explains three columns: To Do for tasks that need doing, Doing for the task being worked on now, and Done for completed tasks.

![1490 · U2 · L04 · 08:30](frames/U02L04-F0830.00.jpg)

On screen: "KANBAN" / "Un tablero de kanban es una herramienta ágil de gestión de proyectos diseñada para ayudar a visualizar el trabajo, limitar el trabajo en curso y maximizar la eficiencia." [A Kanban board is an Agile project-management tool designed to help visualize work, limit work in progress and maximize efficiency.]  
Shows: A definition of a Kanban board.

![1490 · U2 · L04 · 08:47](frames/U02L04-F0847.00.jpg)

On screen: "TO DO 📥" / "DOING 💪" / "DONE 😌"  
Shows: An empty three-column board.

First, write every project task on a note and sort the notes by importance, from top to bottom. When starting a task, move its note from To Do to Doing and work on it. Once the task is finished, move it to Done and take the next one. In the example, the next task is doing the Domestika course exercise. Each task has an estimated duration in parentheses: buying Post-its, for instance, takes half an hour. Estimating project and task times matters because we usually underestimate how long they will take. Moving through the board helps people focus and lets the rest of the team see project progress. Kanban is flexible and can be set up as needed. The teacher shows the board used at the studio in Notion and says unit resources will include templates for Trello and Notion, both recommended tools to start using.
