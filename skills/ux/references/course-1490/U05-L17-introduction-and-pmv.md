## Feature creep and reducing a crowded product

Anchor: 1490 · U5 · L17 · 00:00–02:00

In this lesson, we will learn what a minimum viable product is and see which characteristics our product needs in order to succeed. I will show you.

Feature creep (*featuritis*; captions: *reflective creep o saturitis*) is the tendency to keep adding new features and functions, which inevitably leads to complex products that are confusing and difficult to use. Put simply, adding many functions to a product can spoil the user experience.

Stakeholders will generally bring ideas from their departments. Our job is to add the functions users need to meet their goals. Everything else is unnecessary. As product designers, we have to learn to say no and explain why. Part of our work with Product Managers is to define functions that meet both user needs and company objectives.

In the first image, the entire Microsoft Word toolbar is open, leaving very little space for writing; it is impossible to work with. The other graphic is more visual. In this real case, we were asked to make the interface of a candidate-management system for different kinds of jobs much lighter. The consultants using it to manage job applicants could not find the data, or had great difficulty finding something as simple as a surname. The minimum viable product (*producto mínimo viable*, PMV) was not a complete redesign. We added tabs and drop-downs for the information. We did not create anything new or add more functions; we tried to get the greatest benefit with the least effort.

## Define the minimum viable product

Anchor: 1490 · U5 · L17 · 02:00–02:48

A minimum viable product, or MVP, is the smallest version of a product that can be created to check whether a hypothesis is valid. It lets us gain the greatest amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort. Balance is the key when we create products. If we include too few functions, we end up with a poor product nobody will want to use. Later, we will see products built by companies with more funding and resources that we can use as inspiration. To begin, we should stay in the middle zone: the minimum needed for the product to work successfully.

In the end, a minimum viable product helps us apply the Lean philosophy of Build, Measure, Learn, so that everything we add has a purpose and we do not add it just because someone asked for it.

## Compare waterfall and iterative development

Anchor: 1490 · U5 · L17 · 02:48–03:34

Let us compare developing a product with the waterfall method (*metodología en cascada*) and using our agile approach (captions: *alí*). In the first example, the waterfall method, the parts are made first and then assembled. We do not know whether people will want to use the car or whether it will work until the car is finished.

The middle example would not work either: the product changes. A scooter is not a car, it does not have the same target audience, and one product has nothing to do with the other. In the third example, we start with a minimum viable product: a car that does not go very fast or works only adequately. We improve it through iterations, little by little, always using what we learn from research and what users need.

## Keep the feature set small and design it well

Anchor: 1490 · U5 · L17 · 03:34–04:59

When we make a minimum viable product, we must avoid including every possible function and include the main functions of the product, making sure they work very well. Some programmers think that an MVP means implementing basic functions badly, with poor design and a poor user experience. That is not right: an MVP has the minimum characteristics, but they must be well designed.

I use a chart that programmers often use to explain this idea. The client's brief is generally not what users need. The Project Manager may understand the brief as a need to meet deadlines rather than produce a solution that works for users. The CEO may ask for something impossible. Some programmers may literally throw code at the problem; it will work, but poorly. This is what happens when external consultants try to sell us a mega-project and mega-product that usually do not come to pass.

The project documentation starts with a lot of enthusiasm but is generally abandoned. The Front-end team is supposed to implement the visual part of the code, but it may be left partly to their discretion and half-finished. The project budget goes up and down like a roller coaster: it looks as if we will finish early, then late. The launch date is generally missed. In the final image, what users really needed was a wheel tied to a rope so they could swing. Nothing complicated.

## Choose a platform for the first version

Anchor: 1490 · U5 · L17 · 04:59–06:30

When we talk about an MVP, the same question always comes up: should we make a native app for iOS or Android, or a web app? A native app is faster and more stable, has more functions, seems more professional, and can use native features such as the camera or accelerometer. But it has serious disadvantages. We need to develop for both Android and iOS, which means twice the development team. We also have to design two versions, using Android's design patterns in one and iOS's in the other. Updating takes longer because we have to make the changes, upload them to the app store, and wait for users to download them. Most importantly, developing a native app is very expensive.

A possible solution is a web app. It needs one development effort, is more accessible, opens directly in the browser, and is always up to date because it is a website; people do not have to download it. Its disadvantages are a worse user experience, slower performance, limited functions, and gestures that do not work. The advantage is that it is much cheaper to develop. So for a minimum viable product, I recommend starting with a web app whenever we can. If it works and begins to attract users, we can invest in native apps for iOS and Android.

## Launch small, learn, and update

Anchor: 1490 · U5 · L17 · 06:30–07:24

Here are some recommendations for making a minimum viable product. First, start as small as possible, with the fewest functions that let users satisfy their need. Then, as we experiment, test prototypes, and try the first version, we can continue with the business. If people do not use it or it does not help them, we can pivot and use what we learned for another project.

Whenever we launch a new product, we should aim it at early adopters: people who are generally interested in technology and like trying new apps and services. It is very important to launch as soon as possible and update often.

Now that you know what a minimum viable product is, we will learn a new tool for redefining our value proposition: the Value Proposition Canvas.
