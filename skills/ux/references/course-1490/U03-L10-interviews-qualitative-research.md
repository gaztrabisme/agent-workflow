## Interviews as qualitative research

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L10 · 00:00–00:37

Interviews are the most important qualitative research tool (*investigación cualitativa*) for studying users. They are particularly useful for identifying user needs. To conduct one effectively, we need empathy and, above all, improvisation. We work from a guide, but often need to probe an answer by improvising follow-up questions.

## Whom to interview

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L10 · 00:37–01:15

There are three kinds of interviewees: users, potential users, or clients of the company we are working for; stakeholders (*stakeholders*), meaning people from another department who take part even though they are not directly working on our project; and experts in the subject area. We always interview users and stakeholders, and interview experts only when needed. If the digital product is very technical, for a specific niche, or for a specialist group of users, we should always ask experts about the subject.

## Preparing and conducting an interview

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L10 · 01:15–02:11

When possible, learn about the interviewee and set an objective for the interview. Prepare a draft guide that can be just three or four bullet points listing the topics to cover. At the beginning, introduce yourself and explain the project context. Ask questions simply, in language the interviewee can understand; if possible, use a conversational tone so the person can relax. It is difficult, but we should not give our opinion: stay neutral and seek the information we need without expressing a view. Keep the interview short so we do not overwhelm or bore the interviewee; the slide recommends about 30 minutes.

In this unit’s resources there is an interview the teacher conducted with someone who might fit the audience for the reading app project. He wanted to learn about that person’s reading habits and whether the app might interest them.

## Advantages and disadvantages

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L10 · 02:11–03:11

Interviews help us empathize with users, their needs, and their problems. They help define what a product must include to be useful to them. Unlike a questionnaire, an interview lets us probe questions: we can improvise and ask about concepts that seem useful to the project. The information we get should be scalable and lead to action. The teacher says that interviewing only five people should give us enough information to begin defining the project.

Interviews also have disadvantages. They can lack objectivity: it is easy to drift to another topic and lose focus. An interviewer may react negatively to an answer they dislike. When interpreting answers, interviewers can bring their own biases and prejudices. Interviews also take much more time than surveys.

## Interpreting what users say

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L10 · 03:11–03:32

Jakob Nielsen’s point is that what users say and what they do are different. Users often answer questions in ways they think will please us. This happens often, so we must interpret all the information we gather, from both quantitative and qualitative methods.

## The Five Whys

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L10 · 03:32–04:35

The Five Whys (*los cinco porqués*) is a simple way to probe questions and find the real reason for a problem or situation. Imagine a client asks us to redesign a website. Rather than accept the project immediately, ask why they want a redesign. They say sales are falling. Ask why sales are falling: people buy from the competition. Ask why people buy from competitors: competitors offer payment plans. Ask why the client does not offer those plans: they have not integrated them into checkout. Ask why they have not integrated them when competitors offer them: the CEO said no. The problem, then, may not be a redesign at all; it may be as simple as persuading the CEO to add payment options to the checkout process.

## Recording interview findings

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L10 · 04:35–05:18

The unit resources include a collection of user-research questions and a template for recording each interview’s results. Without the Lean approach, it might seem most logical to transcribe every interview in full so it is documented. The teacher recommends recording the interview and then writing down only the information relevant to the project. Transcribing everything wastes time and resources. The next lesson covers other qualitative research methods.
