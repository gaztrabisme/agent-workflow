## Ten reminders for running a user test

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L30 · 00:00–03:44

The instructor gives ten tips for testing. First, ask participants to think aloud (*pensar en voz alta*; “Think Aloud”; captions: *Think Adopt*). Give them an example so they understand what to do: “When I press this button, it is because I want to go to the shopping cart.” If someone stays silent for a long time, ask what they are thinking. The second tip is that anything can be tested with prototypes and users. He does not mean only screens: prototypes can be made from cardboard, boxes, or whatever is available. What matters is running the test and getting information.

![1490 · U7 · L30 · 00:27.50](frames/U07L30-F0027.50.jpg)

On screen: "CONSEJO 1/10" [Tip 1/10] / "PENSAR EN ALTO: “THINK ALOUD”." [Think aloud: “Think aloud”.]  
Shows: A dark title card for the first tip, with “Think Aloud” emphasized.

The third tip is that behavior matters more than opinion. Participants may say things during a test that their reactions or the way they navigate contradict. Someone can call an app easy and intuitive, yet get lost repeatedly while using it. So watch how the person behaves and give that more weight than their opinion of the prototype or app.

![1490 · U7 · L30 · 00:41.60](frames/U07L30-F0041.60.jpg)

On screen: "CONSEJO 3/10" [Tip 3/10] / "COMPORTAMIENTO > OPINIÓN" [Behavior > opinion] / "CREE LO QUE VES POR ENCIMA DE LO QUE ESCUCHAS." [Trust what you see over what you hear.]  
Shows: A dark slide comparing observed behavior with stated opinion.

As Jakob Nielsen advised, give users defined tasks. Do not leave a prototype open for them to explore freely. Free exploration may be useful for concept testing (*prueba de concepto*), but for a high-fidelity test (*alta fidelidad*) that evaluates a product’s usability (*usabilidad*), define the tasks the user must do very clearly.

![1490 · U7 · L30 · 01:11.60](frames/U07L30-F0111.60.jpg)

On screen: "CONSEJO 4/10" [Tip 4/10] / "TAREAS DEFINIDAS, NUNCA UN CAMINO LIBRE." [Defined tasks, never a free path.]  
Shows: A dark slide advising the tester to give defined tasks.

Unlike Jakob Nielsen's statement that tests should always use the target audience, the instructor says to use that audience whenever possible, but otherwise test with anyone, even a colleague from the office. After one test, improve the prototype and test it again—a retest (*retesteo*; captions: *RETEST-T*). Use different people who have not taken the first test. If you test with the same people again, they will already know the product, so the results will not be as good.

![1490 · U7 · L30 · 01:38.30](frames/U07L30-F0138.30.jpg)

On screen: "CONSEJO 5/10" [Tip 5/10] / "SI SE PUEDE, CON EL PÚBLICO OBJETIVO" [If possible, with the target audience] / "SI NO, CON CUALQUIERA..." [If not, with anyone...] / "PERO HAZ TESTS!!!" [But test!!!]  
Shows: A dark slide about choosing test participants.

![1490 · U7 · L30 · 01:41.60](frames/U07L30-F0141.60.jpg)

On screen: "CONSEJO 6/10" [Tip 6/10] / "RE-TESTEA PARA COMPARAR" [Retest to compare] / "PERO CON USUARIOS DIFERENTES." [But with different users.]  
Shows: A dark slide advising a comparison test with different participants.

A common beginner’s mistake is helping the user. If someone gets stuck, tell them to move on to the next task. Do not give your opinion either. Do not say, “This is a really rough prototype”; stay neutral and do not hint at the answer or give your opinion of the design. This matters especially when you are showing an interface design or a high-fidelity prototype. One tactic is to say that you did not make the design and are only conducting the test, then tell the participant at the end that you were also the designer. That way you do not pressure them or offer your subjective opinion while they are working.

![1490 · U7 · L30 · 02:11.60](frames/U07L30-F0211.60.jpg)

On screen: "CONSEJO 7/10" [Tip 7/10] / "NO AYUDAR AL USUARIO NI OPINAR." [Do not help the user or give your opinion.] / "NO DAR PISTAS NI TU OPINIÓN SOBRE EL DISEÑO." [Do not give hints or your opinion about the design.]  
Shows: A dark slide about not helping or prompting a test participant.

The user is never to blame. Repeat this to participants because many feel under pressure when someone watches them use a product or prototype. Explain that you are evaluating the design work, not the person, and make sure it does not feel like a school exam.

![1490 · U7 · L30 · 02:41.60](frames/U07L30-F0241.60.jpg)

On screen: "CONSEJO 8/10" [Tip 8/10] / "LA CULPA NUNCA ES DEL USUARIO." [The user is never to blame.] / "DÍSELO Y QUE NO PAREZCA UN EXAMEN DEL COLEGIO." [Tell them, and make sure it does not feel like a school exam.]  
Shows: A dark slide reminding the tester to reassure the participant.

Another good practice is to interview the participant after the test, to get more qualitative information about both the prototype and the user. If there is little time or the test is remote, use a questionnaire or survey instead.

![1490 · U7 · L30 · 03:04.30](frames/U07L30-F0304.30.jpg)

On screen: "CONSEJO 9/10" [Tip 9/10] / "ENTREVISTA AL FINAL PARA COMPLETAR," [Interview at the end to complete it,] / "O USA UN CUESTIONARIO." [or use a questionnaire.]  
Shows: A dark slide recommending a follow-up interview or questionnaire.

Finally, test the test itself. Always try out the prototype and the tasks before you start testing with participants. The instructor then gives examples of questions or comments to use during a test: “What are you thinking?” “What do you think will happen now?” “Is this what you expected to happen?” “Was that confusing?” “What do you think that button does?” Sometimes the participant moves so quickly that the tester does not catch what happened. Ask them to repeat the process so you can identify the problem.

![1490 · U7 · L30 · 03:15.50](frames/U07L30-F0315.50.jpg)

On screen: "CONSEJO 10/10" [Tip 10/10] / "HAZ UN TEST DEL TEST." [Test the test.] / "SIEMPRE PRUEBA EL PROTOTIPO Y LAS TAREAS ANTES." [Always test the prototype and tasks beforehand.]  
Shows: A dark slide advising the tester to rehearse the prototype and tasks.

## Setting up the reading-app test

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L30 · 03:44–06:50

The instructor greets Lola and explains that they will do a short test of an app about reading habits. They are evaluating the idea. The design is hand-drawn and very rough; they want to learn whether readers might be interested in it. He shares the prototype in the chat and asks her to share her screen.

Before starting the tasks, he asks about her background. She is a creative copywriter and is 34. Her reading comes in waves: she reads a lot on vacation and less when work is busy, ranging from a book a month to one every six months. She would like to read more but does not have all the time she wants, and fitting reading around work can be difficult. She likes novels that are not too cerebral (the captions say *sensudas*), rather than very demanding books or beach reads; she wants something cultural that will not make her work too hard. She mostly chooses books from recommendations and authors she likes, reading everything those authors publish. She occasionally reads technical books about writing, but finds them difficult. The instructor says that one mistake with technical books is to read from the first page to the last instead of skipping around to find what you need.

Asked whether she underlines passages or takes notes, Lola says she mainly wants the experience of reading and sometimes takes a photo instead. She keeps those photos in her phone’s image library, essentially as screenshots. She reads physical books, not on a tablet or Kindle.

The team wants to bring reading clubs together with book recommendations and discussion, and to build on the activity of its users. They hope the app will encourage people to set aside time to read instead of watching another episode of a series or doing something else. At this stage, the prototype is hand-drawn and has no functionality yet.

He tells Lola there will be four tasks. He will ask for her opinion afterward, first give her the tasks, explain the design in more detail, and then ask a few final questions. ## Proposed screens and first reaction

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L30 · 10:35–13:20

After the tasks, the instructor walks Lola through the features the team imagines for the app. On the home screen, stories at the top would show what people are reading. A user could upload a photo of a book or a passage they like, as Lola does with her own books, or copy a passage from a Kindle, Kobo, or another tablet. The stories would work like Instagram stories. The app would also have its own content, such as interviews with authors. Below that, a feed would show contacts’ activity and the passages they underline, as well as what they have read. The team plans reading challenges. For example, someone aiming to read ten books in a year might have finished six; friends could congratulate them or comment on the update.

## Feedback and features to consider

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L30 · 13:20–16:55

Lola says that on this screen she would rather see what people are reading than what the people she follows recommend. The instructor suggests this may be because recommendations from the app could have commercial interests. Lola agrees. She says she notices something similar with Filmin (captions: *filming*): she prefers recommendations from a person to ones from Filmin. Some platform recommendations are good, but she wonders whether they are made because it benefits the platform or because of a commercial agreement. The instructor mentions Mast, an app for television programmes and series, where people can follow others who keep posting recommendations. He agrees that information from other users can be much more valuable than recommendations from the tool itself.

Other users could comment or like the post. The instructor could open Fede’s profile to see his library and what he is reading, just as on their own profile. Lola says that sounds good.

On screen: [rest omitted]

Lola says she is not sure how adding books should work. The instructor says they will add it, but they have a small UX pain point (captions: *WXPainPoint*): entering every title one by one would be tedious. They have looked at apps that scan a book cover, look up its details in a database, and let the user accept the returned information. They are exploring different approaches. They may also use game-like mechanics, such as levels, points, or a ranking, to encourage users to add books to their libraries.

Lola suggests a queue of books to read in the future. She often has books she has bought waiting on a table, then forgets what she wanted to read. When someone recommends a book, she may put it in her phone notes and later forget about it. She would like a future-reading queue, like a playlist. The instructor says they looked at some examples of a book queue and wrote the idea down, though it did not make it into this first prototype.

They had also considered tracking when someone lends a book, including the date and who has it. Lola remembers this idea and likes it. Their favorite books are often the first ones they lend out and the first ones they lose track of. The instructor is glad the idea appeals to her, and they end the test.

## Analyze results and iterate

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L30 · 17:02–18:17

After concept testing (*prueba de concepto*) or testing the prototype, analyze the results to decide what to remove and what to add, then improve the design solution through repeated iterations (*iteraciones*). Start with the lowest-fidelity prototype because it is easiest to make; in this case, the team drew it by hand. A good practice is to sketch first and make a paper prototype. Once several users have tested it and the team has results, they can make a digital version in any program they prefer: Photoshop, Keynote, PowerPoint, Figma (captions: *Sigma*), or Sketch.

Shows: A full-screen reminder to analyze test results and comments before iterating on the design.

To evaluate a digital product’s ease of use or its interaction design, the instructor says that high-fidelity wireframes (*wireframes de alta fidelidad*; captions: *WildFriends*) are needed so users feel more comfortable. Paper cannot validate the usability of a product. The course has now covered making paper prototypes and validating them with users; the team can iterate as many times as necessary. In the next lesson, the instructor will explain what a successful case study needs so the students can present their projects effectively.
