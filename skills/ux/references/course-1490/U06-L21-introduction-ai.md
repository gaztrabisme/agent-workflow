## What information architecture organizes

Anchor: 1490 · U6 · L21 · 00:00–01:33

In this lesson I’m introducing information architecture (*arquitectura de la información*). It helps us organize content and make it findable for users. Information architecture is the art and science (*articiencia* in the captions) of structuring and classifying digital product content so that users can find and manage it.

Usability.gov defines it as organizing, structuring, and labeling content effectively and sustainably. The book *Information Architecture for the World Wide Web* by Rosenfeld (captions: *Ridley*) and Morville is an advanced read, but it is one of the best-known and broadest books on the subject. It offers several possible definitions. One is the structural design of shared information environments. Another is the combination of systems for organizing, labeling, searching, and navigating information in digital products. A third describes shaping information products and experiences to support usability and findability. A fourth calls it an emerging discipline and community of practice that brings design and architecture principles into the digital world. That last definition makes the book sound dated, because information architecture is now an established discipline.

We’ll use the second definition, with one correction: it applies not just to websites and intranets but to digital products generally (captions: *sitios web sin tránsito*).

## Five ways to organize anything

Anchor: 1490 · U6 · L21 · 01:33–02:45

How would you organize a record store? You could sort records alphabetically or by year. I don’t think sorting them by color would work, but let’s consider how information can be organized. There are only five ways to organize anything: by location, alphabetical order, time, category, or hierarchy. Rome is a city in Italy, so you can locate it on a map. The word “Rome” starts with R, so you would look for it under R in a dictionary. On a timeline, Rome was founded, I think, in 753 BC. If you search for travel, Rome might appear in the category of romantic trips. By hierarchy, Rome belongs to Italy, Italy belongs to Europe, and Europe belongs to Earth. Richard Saul Wurman (captions: *Richard Sol Werman*), an architect and graphic designer, identified these five ways.

## Where information architecture fits

Anchor: 1490 · U6 · L21 · 02:45–03:21

In this diagram, the large umbrella represents user experience (*experiencia de usuario*), and information architecture is one of the areas beneath it. It relates to visual design, interaction, traditional architecture, information, and marketing—in short, to the content and navigation of a site.

When designing information architecture, we need to account for three factors. Context includes business objectives, company culture—because a startup differs from a traditional company—technology, resources, and technical constraints. Content includes its objectives, whether it will be watched in a video or read on a page, document and data types, size, and current structure. Users are the people using the product: how they search for information and what they need.

## Four components and metadata

Anchor: 1490 · U6 · L21 · 03:21–04:20

There are four components of information architecture. Organization systems (*sistemas de organización*) determine how we categorize and structure information in a product, for example by author, date, topic, or geography. Navigation systems (*sistemas de navegación*) describe how users move through that information. We might have global navigation in the menu, local navigation through subcategories, and contextual navigation through links. Labeling systems (*sistemas de etiquetado*) describe how information is represented, such as food labels for gluten-free or vegan products, or scientific terms. Search systems (*sistemas de búsqueda*) describe how users find information, using search boxes, search fields, keywords, and tags. To use these components, we also need information metadata (*metadatos*) such as titles, descriptions, and authors.

## Names, categories, and the shopping experience

Anchor: 1490 · U6 · L21 · 06:13–07:41

There are three terms that we do not use every day but that are important in information architecture. Ontology (*ontología*) is the name that represents a product. In this case, this IKEA sofa is called Strandmon (captions: *Stransmo*). That name represents the sofa. Taxonomy (*taxonomía*) means the product categories. This product belongs in the armchairs category, not the tables category. That category helps us find it if we are looking for an armchair because we are moving and want to buy one. We would not search for a name that tells us nothing; we would look under “armchairs.” Choreography (*coreografía*) is the shopping experience: how ontology and taxonomy come together—where I enter and how I search.

The goal of information architecture is always to help people find and understand information, and to know where they are. We can use it in a library to find books; on a metro map to find stations and move around; in a dictionary; in a restaurant menu that groups dishes into starters and mains; in a bus timetable; in an airport; and even in a hospital’s health records.

## Wayfinding and the first principle

Anchor: 1490 · U6 · L21 · 07:41–09:09

Information architecture helps users understand where they are, what they have found, what they can expect, and what is around them. In airports, people can feel intimidated, but they can still find where to board a plane. A supermarket also has information architecture: products are grouped by aisle, so we can usually find them easily, even though sometimes the store makes that harder. On the Madrid metro map, colors mark different lines and icons represent different map elements.

Dan Brown, a prominent designer and user experience expert, has defined eight principles that can help us evaluate or create information architecture. The first is the principle of objects (*principio de los objetos*). Treat information blocks as things that can change and grow as the project grows. On Pinterest, some cards have only a title; others have no content beyond the user name, like this one. Other cards have text that is cut off with an ellipsis when it does not fit. Our systems and pages may grow, so we should plan for that when we organize information. Pinterest uses different card types depending on the content, and truncates content that is too long so it does not damage the design.

## Principles of choice, disclosure, and examples

Anchor: 1490 · U6 · L21 · 09:09–10:38

The second principle, which we at Elastic Heads really like, is choice (*principio de las elecciones*). Create digital products with few options so the user can complete the task they want to do. A typical Chinese restaurant menu can have 134 dishes on just one side. That creates indecision and makes a decision take a long time. Another kind of restaurant limits the menu so you can decide quickly and buy sooner; that is why it is called fast food. This relates to Hick’s law (*ley de Hick*): the time required to make a decision increases as the number of alternatives increases. Here you can compare a Samsung television remote from 2016 with the one they released in 2018.

## Entry points, multiple classification, and focused navigation

Anchor: 1490 · U6 · L21 · 10:38–12:29

The fifth principle is front doors (*principio de las puertas delanteras*). Designers are often asked to design a site’s home page, without considering that many users may land directly on a product page. If you search Google for Pokémon shirts, you will probably go straight to a product page rather than that shop’s home page. We have to account for this when we design experiences. If a site is already running, we can ask the SEO department which page users usually enter through. In this Kymco motorcycle redesign, the main menu includes a link to the brand’s press office. That is not very useful, because journalists search Google for “Kymco press office” and go directly to the page. We proposed putting the link in the footer, but we were required to put it at the end of the main menu. This is how a journalist finds it.

The screen identifies the sixth principle as multiple classification (*principio de clasificación múltiple*). Spotify illustrates it: we can replay what we have recently heard; find music we like by album or artist; make playlists; and browse by moods, among other options. This gives people several ways to classify and listen to music.

The seventh principle is focused navigation (*principio de la navegación enfocada*). Navigation items should be created and labeled according to the content they lead to. Menu items should tell us what we will find when we click them. On this Kymco site, the insurer’s logo was placed in the main menu. If I do not tell you it is an insurance logo, or you do not know the insurer, you might wonder what “Genius” is and where it leads. That does not make sense, because people will not know what it is or how to use it.

## The principle of growth

Anchor: 1490 · U6 · L21 · 12:29–12:55
