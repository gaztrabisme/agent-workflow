Low-fidelity wireframes (*wireframes de baja fidelidad*) are ideal for quickly laying out and organizing the modules and content with a client. We can work very quickly and apply the first law of creativity from FUDD (captions: *FUD*): to get one good idea, get a lot of ideas. I recommend making many low-fidelity wireframes, then validating them or choosing the ones we think will work best.

## Benefits and limits of low fidelity

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L26 · 02:18–03:13

The advantages of low-fidelity wireframes are that we can develop screens, interactions and processes for our digital product very quickly. They make it easier to focus on structural design decisions: what to put on the left, what to put at the top or bottom, for example. We can preview the navigation, structure and different interactions in minutes.

This level of fidelity usually does not work well for mobile versions. The boxes and lines make it hard to understand what the screen represents, so I recommend high-fidelity wireframes for mobile and low-fidelity wireframes more for desktop. I do not recommend this type for prototypes (*prototipos*) that we will validate with users because it is too abstract; users may not know what each box or line means. Another lesson from our early use of these wireframes is to explain each element when showing them to a client. If you present them without explaining how they work, the client may be perplexed. Tell them, for example, “This box represents an image; this is a paragraph.”

## Testing with content-rich wireframes

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L26 · 04:26–05:16

For mobile apps and websites, we will almost always make high-fidelity wireframes and then design, without first making low-fidelity ones. Remember that for any kind of wireframe, we always sketch by hand first. In this other example, all the interface elements are present—icons, text and so on—but the visual design has not been applied. I recommend adding color only to the clickable parts, such as buttons and links. This level of wireframe lets us test with users. When testing with wireframes or a design, include content even if it is not final, so users can read it and see the product in a way that is as close to reality as possible.

## Choosing fidelity and signaling unfinished work

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L26 · 05:16–06:30

When designing with wireframes, we have to choose the fidelity our project needs. For a desktop version, we can sketch by hand, make low-fidelity wireframes, and then move to design. For an app or mobile version of a website, we can sketch, make high-fidelity wireframes, and then design.

![1490 · U7 · L26 · 05:37.10](frames/U07L26-F0537.10.jpg)

Shows: A slide stating that the project determines the fidelity level to use.

As you can see, we use Comic Sans. Our studio likes using Comic Sans in wireframes, and I recommend it because it tells the client or anyone looking at the prototype that this is not the final design. There may be a client who thinks Comic Sans is the final design, but we also tell them that it is not. This helps distinguish design from wireframes.

## High-fidelity advantages and drawbacks

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L26 · 06:30–07:13

High-fidelity wireframes let us show more detail about design and interaction. We can see which elements are clickable, and include dropdowns, icons and menus. We can add content and validate designs in user tests. These wireframes come close to the final structure and outline realistic product behavior. With more detail, they are easier for users and clients to understand.

The drawbacks are that they take more time to make. Clients may confuse one with a mockup or the final designed screen. It is also easy to spend too much time making a wireframe look good and lose focus on finding the design solution.

## Illustrating interfaces and showing data loading

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L26 · 09:28–10:04

One use for skeleton frames is to make illustrations of a user interface. In the Plexo example, the interface is a simple table with rows. Rather than resizing and pasting a screenshot of the interface—which would become very small and blurry—we can use a skeleton frame to illustrate this kind of interface. It may not be as pleasant to look at because it is enterprise software, but the frame makes a lighter, more minimal illustration than pasting a screenshot as it is.

Another use is to show data loading. When we open a program such as Slack, its chat and conversation data take time to load. The placeholders show where the parts will go, so users can see the layout and get the sense that something is loading. We see this in Slack, LinkedIn on desktop and mobile, and Instagram. Loading usually takes less than a second or a few seconds; the user already knows where each part will appear.

## Put designs in context and choose a template

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L26 · 10:04–11:05

Finally, skeleton frames show the place and size of our designs. For example, if someone asks us to design the YouTube search bar, in the past we might have sent only that piece in an email, without any context for the size of things. A skeleton frame shows how large the search bar would be in proportion to the rest of the page. The same goes for the player section. If you are designing a small part of a web page, I recommend using a skeleton frame to put it in context.
