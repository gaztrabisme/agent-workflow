## Why sketch wireframes by hand

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L28 · 00:00–01:38

In this lesson, we will see how to make a wireframe by hand from start to finish. Let’s see.

Drawing wireframes by hand, which the captions call *wallframes*, is what we know as sketching (*bocetado*). It helps us work quickly when we are conceptualizing products, for several reasons. First, it is inclusive: any member of the team can do this exercise, whether they are a designer, programmer, or someone in marketing. It is also accessible. You do not have to learn to use design programs such as Sketch, Figma, or Photoshop. We only need a marker and can draw; we do not need to draw well.

Third, hand-drawn wireframes are easy to share. We can photograph or scan them and send them through Google Drive, Dropbox, email, or whatever works.

They also help us detect design problems very early. Before making a prototype or high-fidelity wireframes (*alta fidelidad*) for a new feature, we should draw it and validate it first—even before we start applying high fidelity. We should find the first problems while drawing on paper, which is the fastest and least expensive way to do it in terms of project costs.

Next, sketching forces us to focus on the solutions. If we try to make wireframes directly in a computer program, we will keep looking at alignment and shades of gray. If we draw them by hand, we can focus on the solution we are looking for. Finally, sketching lets us visualize ideas very quickly, saving a lot of time and effort.

## Examples of hand-drawn wireframes

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L28 · 01:38–04:32

We can make wireframes to understand a problem, as in this example by Joshua Wall. He has drawn several stages and how he would solve something. As you can see, it is not pretty to look at, but it helps him understand things and how he might solve them.

Here is another example by Michelle Sousa. She has drawn several screens and flows for herself, to understand them before cleaning them up. We can make a somewhat more legible version for a prototype, as we will do later. Basically, this exercise helps us work through problems more deeply than if we start with a design program.

Here is another example by John Conway, who has made something like a high-fidelity wireframe by including all the data that should be on this screen. Imagine a flight or travel dashboard. Before designing screens or a high-fidelity wireframe, we can make a small sketch like this and show it to the business team to see whether all the required data is there. That will save us a lot of work when we validate the data.

This next example is by Denise Bueno and was made for testing with users, which is what we will do. Instead of using thick markers, she used finer ones to make the wireframe more detailed, so users can understand the different screens.

For our app, Catarina Silvarde and the Elastic Hedge team helped me. I had drawn the screens much more roughly, and she cleaned them up a little so we could test them with users. Thank you very much, Catalina.

After looking at the references, interviews, and surveys, we chose several design patterns: horizontal scrolling, stories, activity from my contacts, and reading challenges. For example, if you manage to read 10 books by the end of the year, you unlock a medal. We also have highlights, book recommendations, books that are popular in the community, and collections such as horror and fantasy books.

Here we would have the library, with the books I have read, the total number of pages, and information we thought might be relevant and now want to test with users. They may tell us, “I do not want to know how many books I have read; I already know that,” or “I am not interested in how many hours I spent reading.” If we validate this prototype or these wireframes with users and they do not care about something, we can remove it by erasing it or making a new screen. Here we would have the book I am reading, and so on. There are a lot of screens.

Whenever we draw wireframes by hand—or even when we use a computer—we should look for design-pattern references. We need to see how the problem has been solved before and base our work on apps that are working and successful with their audiences. So we should always look for pattern references.

## Keep the sketching exercise quick

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L28 · 04:32–05:17

One other recommendation is to limit the time. Do not be a perfectionist with sketches: you are not painting a picture or making an illustration. Set a timer, or decide, “I will do all the sketches in one hour or two hours,” but do not extend the time. That would defeat the purpose of the exercise, which is to work as quickly as possible.

Finally, always use a marker. If you make a mistake, put a post-it over it and continue drawing. Do not use a ruler, eraser, pencil, or traditional drawing materials.

You have seen how quick and effective it is to work on wireframes by hand. In the next lesson, we will learn how to animate them.
