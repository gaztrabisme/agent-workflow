## From a sketch to the finished interface

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L25 · 00:00–01:00

In this lesson we will learn the different types of wireframes (*wireframes*; captions: *Warframe*) and when to use them. We can draw the screens of a digital product in several ways. The first is a sketch (*boceto*), which we should always make before designing a screen. It can be on a tablet or in a notebook, but it is important to draw it by hand. When we work on the different kinds of wireframes, we can become focused on alignment, colors and shades of gray; that is not the work we need to do at this point. The sketch helps us decide how to distribute the parts of the screen and how to think through its design.

## What each stage contains

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L25 · 01:00–02:58

We always start with a sketch, then make different kinds of wireframes. In low-fidelity wireframes (*wireframes de baja fidelidad*), we represent content with boxes and text with lines. A paragraph is shown as lines; a title can be a darker line or box. Button text is also shown as a gray line. This lets us work more quickly.

High-fidelity wireframes (*wireframes de alta fidelidad*) add details such as dropdowns and icons, like the stars in this example. We do not add the images yet: if we do, the client or the rest of the team may start commenting on the photograph instead of focusing on the screen layout and the elements it needs. We can include an icon such as a back arrow, text, stars, menus and other components, but we do not apply the visual design. The final stage is the interface design, which we do only after the wireframes have been validated. This is where we apply colors and the typeface, producing the screen as the end user will see it in an app or on a website. In the comparison, the simple design already has one-pixel gray borders around image boxes, orange buttons and the chosen typeface.

In a typical project, we first sketch by hand, then make high- or low-fidelity wireframes. Sometimes we make both, first low-fidelity and then high-fidelity, but not always; we will return to that. Finally, we make the mockups (*maquetas visuales*) or interface design.

A sketch is the first hand-drawn outline of the screens for our digital product. It broadly shows the first ideas and options for those screens.

## Why sketch before designing

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L25 · 05:00–06:03

Sketching lets us work very quickly and show or iterate on versions quickly. When we have spent hours making a high-fidelity wireframe or a designed screen, we become attached to that work and it is harder to make changes. With a sketch we can iterate in seconds rather than minutes. It keeps us focused on the design solution, without spending our attention on alignment, spacing or color. A co-design exercise such as Crazy 8s can involve any member of the team. You do not need to be a professional designer or illustrator to draw screens at this level, and you are less attached to the result.

The drawbacks are that other people may find sketches hard to understand. If you show them to potential users, someone on the team, your boss or clients, make them clear enough to follow, but do not spend too much time on them. Sketches also make it hard to show interface interactions, such as how a menu behaves. It takes time to draw a photo or write out content and text, so showing images and content is difficult.

## Wireframes as the product’s skeleton

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L25 · 06:03–06:59

I recommend always sketching before making screens. Wireframes are the skeleton of the design: they have low visual fidelity, represent the interface in grayscale and do not take much time on the look or aesthetics.

Here are high-fidelity wireframes made by my partner Samuel. They are high-fidelity because they contain text, but still need the design layer: the kind and color of buttons, the typeface, images and descriptions. At this level, we can already see clearly how the website will look once that design layer is added. Wireframes let us see quickly and simply how the different components of our product will be organized, and save us a lot of design time and therefore money.

## Low-fidelity wireframes

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L25 · 06:59–08:20

Low-fidelity wireframes represent a website with boxes and lines. Text is generally drawn with gray lines, with wider or darker lines or boxes for titles. Sometimes we add a title to make the sections clearer.

This is an example from our redesign of the Metrovacesa property website (captions: *MetroBaseso en el mobiliaria*). The low-fidelity version has featured promotions and promotions for the latest homes. If you are used to Photoshop, Sketch or Figma—which we use—you may think you work much faster by drawing directly in your design tool. Always start by drawing by hand, then transfer the sketches into the design tool. Even when wireframes are low-fidelity, they can have consistent spacing and alignment. If you begin there, you are already spending attention on those details. That is why it is so important to draw by hand first and move into wireframes afterward.

## A technology consultancy website

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L25 · 08:20–10:19

In another example, I made wireframes for a client that is a small technology consultancy. We first drew the wireframes by hand, then made these low-fidelity wireframes and went directly to design, without making the high-fidelity wireframes we will see later.

![1490 · U7 · L25 · 08:50.20](frames/U07L25-F0850.20.jpg)

On screen: "HOME"  
Shows: A composite of low-fidelity page layouts for the consultancy website, including a home page alongside service, project and article layouts.

The home page has an illustration or photograph, a headline and a short description of what the company does. It shows the clients, case studies and other sections, and ends with contact information, a phone number and a map of the office location. The services page shows the services, a feature about how the company works, and its technologies, such as JavaScript and iOS. It also shows the clients they work with again because those well-known clients were important to the company and they wanted to show them on almost every page.

The projects page has a header, another illustration, the projects, the clients again and a final contact module. At the bottom there is a footer and a chat in case you want to talk to me. The project-detail page has a photo and an illustration of the designed screens, followed by text about the case study and technologies, more text and images, and a final contact module.
