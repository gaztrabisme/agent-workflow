## Contextual observation

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L11 · 00:00–01:57

This lesson introduces other qualitative methods, including contextual research and testing. The teacher also says he will explain why he does not recommend focus groups.

![1490 · U3 · L11 · 00:08](frames/U03L11-F0008.00.jpg)

On screen: "ÓSCAR SANTOS" / "Otras técnicas cualitativas: investigación contextual y testing" [Other qualitative techniques: contextual research and testing] [rest omitted]  
Shows: Lesson title card with a person holding a mobile phone.

Contextual observation (*observación contextual*) is a qualitative method in which we interview and observe a user in their real context, instead of in a usability lab or an artificial setting. We accompany the user and observe how they interact with products and services. We need to get out of the building (*salir del edificio*) to obtain real information and avoid preconceived ideas that would make us design for what we think is happening instead of what is really happening. The aim is information that lets us design effectively. We need to understand users’ characteristics, the tasks they do, and their values, goals, and difficulties.

To conduct contextual observation, first define the scope (*alcance*) of what we want to observe and identify the users. Plan sessions with them and, possibly, with the people responsible for their work. As with an interview, prepare a short guide. Take notes as users do their tasks or use the product. Afterwards, gather the notes and analyze the results: look for recurring characteristics or patterns and information that can help the research. The unit resources include a video from Jakob Nielsen’s team about why it is important to leave the office to find design ideas and identify users’ problems.

## What user tests measure

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L11 · 01:57–03:08

A usability test (*test de usabilidad*) checks whether interfaces and interaction processes are well designed, and whether users encounter obstacles when trying to reach their goals. By contrast, a test with users is a way to collect qualitative information, mainly to understand how and why users use a product. Usability and user experience (*experiencia de usuario*) are different. Usability tells us whether people can use the app, find what they need, and make the app work for them (the captions render the final phrase as “se encuentran las cosas o se las hagan utilizar”). User experience concerns the experience with the product and asks whether users need the app.

Usability tests help identify different ways to complete a task, make tasks easy and intuitive, find product problems, reduce steps, and remove roadblocks. They also show what users do and how they do it. User-experience tests help us understand the product’s perceived value, whether users feel an emotional connection to the product or brand, why they take particular actions, what they value, what they miss, and how they feel while using the product.

## Usability labs and how many tests to run

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L11 · 03:08–04:45

A typical usability lab has usability and user-experience experts observing a participant as she interacts with an app, with cameras watching her tasks. Most studios cannot afford a lab. The teacher says that this formal setup does not follow Lean principles or “get out of the building”: it requires an artificial environment, a dedicated room, and expensive equipment such as special cameras and eye-tracking devices. The tests are slower and more expensive to organize, and testing many users adds work.

Consultant Steve Krug (the captions read “Steve Krag”) says that testing with one user is 100% better than not testing with anyone. Krug is known for *Don’t Make Me Think*, a book about web and mobile usability. Although it is old, the teacher recommends studying it to see what websites looked like years ago.

How many tests should we run? The teacher says that studies have shown five tests are enough. He recommends five tests each week rather than one session of 50 tests in a row. Those five tests should reveal 80% of the usability problems we will encounter.

## Informal guerrilla tests

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L11 · 04:45–06:26

Since lab testing is expensive, resource-heavy, and needs a dedicated space, we can do guerrilla user testing (*test de usuario de guerrilla*) instead, like the woman in the photograph. Offering a coffee in exchange for help may not be common in Madrid, although the teacher says it seems to be used elsewhere. We should try to reward people who help with our projects. The first three tests can feel awkward, but that is normal; they will still give us useful information.

Guerrilla or informal tests are cheap, require little effort, and give us a lot of direct information by watching users. They fit very short project deadlines and can be run the same day without a room or materials. They can also be used for tests with large groups. We can hold sessions of three to five users every week rather than one very long session with many tests; repeating them over time brings more value. The teacher points to a Jakob Nielsen video about the importance of testing. Nielsen says we are not the same as users because we work in technology, tests are inexpensive, and results can help convince managers, clients, or stakeholders. The teacher disagrees with Nielsen about who must participate: for a guerrilla test focused on basic usability, such as whether an app is understandable, he believes we can test with anyone, even someone from the office, rather than only the people who will use it.

## Preparing and running a test

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L11 · 06:26–09:33

Always prepare a guide for a test, just as for an interview. We do not need to read it aloud, but we should know what we will discuss and which tasks we want the user to do in the product, app, or prototype. The teacher recommends about five tasks—three to seven, around five.

The guide begins with an introduction: thank the participant and explain that the test is intended to improve an app, not to evaluate the participant. Emphasize this, especially when testing employees inside a company, because they may think they are being examined. Briefly explain the project’s value proposition. In the example, the app lets people save information about books, comics, and magazines, write reviews, and read other users’ summaries and highlights. Explain the project’s hypothesis too: the app might help readers track what they read and choose future reading. The test should validate whether this would be useful before we spend time and resources on something people may not use.

Explain how the test works, including the number of tasks and approximate duration. A participant may feel overwhelmed or tired if tasks keep appearing one after another, so always state the number of tasks and how many minutes they will take. Ask the participant to think aloud (*pensar en alto*; the captions misread *pensar* as *pesar*, “to weigh”) and give an example: “I think I’ll press this button because it will take me to the next page.” The teacher calls this *think aloud*. An example makes it easier to think aloud while using a prototype or app. The teacher also likes to remind participants at the end that they are not being judged, and to keep the atmosphere relaxed and fun.

Give the participant the task list in print or on another screen so they can check what to do and see what remains. You can present tasks one at a time, but the teacher recommends letting the participant see the list. Include a prototype link and test the prototype beforehand. He recalls his first professional test, with about 15 people: he had not tested the prototype or the credit card he needed to use, so he could not complete it and had to cancel the test. It was a disaster. Always test the test first.

## Recording test results

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L11 · 09:33–10:48

The teacher’s Notion page includes a table for task results. Tasks form the first column, and the testers appear in the other columns. Record each participant’s name, age, and profession, since these details may indicate their familiarity with technology or whether they grew up with the internet. Number the tasks as in the guide. Use colors to show how each task went: green means it was completed on the first try; yellow or orange means it was difficult; red means the participant could not do it. If someone gets stuck, say, “That’s okay, I’ll tell you the next task.”

The example has a row for user comments. Catalina says the app seems intuitive, she likes being able to filter comments, and she would need onboarding screens the first time she opens the app. There is also a column for the researcher’s comments. When possible, run the test with two people: one records information and observes the participant; the other interacts with them. The unit resources include the template to make it easier to start testing.

## Learn from the findings and improve the design

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L11 · 10:48–13:02

Before testing, define the objectives and type of test. It may ask whether people perceive the value proposition or need the product, or whether they understand how to use it. Have the product or a prototype ready—even a paper prototype—and design about five tasks, no more. An optional interview can follow the test. Select and recruit users; they might be colleagues in another department or a family member who fits the audience, and they do not have to be a perfect match. Prepare the space and materials. A test can take place at home, in the office, or in a café. Test the whole procedure with a colleague or yourself before inviting recruited users, to make sure everything works.

The teacher recommends sessions of five users, held close together—for example, five tests every Friday. Some companies, such as Meetup, have the CEO test products every day. Meetup is a network for organizing gatherings, which the course will discuss in its final lesson. After testing, analyze the results and propose design improvements that address the problems in the proposal.

Bill Gates’s advice is that our most unhappy customers are our greatest source of learning. The teacher says designers should remember this when they become fixed on a proposal that a client has changed. Criticism, as long as it is not destructive, can help improve the product. We should learn from clients whenever possible. The next lesson explains how to organize all this information for the best result.
