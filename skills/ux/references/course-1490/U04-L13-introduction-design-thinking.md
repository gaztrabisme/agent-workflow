## Design Thinking connects user needs, feasibility, and collaboration

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–01:25

In this lesson we will introduce Design Thinking, or design thinking (*pensamiento de diseño*), and its working process. We will also look at user-centered design.

Design Thinking is a method for generating innovative ideas. Its effectiveness comes from understanding and addressing users’ real needs. Tim Brown of IDEO describes it as the intersection of people’s needs, technical feasibility (whether something can be made), and business viability—the usual business test. On one side is the user; on the other is the business; and there is also feasibility: whether it can be done and whether it is too complicated.

Design Thinking is interdisciplinary: people from different departments contribute. It is collaborative: we use co-creation (*co-creación*) exercises to search for different solutions. It is experimental: we make many prototypes (*prototipos*) to find the best solution. Prototypes help us validate our hypotheses, but they do not have to be physical. A storyboard, a scenario, or even an improvised performance can work. The idea is to test our concepts or solutions. The more prototypes we make, the better.

## The process moves between stages

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L13 · 01:25–02:15

The Design Thinking process begins with empathizing (*empatizar*): getting to know users and putting ourselves in their position. Then we define the problem. After that we look for ideas for possible solutions. We make prototypes from those ideas; a prototype can be anything, as noted above. Finally, we test and learn from users’ feedback and opinions. That is the Design Thinking process.

As I said earlier about defining or designing a product, this is not a linear process. We can move from one part to another. During tests we may return to ideation or to empathy. We do not have to follow a straight line. Depending on the project’s state and what we learn, we need to change our prototypes and move between stages.

## User-centered design starts with people

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L13 · 02:15–02:50

User-Centered Design (*diseño centrado en el usuario*) aims to create products that meet end users’ specific needs, giving them the greatest satisfaction and best experience with the least effort. We must always put the user at the center of our work. It is a broad approach to design in which the process is guided by information we gather from users, or from possible users when the product does not yet exist. This approach does not come from technology.

## Henry Dreyfuss redesigned a telephone around its users

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L13 · 02:50–04:04

Here you can see the Western Electric Model 500 (1949), as identified on the slide (the teacher calls it “a 1928 Bell System telephone” [02:45]). He says it was used in the United States and, he thinks, in part of the world [02:45–02:51]. Henry Dreyfuss (*Dreyfus* in the captions) redesigned this telephone. He is one of the most important designers in history, perhaps the most important designer in the history of humanity; this telephone can still be seen in many homes.

When Dreyfuss redesigned it, he researched users and kept thinking about them. He made the cord extend like a spring so that the phone would not fall when someone moved, and made it long enough to allow movement. The supports that hold the handset prevent the call from ending if the phone tips over. The semi-rectangular body keeps the phone from turning when you let it go. He also had to research how people hold the handset: its shape is not circular, so it fits the hand better. He calculated the distance between the microphone and the earpiece using the average distances measured across more than 2,000 faces. For durability, he put the numbers and letters in recessed relief and outside the area worn down by the finger used to dial.

When I see this phone and then my iPhone, which falls on the floor and breaks because it is made of glass, I do not understand it. This is user-centered design.

## Users work in imperfect conditions

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L13 · 04:16–05:04

You may know the game Lemmings. In it, you have to guide the little creatures that fall from the door at the upper left to a truncated-pyramid shape on the right. You have different tools to stop the lemmings from dying constantly, as they are doing now.

We should not think of users as lemmings, but we should remember that we do not always use digital products in ideal conditions. Sometimes we use an app on the subway while talking to a friend. Other times we use a design program while having coffee. In general, we should not make users think hard. That is why we need user-centered design: we must study their needs and the environment they are in. I recommend trying this game with an emulator.
