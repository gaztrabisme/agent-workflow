## What a paper prototype is

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L29 · 00:00–01:15

In this lesson, we will learn how to make paper prototypes to show and test our idea as soon as possible. I’ll explain how.

A prototype (*prototipo*) is a medium- to high-fidelity representation that includes or simulates interaction with an interface. With this representation, users can experience the use of the product to some extent. That is the classic definition of a prototype. But we are going to make paper prototypes to validate our value proposition (*propuesta de valor*).

We will use a very simple, free tool called POP, available for both Android and iOS. By photographing our screen designs, we can add interactivity between them. Using POP is simple: we take photos of our interface, the drawings from our sketching session, and our proposed solution. Then we link the different screens with interactive areas (*hotspots*): we draw them over the parts that are interactive. We only have to test the prototype to make sure everything is linked correctly. Then we can use it with users or potential users who are interested in our digital product.

## Validate the reading-app idea

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L29 · 01:15–03:40

This is a proof of concept (*prueba de concepto*). A proof of concept uses qualitative and quantitative methods—interviews, surveys, and prototypes—to evaluate how well a new product idea will be accepted in the market. We do not need medium or high fidelity. We simply want people to understand what we plan to develop. If the idea does not make sense, there is no point in drawing high-fidelity wireframes or designs for a project that may never see the light of day.

Here are three screens we made for our reading-app prototype. We included a little text so people could understand them, and they are drawn by hand. One screen has something like Instagram Stories, where people share phrases or say how they are getting on with the books they are reading. Then there are news items, including interviews with writers or users, and updates from our friends.

In this example, Fede from Elastic Heads has underlined a couple of phrases in the last book he is reading on his Kindle. You can also see the menu at the bottom, the navigation bar (*navbar*), with the sections of our app.

In the end, I chose a home screen, a section for discovering new books, and a button—the most important button—for adding the book you are currently reading. We want users to add books, especially when they first start using this app. Then there is a Readings section with what I am reading right now and my library, and an area for the user.

Here is part of the Readings screen, showing the book we are reading now: Lean UX (captions: *el Injuke*). There are notes we can underline in the book, or things we want to write down or highlight. We can see which other users are reading it; it looks as if Samuel is reading it too. Then there are the platform users’ opinions.

In the last screen, you can see the profile: photo, name, username, and a timeline we saw in the references. It lets us see this person’s activity as a user.

Here you can see how the prototype behaves. It is made in POP, so there is no scrolling; we can simply move from one screen to another. We go to Readings, then to the library, where we see that the person has read three books, and then to the book notes and who else is reading it. It is a very simple app, it works quickly, and it is more than valid for a first idea.

## Link the paper screens in Marvel

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L29 · 03:40–09:29

The POP app is easy to use, but it has one problem: we cannot keep its navigation bar fixed at the bottom. If you want to keep that bar in place, you can do it with Marvel, which is like POP’s parent, so to speak. The company bought POP, and the tools are very similar.

Let’s see how Marvel works. We would sign in and create a free project with a free account. I will open the project; it is empty and always shows this blank screen. We have not designed screens in Sketch for this example. We could try some sample images included for prototyping, but because we drew our prototype by hand, we will click Add Images. Marvel lets us design directly in the tool or upload our drawings.

I recommend numbering your image files and giving them descriptive names so it is easier to link them in the tool. We select them all and click Open, and Marvel imports them. We can add more images or preview the prototype if it is already made. Let’s open what will be our home screen and choose Prototype. As in POP, we can click to create links between screens.

The first thing I will do is set the footer to fixed so the navigation bar stays visible when we scroll. Let’s preview it. Now the navigation bar stays put. Since it is drawn by hand, it feels a little more like an app; if the menu is not shown, it would be harder to understand. We can close the preview and make hotspots. For example, we can make the image start at Discover, and add the different sections for Readings and User.

We can preview the prototype at any time. We can scroll and try to go to the user area. Here is the user screen. Because we have not fixed the navigation bar, it disappears when we scroll. This is the result of the preview. Now we can close it because we have finished the first screen.

We will open it again and add links. I recommend making the boxes large, because if the area for tapping is too small, it might not work well. Then this would be the Discover screen. Besides the main navigation, we can add secondary navigation. In Recommendations, for example, we can show books to discover and the books or types of reading we follow. I will add another hotspot for Following. We have to do the same thing at the bottom and add the different hotspots if we want to navigate from here. Then it would go to Home. We can preview it to see whether our tabs work.

As you can see, the fidelity is minimal; it is hand-drawn. When we show this idea, we want to get information and hear what users or readers think before we even make a wireframe. Many of these features may later be removed.

## Test the prototype and keep it simple

Anchor: 1490 · U7 · L29 · 09:29–12:09

Once we have made all the interactions between screens, we test the prototype. We can navigate among the sections. We have not built the Add Books feature yet; we only want to see the app in general. In Discover, the tabs work. We need to check Recommendations and Popular, the topics and people I follow, and the books I share. In Readings, there is the book I am reading now and my library, which has three books.

To scroll, we need the version that supports scrolling. That is the one thing that differs on a computer: it will not let us create this kind of scrolling while keeping the menu fixed. The prototype works well for us and is easy to use anywhere, including on a computer. I recommend trying POP, and if you want to go further, trying Marvel. It looks quite good even when it is drawn quickly by hand, and it lets us show users the features in our digital product and hear what they think.

Here are a few recommendations for prototyping. First, include only the necessary interactions. We do not need every button and link to work. We only need the main interactions to show the main paths users will take. Otherwise, we waste a lot of time and turn the prototype into a maze. Second, design all the screens before making the prototype, so you save time and work in stages. Finally, test the prototype many times before showing it to users or running a test. It may seem obvious, but we have gone to usability tests—even with high-fidelity prototypes—only to find that the prototype was wrong and have to edit it while people were waiting. Always test the prototype before a test.

There are hundreds, if not thousands, of tools for making prototypes. In this course, we have used Marvel, one of the simplest; we do not need much more for a prototype. For a long time we used InVision, which imports hand-drawn sketches or images from Figma, or whatever program you use. We also made prototypes directly in Sketch. We have moved away from InVision, and now we do everything in Figma: all the wireframes, the interface design, and the prototypes we use to validate with users.
