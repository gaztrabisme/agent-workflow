## Why UX portfolios need success stories

Anchor: 1490 · U8 · L31 · 00:00–00:51

In this lesson, we will look at why it is important to document our design process and what a success story needs in order to tell that story well. If you speak with any professional, they will recommend writing success stories or case studies (*casos de estudio*) about your user experience (UX; *experiencia de usuario*) projects. A portfolio with images that do not show the process or context of our work no longer works. That might have worked for the old advertising-agency model, but not if we want to work in UX. Being able to show these case studies can be the difference in whether someone hires you as a UX designer.

## Build a success story around context, process, and results

Anchor: 1490 · U8 · L31 · 00:51–02:31

Here is an outline; let’s look at it more closely. Start with a short introduction that puts the company or product in context for the reader. Then state the problem: what are we going to solve with this project? Define the likely user or audience. In many cases, we will not do the project alone, so describe each team member’s role and skills.

A point people often forget is the scope (*alcance*) and the limitations. Having one or two weeks for a project is different from having three months. The most important part of the case study is showing the work process: explain which exercises we did, what we learned from them, and what they helped us do. Finally, describe the results: if it is a real product, how it affected users, and what we learned from the project.

A shorter version would start with the company’s challenge and the roles and skills of each team member. Then describe the research findings and the opportunities for design, including the problems or pain points (*pain point*) we addressed. Present the solutions for those pain points, along with the prototypes and user testing (*testing con usuarios*) that helped us redefine and improve our value proposition (*propuesta de valor*). Then show the final solution. If the project includes interface design, we can also include the screens. Finish with conclusions and what we learned from the project.

## Explain decisions, mistakes, and pivots

Anchor: 1490 · U8 · L31 · 02:31–03:07

One thing I think is very important, and that people often leave out when they describe a process, is mentioning the mistakes and blockers, how they solved them, and whether they had to change the project idea. When hiring someone, we want to know whether the candidate understands why they chose one exercise over another. The internet is full of UX case studies that show a sequence of exercises and a final design without any reasoning. I recommend that you do not simply repeat a process like a parrot: be critical. Some exercises may have helped you; others may only have wasted your time. Including that in your case study is valuable.

## Show fewer projects with more depth

Anchor: 1490 · U8 · L31 · 04:00–04:47

When you show your work, quality is better than quantity. If you come from graphic design, you may have many corporate-identity pieces, posters, and logos. It is better to remove them or move them into the background. Aim to show three, five, or seven success stories, and explain each one in detail: show the process and the work behind it before showing the interface design.

Your success story, like the other case studies in this course, should show what exercises you did and why you did them. These stories justify our work and the value we add to the project. They show the most valuable part of design, which cannot be seen in the interface design. They also help someone who is hiring you make an informed decision.

## Tools for publishing a success story

Anchor: 1490 · U8 · L31 · 04:47–06:04

Here are four resources that can help you make a project case study. Bestfolios collects portfolios from different kinds of designers and has a user-experience category that I recommend studying. Next is UXfolio (captions: *Juxfolio*), a set of prebuilt pages, like WordPress, where you can post a success story without designing or programming anything; it has a free version.

The third resource, which we at Elastic Heads (captions: *Elastic Hedge*) love, is Webflow. It lets us make websites without writing a single line of code. Finally, the leanest and most minimalist option is to make your case study on a Notion page. I recommend looking at UXfolio, which has a blog section with interesting posts about creating a résumé or success story. I am sharing a Notion template in the unit’s resources so you can make your case-study template as quickly as possible. Now that you know how to document your project through a success story, in the next lesson I will give you some final advice so you can keep moving forward in digital product design.
