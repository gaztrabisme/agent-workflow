## Use user personas to validate design decisions

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–01:04

A user persona is one of the most useful tools for empathizing with users. I will show you how to make one as quickly as possible. User personas—or “dry personas,” as we call them in the studio—are archetypes (*arquetipos*) or fictional characters representing possible users of a product or service. They help us validate each design decision. We base them on behavior patterns we identified in research, and they help define the product’s characteristics from a user-centered design approach.

We use personas constantly when making decisions, so they give us early validation. Whenever I release a feature or design screens, I check them against the persona by asking: would this person use this kind of design? Would they find it attractive or useful?

## Why the detailed persona examples do not work for this exercise

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L14 · 01:04–02:34

Here is a user-persona example I found on Dribbble. It has a photo representing the target audience; the profile is called Modern George (captions: *Modern Yorke*). It includes demographic details and technology knowledge: this person knows a great deal about the internet and social media. It also lists habits, influences, personality, and brands. I would not make a persona this way because it is designed in a way that only a designer could produce. Design Thinking requires us to work collaboratively, so a programmer or someone in the business team might not be able to make this persona. It does not work for me.

Here is another example, which anyone could make. It is called Nya (captions: *Mía*). It includes demographic details, personality, activities, needs and goals, frustrations, behavior and attitudes, motivations, and favorite brands. This one is better, but it still will not work for us. It has too much text, and our exercises and deliverables need to be easy to consume. Here are two more examples with the same problem: too much design and text. That is a waste of time. Remove the titles and colors; this is not what we need.

A proto-persona (*protopersona*) is a reduced version of a user persona. It helps us identify the characteristics our product needs, based on users’ needs, and form hypotheses about the product or service so we can validate them through experiments and prototypes. Even if you find it awful, this is the version we will make: no design, just the basic information we need for the project.

## Build a simple persona from a project’s needs

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L14 · 02:34–04:44

This example comes from Jeff Gothelf and Josh Seiden’s book *Lean UX* (captions: *Jeff Goddard, Link UX*). First, find an image or make a drawing, then give the person a name and a nickname. The nickname helps us remember the persona: many people are called Mary, but “Mary, the stressed mom” points us to this particular proto-persona.

Next, write demographic details. All the information in this exercise should come from research. But Jeff Gothelf says that if we have not yet done interviews or received survey results, we can invent the details and update them easily once the research is available.

Mary, the stressed mom, is a working mother, 34 years old. She lives in Reading, works in London, is married, has two children, and has an average income. In the behavior quadrant, a household employee helps with the household chores (captions: *tallas del hogar*); Mary buys takeout three nights a week and feels overwhelmed when she forgets something. She seems to be very busy. In the needs and goals quadrant, she needs help with errands, the children, and keeping things running. She wants to spend time with her friends, feel that she has everything sorted out, and be able to clone herself.

When we make a proto-persona for a product, its behavior, needs, and goals must relate to that project. Mary’s example would not fit our reading app. It might fit a productivity app, a food-delivery website, or a babysitter social network. In summary, the top-left quadrant has an image, a name, and a nickname. The other three quadrants hold demographic details, behavior, and needs and goals related to our project—not things like needing to buy a car or a house. These proto-personas may look awful, but they are perfect for giving us the basic information we need to validate design proposals.

## Keep personas brief, visible, and connected to the product

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L14 · 04:44–06:13

One recommendation from Elastic Heads is to use a thick marker so that the persona can be read from a distance by anyone who walks by, without stopping. Summarize as much as possible and always use the fewest words you can.

Here is an example we made in Mural for an app where people can upload dishes from their favorite bars. The persona is Oscar “El Bravero.” The board includes a nickname and something he might say. He is an early adopter who likes technology. His behaviors include caring about what people think, wanting to live from his passion, and being proactive. His needs include having a place to stand out. The social network might give him that opportunity: he wants to connect with an audience of fans of patatas bravas and to monetize his content.

This other example is for a university website. We used a photograph so that the persona would not look too childish. We included a quotation, demographic details such as age and income, behavior drawn mainly from interviews, and the person’s goals and needs for the university site. In this case the goal was to enroll in a course. We can change and update the design from one model to another, but each persona needs a name and nickname, demographic details, behavior, and goals and needs related to our digital product.

## Make a reading-app proto-persona with the whole team

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L14 · 06:13–07:28

For our project, we are making a reading app to manage the books you own, your highlights, who you lend books to, reading lists, and more. We will make one proto-persona for the book app: Mila, or “Super-reader in her free time.” We give her a drawing. She works in an office, is 52, lives in a city, is separated, has two children, and has an average income.

Mila reads every day when she goes to bed. She likes reading on paper or using a Kindle for free. She reads recommendations and trusts books that other people recommend. Her needs and goals are to write recommendations and rate other people’s, get recommendations for new novels, read more books, and keep a reading journal so she can see whether she is reading more or fewer pages.

Anyone on the design team can do this exercise—a programmer, the CEO, or anyone else. Make everyone draw the character. If we made it by hand and need to present it to a client or another department, we can clean it up. This is the same proto-persona, cleaned up; the photograph also helps her seem more like a real person.

## Proto-personas are quick to make and update

Anchor: 1490 · U4 · L14 · 07:28–08:17

Proto-personas can be made before research results are ready. Because they are easy to make, we can update them quickly at any time. We do not need design skills to create them, so we work faster. They also help us work in a user-centered way. We can use notes, markers, and paper, or tools such as Mural, Miro, Figma, or Sketch—whichever you know how to use (captions: *sigma sketch*). In the resources section of this unit I will share our template.

Now that you can identify users’ goals and needs with a proto-persona, the next lesson will show you how to make empathy maps.
