## Desk research and this lesson’s route

In this lesson we’ll learn about desk research (the captions render the term as “The Research”), or researching without going out to conduct fieldwork. We’ll search for articles and news online that can help with our project. We’ll also learn to make a reference document that helps us identify patterns and good practices for our project.

## What desk research can tell us

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L08 · 00:33–01:45

Desk research, also called secondary research (*investigación secundaria*), means collecting information from studies and research that have already been conducted and published. It is very useful at the beginning of a project because it lets us identify insights or discoveries about the topic we need to research.

This type of research is much more cost-effective because it uses existing data we can get from studies and news found online. We can look for scientific studies published on the internet, news, books, or essays on a particular topic.

To find scientific studies, you can search ResearchGate and Academia, two sites that collect scientific studies. You will find paid studies. Sometimes it is worth paying a little to save a lot of work and time on the project. In the unit resources I’ll share a completely legal plugin so you can download paid studies completely free. Something very important that I want to emphasize is that secondary research will never replace the primary research we do with users.

## Making a reference document

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L08 · 01:45–03:14

A reference document (*documento de referencias*) helps us collect ideas for our project. We include images of other digital products and comment on them. The aim is to gather ideas, features, and solutions, then combine them to make a completely new product. Basically, we paste screenshots of websites or apps and comment on ideas that might be useful for our project.

In the example I’m showing you, we were redesigning the website of a famous motorcycle brand. We do not have to analyze only competitors: we can include images of products that have nothing to do with our activity if we can draw ideas from them for our project.

The size of the reference document depends on the project, its budget, and the time we have. At the end of the document, we include conclusions with the main ideas we want to highlight. You can adapt the document to whatever format you want or need. Everyone on the team should help make it, and we should always share it with our clients. When presenting it, tell them only the conclusions; there is no need to explain every page and everything we saw.

It is important to limit the number of references we study. If we work in a team of four, we could each analyze the three best ones we find. In this unit’s resources, you can find examples of a template showing how we do it. It is important to use a program that lets us work collaboratively. I recommend Google Slides or Keynote.

## Conclusions from the references

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L08 · 10:00–11:12

Now let’s look at the conclusions from the references. This is what we should present to the different stakeholders or to our client. Here are the features and functionalities for our reading app that seem interesting.

People could write ratings and reviews. They could read Kindle highlights or write them by hand, so that they do not lose the highlights they made in UX books or books they want to learn from. They could rate and comment on reviews, and make book lists created by users or important public figures. Other possibilities are reading challenges, group reading competitions, a feed with status updates, challenges and news, Kindle information, reading progress, and book pages with highlights and ratings.

We could even add a quiz—a contest-style questionnaire—to see if you understood what you read. We could offer seasonal book selections and track progress. A reading planner could let people set goals, with charts showing how many books they have read, their reading pace, and so on.

There are many features that we will have to filter, keeping only the ones that really add value to our product. Later, if we are successful, we can see which ones to add.

## Design recommendations and closing

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L08 · 11:12–12:11

Finally, here are a few recommendations. We would like a minimalist design that does not overload the screens the way Goodreads can. Some users may find that prevents them from using the app smoothly. Use a single column to make it feel simple and easy to use. Do not put more than three or four icons in the navigation bar. Use neutral colors and white for the background, and create contrast. Improve legibility and color hierarchy, among other things. These are recommendations and ideas to use when carrying out our design.

Finally, we always include a thank-you for taking the time to read our documentation. I hope this helps you make your reference document. If you have questions, just write in the course forum. You have seen how many ideas we can get from a reference document. In the next lesson, we’ll learn a quantitative research technique with users: surveys.

### Key points (derived)

- Secondary research supplies published evidence, while a reference document helps a team compare products and collect ideas for its own design.
- Study enough references to see patterns, then present conclusions and prioritize only the features that add value.
- The reading-app examples suggest features around discovery, collections, highlights, reviews, progress, and lending; validate ideas with users before committing to them.
