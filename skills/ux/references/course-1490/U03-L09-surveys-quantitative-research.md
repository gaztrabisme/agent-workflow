## Survey research and two types of research

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:25

In this lesson, we’ll learn how to make surveys using the Lean Survey Canvas (the captions render its name as “LeanSarvy Canvas”). We’ll look at its advantages and drawbacks, learn how to use the tool, and I’ll recommend platforms for hosting surveys.

We’ll distinguish between two types of research. Quantitative research, as its name suggests, is based on quantity. Qualitative research is based more on the quality of the information we obtain.

Here is an example. In the first panel, a researcher is hidden behind a bush and counts the people who take free ice cream. This is quantitative research: it gives us the exact number of ice creams people take (the captions read “lados”). In the panel on the right, the same researcher interviews someone who took the ice cream and asks how they felt while taking it. This kind of research, an interview, is qualitative. Quantitative research gives us statistical data, while qualitative research lets us go deeper and get higher-quality information.

## The three parts of a survey and what numbers can tell us

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L09 · 01:25–02:20

A survey has three fundamental parts. First are the questions. In this case, we asked users how they preferred us to present information about a battery: in more detail, or by explaining the benefits or features in a way users could understand.

Next are the responses. Most people preferred a simple way of putting it: the battery’s autonomy. The third part, which we must not forget, is the results and conclusions. We need to analyze all the results and write down the discoveries we made through the survey.

Surveys are useful when we want quantitative information about particular aspects of a product, service, or its users. Their drawback is that we cannot explore the information in depth. Numbers and statistics from quantitative methods such as questionnaires may show that a problem exists, but tell us little about its cause or how to solve it.

## Filling in the top half of the Lean Survey Canvas

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L09 · 02:20–04:02

Doing surveys can be difficult, so we can use the Lean Survey Canvas. It helps us create a survey that is easy to launch. The first quadrant to fill in is the upper left. There we state what we want to investigate. In our case, we want to know whether readers would use our app, what reading needs they have, and where and how they usually look for books.

I recommend putting no more than two or three sticky notes in this quadrant. If we put in too many, our survey may be weak because we will only ask users eight questions.

## Writing the questions and introducing the survey

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L09 · 04:02–05:48

If you are doing this as a group, I recommend giving everyone five minutes to write down every question they can think of. As we write each question, we need to compare it with the information above. Check that the question relates to the topic we are investigating, is aimed at the people we want to investigate, and asks for information we do not already have.

The first questions are often about the age or profession of the people taking the survey. In our case, we will ask directly about the project. The first question could be: How many hours a week do you spend reading? Next: What have you read during the past week? Another question could be: Do you think you read enough? If not, explain why.

We’ll ask why they read: for professional reasons or simply to pass the time. We’ll ask how they choose the books they read; this tells us whether they look for opinions or ask people they know. We’ll ask how they save parts that interest them: do they underline, take notes, or just keep what they remember in their minds?

Then we’ll show a list of features our app might have and ask users to rate their interest. We’ll finish with a question in case they can think of a feature we did not list. We’ll always thank them for helping with the survey.

We’ll also always write an introduction explaining the survey’s purpose: we are developing a new app, answering the survey will take no more than three minutes, and it will always be anonymous. As you can see, we’ll include about eight questions. If we include more, users may get bored and abandon the survey. You can use the template I’m sharing in this unit’s resources to start researching the users of your project.

## Advantages and limitations of surveys

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L09 · 05:48–06:25

Surveys and forms are great for obtaining quantitative data. They are easy and inexpensive to make and take very little project time. We can get a lot of information quickly and make surveys for different types of users. With an asynchronous approach (the captions read “Asynchrona”), we only have to launch the survey and wait for people to answer.

On the other hand, if we word questions poorly, the data may be low quality and confuse our research. Another drawback is that we cannot explore the answers in depth. Wording can manipulate responses. We cannot know whether respondents completed the survey carefully or answered just to get through it, and numerical results are very easy to misinterpret.

## Question types and response options

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L09 · 06:25–07:51

Now let’s look at types of questions we can include. We can use open-ended questions, but I recommend using only one or two. Imagine that 800 people answer: “If you could change something about our product, what would you change?” We would have to analyze 800 different responses. This kind of question gives us valuable qualitative information, but, as I said, do not include many in your survey.

The questions we will use most are nominal questions, where people can select multiple answers. We can also use a Likert scale from one to five, where one is the lowest rating and five is the highest. We can also ask ordinal questions: for example, list the features of our product and have people say whether they are interested in them. Finally, we can ask closed yes-or-no questions.

I recommend adding the answer options when uploading the survey to a tool such as web forms. Remember to balance the ranges so that the number of possible answers stays manageable; do not, for example, offer ten answers. When we use numbers, label them—for example, one means “impossible” and five means “very easy.” This helps users choose a rating. Whenever you can, offer an “Other” response so people have an option if none of the answers we wrote fits.

An internet search will show you many tools for running an online survey. I recommend Google Forms (the captions say “Google Fonts”), SurveyMonkey, or Typeform. We use Google Forms because it is completely free. I’ll leave some examples in the unit resources to inspire you as you make your first survey.

### Key points (derived)

- Use the canvas to define the research topic, existing knowledge, target users, and outreach before drafting questions.
- Keep a survey focused at about eight questions, and make each question address a gap in what is already known.
- Surveys reveal quantitative patterns but cannot explain their causes; word questions carefully and keep response options balanced.
