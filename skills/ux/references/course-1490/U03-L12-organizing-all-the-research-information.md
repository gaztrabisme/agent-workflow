## From gathering information to redefining the problem

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L12 · 00:00–01:10

Now that we have a lot of information, it is time to organize it and discover what characteristics our product needs. Research is a two-step process. First we diverge (*divergir*): gather all the possible options and information—in other words, collect the data. Then we converge (*converger*): choose the best options, synthesize the data, and identify the real problem.

This is the first diamond in Design Thinking (*pensamiento de diseño*): investigate, then discover, until we redefine the actual problem. We apply this to the brief. All the information from the discovery phase should help us rewrite the brief around the real problems, rather than the problems the project stakeholders initially thought they had.

## Building an affinity diagram

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L12 · 01:10–03:41

An affinity diagram (*diagrama de afinidad*) categorizes research information by grouping related topics. It helps us see the whole research effort at a glance. A finished diagram has research findings on notes and titles for the groups.

Write each piece of research information on a separate sticky note or card, then put the notes on a wall or a large table because they usually take up a lot of space. We can use a different color for each source—for example, yellow for surveys and blue for interviews—so we can see whether sources are mixed or separate. The teacher says the exercise is richer when done with several people, though doing it alone is also useful. Put only one concept on each sticky note; do not put five bullet points on the same note.

Once all the information is on the wall, everyone moves the notes into similar groups in silence. A colleague may move a card you have placed; that is normal, and the group should not talk yet. A large group, such as one about content, can be divided into subgroups, but do not add titles yet. If the same information comes from multiple sources, stack those notes together. The example is a lack of good technology resources, with one note from a survey and another from interviews.

After placing the notes, discuss and agree on titles for each group and subgroup. The example titles include functionality, visual design, and the kinds of copy the product needs. These are the three steps: write the findings on notes and place them on a wall or large table; group similar notes silently; then name the groups and subgroups together.

## How the diagram supports the team

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L12 · 05:37–07:53

The affinity diagram helps the whole design team internalize the research, not just the researchers. Programmers and stakeholders can take more of the research in when they participate. The diagram also gives the team a visible map of all the information. We can put a wall of sticky notes where it remains visible through the project, or work remotely if we have little space. We can revisit a category whenever we need it instead of opening separate survey and interview results.

At Elastic Heads (the captions say “Elastic Head”), the team says that the categories become the main pillars or themes of the project. If one category is an improvement to information architecture, for example, that may be a major part of the work. The diagram also helps us connect findings from different sources. Users may report navigation problems in a usability test, and survey respondents may report getting lost on the website. Seeing the same result in different sources is useful.

For the exercise, the teacher suggests trying several tools. Mural and Miro are designed for Design Thinking and let us add sticky notes online, work remotely, and keep the results saved in one place; they have free accounts. His studio uses Figma because it supports collaborative design and stakeholders can use it easily, so everything can be kept on the same platform. The best way, he says, is still sticky notes and markers on a wall. He recommends doing the exercise with stakeholders or clients so they can appreciate the value and the information gathered through research.

## Creating a research poster

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L12 · 07:53–08:54

A research poster (*research poster*, or *póster de investigación*) summarizes the most important parts of the research. The affinity diagram has grouped the information; the poster selects the most important findings. It is a small deliverable, even though Lean methods call for preparing as few deliverables as possible. A poster is useful because stakeholders or clients can share the research results inside the company, which gives value to the work.

## What to include in the research poster

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L12 · 08:54–10:04

Give the poster a title and date. List the categories and subcategories, which can come from the affinity diagram. Include information users gave in interviews because it carries weight. State the data sources, such as how many people answered surveys and how many interviews were conducted.

The university example surfaced serious problems. Course pricing was not communicated transparently, downloaded PDFs did not show the amount even after a person entered their details, and promotional-email checkboxes were compulsory. The team first presented these problems. It also proposed features seen on other platforms, such as buddy systems to accompany students, a chat with an adviser, and chatbots. Visual recommendations addressed text legibility and giving the page more room to breathe. The content recommendations were to use microcopy instead of a lot of text, avoid repeating the same content throughout the website, and use familiar, non-technical language. Finally, include what users said in the interviews; the teacher emphasizes how important this is.

## Making the poster useful

Anchor: 1490 · U3 · L12 · 10:04–11:01

Do not spend too much time on visual design: it can dazzle the reader and distract from what matters, which is the research content and what we learned. Work quickly, in keeping with Lean. Avoid jargon and use clear language so everyone can understand the poster, including marketing directors and the CEO. Put the most important discoveries at the top because many people will not read the whole poster. Choose a compatible format: PDF, image, or even a Word file. The next lesson introduces Design Thinking.
