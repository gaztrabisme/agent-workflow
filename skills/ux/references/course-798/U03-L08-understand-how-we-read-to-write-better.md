## How the eyes move when we read

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–03:42

I’m going to explain the science behind how we read. You may wonder why this matters to you, but to write well for your users, you first need to understand how their brains work while reading.

When you read, you do not simply take in one word and then the next in order. Your eyes jump from place to place. If you look at yourself in a mirror while reading, those movements can look a little unsettling. They are called saccades (*movimientos sacádicos*): jumps from one fixation point (*punto de fijación*) to another. We retain information during the fixation points, not during the saccades.

There are three areas to consider. Zone 1 is the first letters of a word: that is the first thing the eyes see and the brain receives. Zone 2 is the rest of the letters in that word, plus part of the word to its right; our eyes and brain are already dividing the text into pieces. Zone 3 is the next part of the text.

In a diagram of the reading process, each circle marks a fixation point, each straight line between circles marks a saccade, and arrows pointing back show regressions. You have probably experienced this: you read something, do not quite understand it, and have to go back and read it again. That is a problem because going back to ask yourself what the text is trying to explain increases cognitive load (*carga cognitiva*) and tires your brain. Doing it once or twice is not an issue. But imagine reading unclear language on a screen and having to go back repeatedly; eventually you get tired and leave.

That is why, as a UX writer, you need to understand how people read and apply the same patterns to your writing. Your brain joins information together and fills gaps. From about the age of nine, you may not read 30% of the text on a page or screen, yet your brain can predict the rest quite accurately. That is why you can read the example.

Keith Rayner and Alexander Pollatsek (captions: “Keith Reiner and Alexander Polastek”), in their book *The Psychology of Reading*, define reading as the ability to extract visual information from a page and understand the meaning of the text. Your eyes see shapes on a screen; your brain immediately takes in what is there and translates it without conscious effort. You are a simultaneous translation machine: letters are little shapes and strokes that your brain understands, gives context to, and deciphers. Understanding this process helps you design content that guides users more easily.

## What readers notice and how to make text easier

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L08 · 03:42–06:38

If you write, you have probably heard people say that readers do not read much, or that they do not read at all. The Nielsen Norman Group, a large UX consultancy, studied how much users read and found that people read only 20% to 28% of a web page. Keep this in mind when writing: make the most relevant information concise and current so people can use it. Cognitive or mental load rises by 11% for every 100 words.

When a person lands on a web page and sees a huge block, or wall, of text, the brain’s first reaction is that it is too much and will take effort. The brain is having to work and work. The more words there are, the harder it is for an average person to read.

There is a third obstacle: people tend to scan. In another study, the Nielsen Norman Group used heat maps to track how people read content and found what is called the F-shaped pattern (*patrón F*). People read headings, subheadings, and the first words of the rest of the content. You might wonder what the point is if people do not read, or why we should write at all. The answer is the opposite: if you understand the rules and science behind reading, you can use the patterns in your favor. If more content makes people more reluctant, break it into pieces. If they scan in an F pattern, put the most important information in headings, subheadings, and the first parts of the content. We can turn obstacles into potential benefits when we design content.

Look at the word “electroencephalographer” (*electroencefalografista*). It is hard to read because it is complex and not part of everyday language. Whenever you can, avoid that kind of wording in an interface. Use simple words and synonyms. Spanish has many ways to say things, so choose the simplest and most common option that fits your product. You want people to keep moving forward and understand what to do, while reducing stress and mental load.

Sarah Richards writes in *Content Design*: “It is not about lowering the level, but making things more accessible.” If you write in a way that is quick and easy to understand, people will understand it and more people will have access. That means more users. Simplify.

## Simplifying a welcome screen

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L08 · 06:38–08:38

Now I’ll show how I would apply simplification to the home screen for my taco reservation app in Figma. Looking at the current screen, I am already lost: there is too much text and too much information. Some of it may be important, but not all of it is relevant to someone who wants to book a table at a taco restaurant, especially in a welcome message.

First, create the boxes, as we saw earlier. Then reduce the number of words. One screen explains the action; in the other, I explain what does not work and what the person needs to do. The button should be clear and direct, and call the user to act with a verb. Fewer words also reduce mental load. Simplify the language and apply an F pattern to the screen. As you simplify, keep what the person really needs. If you have a lot to communicate, consider making subsections or splitting it across two screens. When you design the first screen or welcome for your final project, reduce the number of words, simplify the language, and apply an F pattern.

## Words are design material

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L08 · 08:38–10:20

Now that you know how the mind reads, these techniques can help when you write. Words, like pixels, are tools of design. People use both the visual elements and the words to understand what they can do. As a UX writer, your role is to guide them through words. The design of an experience also depends on words.

In his book *Writing for Designers*, Scott Kubie introduces the idea that words are also materials of design. I’ll include all the publication details in the additional resources. Product designers use pixels as their design material; UX writers use words. Words are a very powerful material: they can reduce complex concepts into very small spaces, especially on a mobile screen. They load extremely quickly, are easy to manipulate and transmit, and, best of all, do not need to be invented. They already exist; you only need to use them in the right context (captions: “en el contexto de Cuauhtémoc”). Just as with visuals, code, animations, and interactions, words need to make sense. When words are left until the end to fill boxes, instead of being considered from the beginning, a large disconnect can result.

## Error messages and writing early

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L08 · 10:20–12:04

You have probably seen examples of this disconnect. One error message says “Error 99-34-W” (captions: “Error 9934W”). Do you know what that means? I do not, and this is supposed to be a helpful error message. Another example says that memory could not be written. A third says “Error 438” and tells the user that an object does not support a property or method. What is the user supposed to do—look up error 438 in the manual? These messages do not guide the user or help them; they create more questions. In the end, I feel like the person in the image. A digital product can have the best code and a spectacular interface, but if users do not understand what to do, there is a problem.

Start working on content as early as possible. Speak with the people responsible for design so that the illustrations and text work together, tell the same story, and guide the user. Then people can come in, do what they need to do, and get on with their lives. That is why we are here: to help.
