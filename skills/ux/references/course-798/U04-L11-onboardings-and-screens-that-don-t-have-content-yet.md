## Bring words into the design from the start

Anchor: 798 · U4 · L11 · 00:00–01:14

All right, let’s get to work—fingers on the keyboard, however you want to put it. First I’ll explain how to write for an onboarding screen. Many design teams may never have worked with a UX writer, which is completely normal. They tend to solve design problems visually, without taking the words into account. At the start of the course, we talked about how lorem ipsum means that the words are overlooked and the designs break as a result.

As a UX writer, use these moments to guide and explain how the design team can bring words into the process from the beginning. The final copy does not have to be ready. Start thinking about the message at the same time as the layout and how to put the design together. Think about how the words, layout, and flow fit together (the captions read “Cuesta flujo”).

## What an onboarding should teach

Anchor: 798 · U4 · L11 · 01:14–03:22

Now let’s look at practical examples, starting with onboarding (*onboarding*). An onboarding welcomes people who are going to use an app for the first time. I know that the excitement of welcoming someone can make you want to tell them a lot, but you need to take it step by step. Too much information increases cognitive load (*carga mental*). Think about how to provide relevant information at the right time, at the right pace, and with the context in mind (the captions read “Hilton”).

A good onboarding focuses on the steps the user will follow in your app, so you can explain them gradually. Microcopy has a great deal of importance here: every word is considered and belongs in the right step. As a UX writer, you guide the user with those words. They are like a map and a welcome to your classroom (*aula*). If you do the work well, the onboarding should make three things clear. First, users should understand what the app is about and what they can do with it. Second, they should understand what value it offers and why it is different from other apps. Third—and it may seem more obvious, but it is sometimes more complicated—help them understand how to use your app (the captions read “agua”).

Remember that you are not the user or the person who will ultimately use the app (the captions read “al final de semana”). You have a great deal of information because you are inside the team, and sometimes you make a lot of assumptions. Share that information in a way that is easy to take in and understand, so people can start using your digital product or service.

## Build and prototype the onboarding

Anchor: 798 · U4 · L11 · 03:22–07:26

Let’s look at Figma and how you can apply this to your final project. I had quite a bit of information to share, so I thought about how to explain one easy-to-understand, easy-to-read piece of information on each screen. The first screen says hello and tells users that they can reserve a table at the taqueria. The button is clear: “Next.” The next screen asks them to choose a day and time, or one of the available options, and again the button says “Next.” The third screen tells them to enter their email and reserve. The fourth says that the reservation will be confirmed by email and asks them to start the reservation (the captions read “aturacer”). The captions read “IME” in this spoken line; the screen itself confirms the intended word, email.

The person has not started using the app yet, but these four steps make it clear what they can do. The first one welcomes them, says what the app helps with, explains how to use it, and makes a clear call to action. Together, the screens show the value the app offers (the captions read “¡Lab! Le aporta valor”).

The visual logic is the same as before: make boxes or rectangles (the captions read “Pujar cajas”), change their colors, use the text tool (the T) to add “Hello,” and add a button. In a few clicks you have a small screen. If your text is static, sharing it with the rest of the team can be difficult, so Figma also lets you make a simple prototype.

Make sure each screen is a frame. Select the whole group, right-click, and choose “Create frame from selection” (the captions read “Gracias”). Figma names it Frame 1; repeat this with the other screens until you have four frames. You will usually work in Design, where the fonts, colors, and similar settings are. In Prototype, place the small connection node where you want it and drag an arrow to the next screen. Then you can follow the flow with the play button instead of seeing all the screens together. The welcome explains the problem the app can solve, the next screen explains how to use it, and the last screen gives a clear call to action. You can share this prototype with teammates or stakeholders in marketing, legal, product, and development. These are basic wireframes; they may never go into production. The point is to make simple prototypes that you can share and send, like a model.

## Use an empty state to orient and invite

Anchor: 798 · U4 · L11 · 07:26–10:30

Onboardings are interesting because they create one of the first impressions users have of an app. Now let’s look at an empty state (*pantalla sin contenido*). A screen with no content that is well designed visually and in its wording is a strong complement to a good onboarding. Its main purpose is to help teach users how the actions in that section work, while keeping the app’s voice and tone (*voz y tono*; the captions read “Abba”). Remember to invite them to take an action.

My mother always said that you have only one chance to make a good first impression. An empty state is your chance, as a UX writer, to make one. The text should help users understand how that section works. It should give context and describe what content they will find after taking an action. The microcopy should make it clear where they are in the experience, how they arrived at a page with no content, and where that page sits in relation to the rest of the app.

A page with no content is also a good opportunity to explain the benefit of that particular app section, not the entire product or service. Use simple language because it may be the first time users have seen that section. When you suggest an action, ideally give them one button with clear wording and a call to action related to the content that will appear. This focuses attention on the most important information and avoids confusion.

Here is how I would use the idea in my taco-reservation app. The screen is for a person who has not made a reservation yet, or has no active reservations (the captions read “no tiene las células activas”). If it is their first time using the app, they probably do not know how to make a reservation in this specific case. It has a title that identifies the section, “Your reservations”; a message that explains what is happening, “You don’t have a table reserved yet”; a question that invites the next step, “Would you like to make your next reservation?”; and a button, “Reserve a table.” Instead of leaving users in a blank section, it tells them where they are, what is happening, and what they can do. The captions read “una pantalla que uno tiene contenido,” even though the example and explanation are about a screen that does not yet have content.

## Practice and choose a useful screen count

Anchor: 798 · U4 · L11 · 10:30–11:05

You now have information about how to make an onboarding, including a small prototype, and how to design a page with no content. Apply these ideas to your final project, whether it is for a client, a hobby, or your personal brand. Go into Figma, experiment, and review your screens. One tip: an onboarding should ideally have three to five screens at most. Every app and website is different, but those are the suggested proportions. Practice the ideas. In the next lesson I’ll explain how to write error messages, notifications, and buttons.

### Key points (derived)

- Introduce words while the design is taking shape, and give onboarding information one step at a time.
- Use an empty state to explain the section, describe what will appear, and give one relevant next action.
- Prototype the screens so teammates and stakeholders can follow and share the flow.
