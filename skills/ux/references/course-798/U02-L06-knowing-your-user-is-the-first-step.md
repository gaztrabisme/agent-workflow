## Balance user needs with business needs

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L06 · 00:34–01:30

798 · U02 · L06 · 00:34–01:30

As we have discussed, design depends on words, so words are part of the experience design. That brings to mind Uncle Ben’s line from *Spider-Man*: “With great power comes great responsibility.” Why do I say that? You and your team probably know a lot about your own product, but that gives you a biased view. You are working on the same thing, and you can be so focused on one tree that you cannot see the whole forest.

One key part of being a UX Writer is understanding that you need to know the people who will use—or already use—your website or app very well. That helps you do your job better. You can balance your users’ needs with the business’s needs and, with your team in the middle, work out where both can win.

## Learn from research and support

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L06 · 01:30–03:08

798 · U02 · L06 · 01:30–03:08

How can you get to know users once you start working? First, I recommend inviting someone from user research (*investigación de usuario*) or UX research (*investigación UX*) for coffee. These people know the product and know the people who use it because they are in contact with them, carry out research, bring people in for tests, and understand what motivates them, what catches their attention, and what they find difficult. You can work with them to test your content later.

You will need quantitative tests that measure clicks, such as A/B tests (*pruebas A/B*). You put two different texts into the product and see which one gets more clicks, almost like a race. This gives you interesting information about what is happening. The other part, where the research team comes in, helps you understand why it is happening.

![798 · U2 · L06 · 02:05.80](frames/U02L06-F0205.80.jpg)

On screen: “UX research” / “La gente que hace investigación con las personas que usan tu producto.” [People who do research with the people who use your product.]  
Shows: A heading about UX research and an image of two people in conversation.

Once you have spoken with the researchers, talk to another part of your team: user support (*soporte al usuario*). The department may have a different name, but find those people. They are a gold mine of anecdotal evidence because they are in constant communication with users. You can learn, almost without filters, what motivates people, what they understand, what they find difficult, what they hate, and even whether there is something they would like to set on fire. That outside perspective from support teams is vital.

## Listen to the words users use

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L06 · 03:08–04:11

798 · U02 · L06 · 03:08–04:11

Pay attention to the words people use to describe the things in your product. Internally, we may have a name for something that feels normal or obvious to us, while people outside the team call it something completely different. If you see many people changing the name, that may make the product easier for them to use.

That is why I always say it is important to know the people who use your product or service as well as possible before you write. Until you know their needs, you will not know what to do. You cannot try to guide someone without knowing their needs or goals. You cannot invent this information. You may have ideas, assumptions, or concepts, but until you know who your users are, what they think, what they need, and how you can help them get it, you will not be able to guide them.

## Find clues in related products

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L06 · 04:11–05:52

798 · U02 · L06 · 04:11–05:52

Now that you know why it matters to know your users, I will show you a proto-persona (*protopersona*) in Figma. For your final project, you will use the product or service you are working on. Ideally, you will already know something about the people who will use it.

Here is a useful tip: you can sometimes discover an app’s communication style on its support pages or social media. That can help you pick up its voice and tone (*voz y tono*), which we will discuss later. To begin, research the competition. Look at what information competitors provide, how they do things, how they address users, and how they write to them. If you do not have much information about your own product or service, you can use this as inspiration and make a remix of it: learn from what others do and apply it.

Another option is to look at similar products, even if they are not direct competitors. This can be useful when you do not have information about your own users. It is a bit like working backward. It is not ideal, but it is a valid option. For my app, which does not exist yet, I am starting to research users and also looking at what people with similar products are doing. For example, TacoGuru and TacoCerca (captions: “Taco Guru” and “Taco Serkan”). That gives me ideas and helps me understand what is relevant.

## Build a proto-persona from what you know

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L06 · 05:52–08:19

798 · U02 · L06 · 05:52–08:19

When you have enough information about what people are doing with products similar to yours, or things you like and find motivating, the ideal next step is to make a proto-persona. This is a template; you do not need to build it from scratch. I will leave it as an additional resource so you can copy it and avoid starting from zero. That matters too: other people have already made things we can use.

For my taco app, the proto-persona is Marta López. A photo gives this person a human face and a name. Her age gives you a range of cultural references, what she might be interested in, and what she might not. Her role is former producer. She is a foodie: she likes food and places that offer that kind of experience. She also tends to use apps on her phone.

Marta is fictional, but these details already help you form an idea of who she is. Her aspiration is, “I want to eat well in a pleasant place.” What motivates her? Eating tacos in a clean, pleasant place; being hungry and craving tacos; and enjoying them with her friends. What are her goals and needs? She does not want to wait in line, wants to go at a time that works for her, and does not want to spend a lot of time deciding where to eat. What gets in her way? She does not like not knowing whether there is a table until she arrives, does not like having to eat standing up, and would rather not wait long to eat.

If you had research information about several people, this persona could combine those findings. For practical purposes, since we have not done that research, you can make a first version from what seems most obvious. At a glance, this tool gives you a much clearer idea of who you are writing and designing for. That is a key difference in UX Writing: everything we design at the content level needs to connect back to users’ needs, motivations, and aspirations.

When you have the template in Figma (captions: “prendilla”), you can copy it rather than start from scratch. You have now seen a little of the basics of Figma (captions: “fake”).

## Shopify gives content a place in the process

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L06 · 08:19–09:59

798 · U02 · L06 · 08:19–09:59

In UX Writing, it is important to have people and companies whose good work can motivate and inspire you. The first example is Shopify, a Canadian company. Its product gives people who want to create an online store all the tools and support to do so without writing a line of code. Shopify has made a system of small building blocks, like Lego: people can stack them, build a store the way they like, and customize it. There is a lot of complexity behind this, and what fascinates me is how Shopify has made it look so simple.

At Shopify, the UX Writer role has a different name: Product Content Strategist. That role is present at every step of the design process and has the same level of responsibility and work as product designers, user researchers, and front-end developers. Whenever they create something new or optimize something, there is a team of at least four people.

Another interesting point is the ratio of UX Writers to product designers. By ratio, I mean how many UX Writers there are for each designer. Ratios are usually high because the role is new, but Shopify has done this well. It has one of the lowest ratios in the tech industry (captions: “one of the smallest plates”).

## Polaris documents content as well as components

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L06 · 09:59–11:10

798 · U02 · L06 · 09:59–11:10

The next thing I want to share from Shopify is its design system (*sistema de diseño*), Polaris. A design system is a place or collection where a product brings together all its small building blocks and documents them. A design system usually shows two things: how a component looks visually and what code you need. Instead of starting from zero each time, you can copy and paste it, then modify it if needed.

For example, Polaris shows what an error message looks like and the code you can copy. The difference is that it also gives you best practices for using it. That is what is wonderful about it: it includes content guidelines (*guías de contenido*) with examples of what the writing should be. This is not very common today. With a quick look at one component, you have the basics covered in one place: how it looks, what will appear on screen, the code, and the content guidelines.

## Intercom and Slack show different approaches

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L06 · 11:10–13:21

798 · U02 · L06 · 11:10–13:21

Another example is Intercom. A couple of years ago, Jon Colman, the manager of its UX Writers—called Content Designers there because the role is a little different—changed how the team worked. He found that each content designer was doing many projects at once and argued that it is very difficult for someone to make a real business impact while doing so many things at once. So they reduced the work to one large, important project and gave the content designers the same salary and parity as product designers. With more focus, they can do more and do it better.

![798 · U2 · L06 · 11:53.90](frames/U02L06-F1153.90.jpg)

On screen: “Deliver great experiences, no matter what” / “JON COLMAN” / “Intercom – Content Design”

The next example is Slack, which people in technology companies have probably seen. Its voice and tone (*voz y tono*) are very playful. Whenever it can, each screen gives you a spark that makes you laugh or smile while guiding and helping you. When you have no messages left, Slack could simply leave the screen empty, but instead it says, “Boom. You’re all caught up.”

These companies work in different industries and some focus more on B2B or B2C, but in all the examples I have shown, content is an integral part of the experience they give to young people.
