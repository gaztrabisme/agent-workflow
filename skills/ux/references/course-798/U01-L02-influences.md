## Influences from comedy and other fields

Anchor: 798 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–01:08

In this lesson I’m going to explain what I consider my influences. On one hand, there are my influences; on the other, there are the people I feel I am constantly learning from. It is important to me to have influences that are not tied to what I do every day, and to learn about what is happening elsewhere, in other media and other fields.

I really enjoy comedy and stand-up. Among my major influences are people who write for comedy series or perform stand-up, such as Jane Kirkman, who is also a stand-up comedian and helped write the series *Marvelous Mrs. Maisel*. Another is Dan Harmon, very famous for *Rick and Morty* and also *Community*. Finally, Carlos Vallarta is one of my favorite stand-up comedians from Mexico.

I find comedy writers very interesting because they can hold their audience’s attention for a long time. They can move from a moment of “What is going on here?” to actually making you cry with laughter.

## Authors, comics, and storytelling

Anchor: 798 · U1 · L02 · 01:08–02:17

I know this may sound a little cliché, but I also have favorite authors whose work I enjoy reading. For example, Juan Rulfo and his book *El llano en llamas* (*The Plain in Flames*). He is also from a part of Mexico I come from, Jalisco. I find the way he tells stories and his distinctly local, *costumbrista* language (*lenguaje costumbrista*) impressive.

Then there is *Calvin and Hobbes*. Its author, Bill Watterson, created the whole comic and wrote it too. It has a very special sense of humor: a little acidic and a little dry. When you are young—as I was when I discovered it—you may not get it so much. Now that I am an adult, Calvin’s antics and craziness make me laugh so hard.

And finally, the master of masters: Gabriel García Márquez. This man redefined Latin American literature when he gave the world what we know today as magical realism (*realismo mágico*). The way he tells stories and draws us into his worlds and universes is impressive to me.

It is true that in UX writing (*UX Writing*) we will be telling stories. Influences like these are always important: they help you write and develop your work in a more interesting way.

## The slower rhythm of analog photography

Anchor: 798 · U1 · L02 · 02:17–03:14

When you work on digital products and services, the pace is frenetic. That is why I also enjoy taking a break with analog photography (*fotografía analógica*). Why? You go out for a walk with your camera. They range from these slightly crazy plastic things to old cameras that weigh a lot. In the end, you take your photo, and you do not know whether it turned out or not. If you are lucky, you can develop it at home or take it to a lab to be developed. This process helps you slow down a little and learn that not everything has to be fast and immediate.

Alongside the influences I consider mine, it is also important to pay attention to the people I am constantly learning from.

## Sarah Richards, Content Design, and the Readability Guidelines

Anchor: 798 · U1 · L02 · 03:14–03:58

The first book I want to talk about is *Content Design* by Sarah Richards. Sarah Richards used to work for the British government. She realized that content came at the very end of the process, and that this was always a problem (captions: *la estrella de problema*). So she founded the discipline of Content Design, which is a central part of our work as UX writers.

In addition to her work in this field, through her consultancy Content Design London she is involved in one of the most interesting current projects, called the Readability Guidelines. They are bringing people together from around the world and working collaboratively to create a universal style guide (*guía de estilo*) in English. Best of all, everything is based on usability evidence (*evidencia de usabilidad*).

## Erika Hall and Conversational Design

Anchor: 798 · U1 · L02 · 03:58–04:19

During the course I will also talk a lot about Erika Hall and her book *Conversational Design*. For me, it has been a turning point. Although its premise may sound simple—how can we make digital products and services less robotic and more human?—you will see that it is a great influence when writing.

## Strategic Writing for UX and Writing Is Designing

Anchor: 798 · U1 · L02 · 04:19–04:49

The next person I learn from, and keep learning a lot from, is Torrey Podmajersky (captions: *Torri por Madjersky*). Her book *Strategic Writing for UX* has many tools that have helped me show product teams how content is part of the process and how it can be integrated much better from the start.

Finally, we have Michael J. Metts and Andy Welfle with their book *Writing Is Designing* (captions: *Michael J. Metz y Andy Wellfleet*). They have given us the Bible of UX writing. Here you will find a lot of information, especially practical information about what you can do and how to start using it in your daily work.

## Personal and professional influences

Anchor: 798 · U1 · L02 · 04:49–05:09

You have learned a little about my influences, both personally and in my work. This is a very new field, and more and more information keeps appearing. I will leave you some additional resources. In the next lesson, I will tell you what we are going to do in this course. Will you join me?

### Key points (derived)

- Seek influences outside your daily work, including comedy, literature, comics, and analog photography.
- Give yourself time to slow down, and bring content into a product process from the beginning.
- Learn from storytelling, human conversation, and evidence about how people use content.
