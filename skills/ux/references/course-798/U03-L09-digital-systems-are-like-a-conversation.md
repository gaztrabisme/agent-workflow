# Lesson 09 · Digital systems are like a conversation

## Why digital systems should feel like a conversation

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–02:40

In this lesson, I want to explain why we say digital systems should be like a conversation. The conversation is always between a digital product or service—your app, website, or whatever you have—and the person who will use it.

As brands have tried to make digital products and services seem more human, they have moved away from robotic language and started using language that is more commercial and approachable. They do this for several reasons, but mainly to make sure that people understand what is happening and, above all, to reduce cognitive load and stress.

In 2018, Erika Hall shared an interesting premise in her book *Conversational Design* (captions: *The Conversation of Design*). She asks what we can do to make digital systems less robotic and more human. In the book, she develops her theory about why digital systems should work more like a conversation between people. If that is how they work, it makes more sense for the product or service to collaborate directly with the user to achieve their goal.

Think about how a conversation works. First, there are two entities: the person using the app on one side and the app on the other. It helps if they speak the same language. I do not mean that you speak Spanish while the app is in Polish. I mean that the language should be the same in the sense of being clear and easy to understand. For example, if you use an app to book a trip and everything is explained in airport and flight codes, it is difficult to understand. The app may technically be using the same language as you, but it is speaking very technically, and you do not have that information.

The next thing is that both sides need a shared goal. In this case, the app exists to help the person using it. To review: there are two entities who speak the same language and share an objective. If the system is too technical and robotic, it is very difficult for the user to understand what to do, and that also creates frustration.

## Draft the interaction as a chat

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L09 · 02:40–06:25

Now I’ll show a small tool Erika Hall (captions: “Erika Holm”) discusses in her book for applying conversational design to interface writing. Once you understand the structure of a conversation, you can apply it to an interface by drafting what an interaction might look like. In my case, it is an app for reserving tacos.

First, you need to know the user’s goal. It was easy in my case because we have all this research, but you need to have that prior information clearly in hand to apply it here. Erika suggests writing the conversation as if it were a chat: on one side you have your person or user, and on the other side you have the app. When you format it like a WhatsApp, Messenger, or other chat conversation, it is easier to start using simpler language. You are used to doing that in chat apps, so this helps you simplify.

For example, the person says, “Hi, I want to eat tacos.” The app can answer, “Hi, when would you like to eat your tacos?” The person replies, “I feel like having them today.” The app asks what time. The person says around 8 p.m. The app replies, “Perfect. I’ll make a reservation for today at 8 p.m. Does that sound good?” The person says yes, they want tacos, and the app confirms that the reservation is made. With the information gathered in the conversation, the app can guide the user.

I am not saying you are going to create a chatbot. This is a template. Instead of immediately drawing little boxes to decide where text should go, you practise to see what main message you want to communicate. You can also do this as a role-play with another person: one person acts as the user and the other as the app. When you hear yourself say the lines, you start to notice things like, “Maybe the app would not say it that way,” or “A normal person would never ask something like that.” When you work inside a product, its language is already deep in your head and you may use it without thinking.

It is easy to make the template: create a rectangle, then create more rectangles. You can change the color, or duplicate what is already there. Then you have a template to work with and can enter the conversation. I suggest doing this exercise for your final project, and seeing what the conversation between a user and your product or service would be like.

This exercise helps make sure the message is short and clear, the language is conversational, and both sides understand what is happening. The user needs to understand what they can and cannot do, and the next steps need to be clear. The text in the draft probably will not appear exactly this way in your interface, but it helps you get started, unlock the work, and refine the microcopy. It is useful for things like onboardings, error messages, empty screens, and other interactions we will look at later. Use it especially the first time you write for a particular interaction.

When you start writing for interfaces, you will see why the text is called microcopy: the space available is very small, especially on mobile screens. Every word in the interface has to make sense, have a reason for being there, and fit its context. One famous motto for microcopy is “clear, concise, and useful.” You will hear it a lot. There is a fourth point in Michael J. Metz and Andy Welfle’s book *Writing Is Designing* (captions: “Andy Wellfleet”): be human. Let’s go through each point.

## Clear, concise, useful, and human

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L09 · 06:25–08:48

Being clear means avoiding technical terms, acronyms, and difficult words to read. Brands often invent words to describe their products; avoid those whenever you can. Clarity also means honesty and giving users the context they need.

Being concise means getting to the point and being direct, without going in circles. Explaining something in as few words as possible, while keeping it clear, reduces cognitive load. Users have other things to do. Ideally, they should spend only the time strictly necessary to achieve their goals in your app. If you can explain something with fewer words, do it.

Your microcopy also needs to be useful: give the user directions about the next action, or tell them how they can recover from a system error. If it is unclear, not concise, and not useful, things become complicated.

What do I mean by human? Try to be empathetic. Think about people’s situation and context; you cannot disconnect yourself from the reality they are experiencing if you really want to help. Add that human touch to the microcopy. It helps your digital interface stand apart from others and gives users the sense that someone is behind the text: someone who is thinking about context and language. The text is not automatically generated by a robot; a person is considering these things.

## Apply the qualities to error messages

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L09 · 08:48–11:50

Now let’s look at how clarity, concision, and usefulness show up in microcopy. This is a well-known example from Google that you can find online. The original message says, “Error. An authentication error occurred. OK.” It is understandable and more or less clear. What if we put the message through these filters?

First, make it clearer: “Error signing in.” That is three more words and gives much more context. If the message then says, “You entered an incorrect password,” I know exactly what happened and why. One more pass makes it clear and concise: “Incorrect password.” But something is missing. It is not useful yet. When the message is clear, concise, and useful, it can end up as: “Incorrect password. Try again or recover your password.” It is clear because you know what happened, concise because it goes straight to the point, and useful because it tells you what to do.

This also influences the way the content is designed. We start with a title, body copy, and a button. In the end, we have just body copy and two button options.

Now let’s apply the same approach to my taco reservation app. The original says, “Error. A reservation error occurred. OK.” Let’s make it clear: “Error creating a reservation. Table unavailable at this time. OK.” That is a little clearer. Then we make it concise: “Table unavailable. OK.” You can see how robotic that sounds. What if we add the useful part? “Table unavailable. Choose another table or another time.”

Apply this to your final project, perhaps at the beginning as an exercise. With practice, you will be able to see it as you write. When you have a screen ready, use a small checklist in your head. Is the original message clear? Yes: check. Is it concise? Check. Is it useful? No? Then rewrite it. Perhaps it is clear and useful but not very concise; work on that. The idea is to balance the three so they work in the screens you are writing, on a checklist, or even just in your head. Clear, concise, and useful.

These three concepts may seem basic, but they are the pillars of doing your job well as a UX writer. In the next lesson, we will look at voice and tone. They are not the same, but I will explain that now.
