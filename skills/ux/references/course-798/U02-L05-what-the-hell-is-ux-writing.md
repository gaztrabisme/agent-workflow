## Words and visuals make one experience

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L05 · 00:07–01:55

798 · U02 · L05 · 00:07–01:55

In this lesson I’ll explain what UX Writing is, go over some basic principles, and look at what it is and what it is not.

Imagine this page. The idea is good, the colors are nice, and the layout is good. But if I ask what the page is about, what it does, or what might be happening here, you probably cannot tell me, because you are missing half the experience: the text. When people use an app or website, they do not interact only with the visuals or only with the words. They interact with both at the same time. The content and visual design have to work together; that is the only way to ensure the best possible user experience (*experiencia del usuario*).

There is still a long way to go. This happens on many sites and in many teams, as the cartoon shows. One person says, “Perfect, I’ll start designing when you give me your text.” The writer replies, “No, no, I’ll start on the content once I see the design.” They repeat this back and forth and get stuck in an endless loop. That is what happens when we do not work together and do not treat words as part of the experience design.

When text is left until the end, problems slip through. Changing a line is relatively easy, but by then the page has already gone out and people have seen it. These mistakes happen when content is not considered from the beginning. Several examples have always stood out to me, including situations I have experienced; over time, you learn the value of starting to work together.

## Wireframes need real content

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L05 · 01:55–03:03

798 · U02 · L05 · 01:55–03:03

Here are a few examples of what can go wrong. A designer has a very carefully laid-out wireframe, with spaces and timing set, and lorem ipsum marking the text areas. Then the content arrives and the designer wonders what happened: everything has shifted and the words do not fit. If you design without the content and without considering how much space it needs, you cannot tell how the finished design will look. You are designing only halfway.

So someone learns from the last time and leaves much more room for the text. Then the copy arrives and, to everyone’s surprise, it needs much less space than expected.

What about this example, one of my favorites? The screen looks well structured: there is a title, subtitles, body copy, and two buttons. Then imagine that the second button has to use a longer language, such as German, Spanish, or French. It no longer works. Maybe that is not the right text for a button; perhaps it should be an auxiliary subtitle followed by a much shorter button. But if the writer receives a wireframe and is asked to fill in the boxes, this is what happens.

## UX Writing is part of design

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L05 · 03:03–05:12

798 · U02 · L05 · 03:03–05:12

We will talk about what UX Writing is throughout the course, and I will also explain the UX Writer’s role. The name itself is fairly new, but the work has existed for many years, almost as long as digital systems, products, and services. What is new is having someone specifically responsible for designing this part of the experience.

Over time, design and product teams at different companies have realized that if someone is responsible for the visual side, it also makes sense to have someone polish and consider the content and words from a design perspective. If you search LinkedIn or Google, you will see that the role is not only new; people also do not agree on its name. You may see Product Writer, UX Copywriter, or UX Content Writer. My favorite is what people sometimes call me: “the person of words.”

Because the role still has no settled name, product teams may not know exactly what you do.

For me, a line from *Writing Is Designing* by Andy Welfle and Michael J. Metts helps define the distinction (captions: “Andy Wealthy” and “Michael J. Metz”). The line is: “Writing is part of the design process, and people who write are also designing.” The key is that writers are designing, too. They approach content not as a set of spaces or boxes to fill, but as designers asking how to use words as design tools to create a better experience. That makes the distinction clear to me and helps us see the work from a new perspective. The book is listed among the additional resources.

## A UX Writer guides people

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L05 · 05:12–06:04

798 · U02 · L05 · 05:12–06:04

I have explained a little about what UX Writing is (at [05:12], I slip and say, “What the hell is UX Rating?”); now I will explain what a UX Writer does and how the role fits into a product team. People often ask what I do as a UX Writer. I explain that we are a little like Gandalf—not because we are wizards, but because we are guides. We use words in an app or website to guide people toward what they need: doing the shopping, buying a plane ticket, or even buying a ticket to the movies. Our words, together with the visual elements, help users reach what they came for when they use an app or website.

## Learn the design process and understand users

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L05 · 06:04–08:15

798 · U02 · L05 · 06:04–08:15

UX Writing is much more than writing text for an interface; it is more than filling gaps or boxes. One of the first things you will need to do is learn about UX design: its processes and methods. The way to do that is to read, ask many questions, and practice. If you come from a writing background, moving into the visual side may take you out of your comfort zone. You do not need to become very good at making visuals. You do need to be able to make simple wireframes showing how you want your content to appear.

When you and the designer speak the same language and you understand the UX design theory and process, it is much easier to understand each other.

Once you understand the process and what UX design means, get to know the people who use your product or service. Talk to them. Find out what motivates them, what they like or dislike, what they understand, and what confuses them. This information helps you discover why things happen on your website or app. You need to speak with people who use or might use the product or service to understand what you can do and take their needs into account.

You will still use inspiration and creativity to solve problems, but you will also need to research and use data. Those data will inform your text. Learn to use data tools and understand what is happening: depending on the metrics your product uses, find out why a metric is rising or falling and what you might do to help it. Looking at data helps you improve what already exists and learn what has been tried and where it failed, so you do not repeat the same mistake. Without looking at data or talking to people, that is very difficult.

## Work with the rest of the product team

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L05 · 08:15–09:05

798 · U02 · L05 · 08:15–09:05

Another thing you will notice when you start working as a UX Writer is that work on a product team is teamwork, as we will discuss later. Sometimes you can go off to find inspiration in a corner, but generally you will not work that way. To create a more positive user experience, you need different perspectives on how to solve the problem. Those perspectives complement one another. If you do your own work and then try to bring it back to the team, you are forcing things together. If the graphic designer, researcher, developers, product managers, and business team work together, their perspectives help create a much better final product.

## Copywriting and UX Writing have different aims

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L05 · 09:05–11:31

798 · U02 · L05 · 09:05–11:31

People also ask how a copywriter can move into UX Writing or train for the role. I explain that the difference is in each role’s objectives, and that both are important and complementary.

Copywriters’ main objective is sales and the brand. They persuade. You will usually find them in marketing or branding teams. They tend to use more complex, beautiful, attention-grabbing language and focus on a key message for the campaign, its information, or the brand.

UX Writers start from the user—or at least they always should. Our role is to guide people through the experience. UX Writers are usually on a product team and use simpler language. The biggest difference, for me, is that a copywriter may be responsible for one key message, while we have to understand a whole flow and experience. We have to understand where a person came from when they arrive at a screen and where we will send them next. We have to stop thinking about one small screen by itself and start thinking about a much larger map.

These differences are subtle, but they help clarify the roles. I have always seen the roles as complementary. It is not about which is more important or who starts first. The copywriter’s work and the UX Writer’s work have to fit together as parts of something larger, because both affect the user experience. People see everything on their screen as the same product, service, or brand speaking to them. So you have to work with the other teams, especially the people who also create content, to make sure everything presented to the outside world makes sense.
