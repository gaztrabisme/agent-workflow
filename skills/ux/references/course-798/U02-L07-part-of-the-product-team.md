## Product design is collaborative

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L07 · 00:09–01:09

798 · U02 · L07 · 00:09–01:09

In this lesson I’ll explain what it means to be part of the product team. If you write for a living, you are probably used to working alone. But when you work in digital product design, you can no longer do that.

The UX Writer’s role sits within the product team (captions: “reloj”). That means working with other people. Product design is teamwork, and it is constantly evolving. Having people with different profiles, different ways of seeing life, and different ways of approaching a problem makes the search for a solution richer. Those perspectives complement one another. But if people are not in constant contact and collaboration, the pieces will not fit together.

## A product team adapts to its project

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L07 · 01:09–02:14

798 · U02 · L07 · 01:09–02:14

A product team can include many different people, and its members vary. I’ll explain the roles you will usually find, but what you meet will depend on the company or project. Normally there is someone responsible for product design, the visual side; someone in user research; a product manager; and sometimes a Scrum Master. There is a development team, the people who work with code (captions: “Conigo”). You may also have data science, the quantitative side of user research, while user research is the qualitative side. Ideally there is also a quality or QA team, which helps catch problems before a release. And, of course, UX Writers are part of the team too.

What distinguishes a product team from other types of teams is that it is multidisciplinary and adapts to the project.

## Roles, autonomy, and two-week sprints

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L07 · 02:14–02:58

798 · U02 · L07 · 02:14–02:58

Some projects are simpler and do not need the whole team; they need only a few people. More complex projects need more roles. One good thing about having all these roles available is that the team can grow or shrink to suit the project. That gives teams autonomy and flexibility. Each team is like a small company that can keep moving and doing its work, while staying in contact with everyone else. This autonomy and flexibility also let people spend more time on their own work and focus better.

![798 · U2 · L07 · 02:16.00](frames/U02L07-F0216.00.jpg)

On screen: “Autonomía y flexibilidad a los equipos.” [Autonomy and flexibility for teams.] / [rest omitted]

Time is measured differently. The most common arrangement tends to be two-week sprints (*sprints de dos semanas*), because that is roughly the time the whole team can work together to deliver a section or a small part of what they are building. Before starting, the team agrees how much work it can do. It has to be realistic about the number of people on the team, what else they are working on, priorities, and any deadline.

## Four common team rituals

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L07 · 02:58–04:54

798 · U02 · L07 · 02:58–04:54

Being part of a product team is interesting because you discover new things. Especially at the beginning, stay calm and be present whenever you can. Every team and company is different, and names can change, but I’ll describe four common rituals in product teams.

The first is sprint planning (*planificación del sprint*). The team sits down and agrees what to focus on during the sprint. It is a conversation: one person needs this, another needs that. You reach agreements about what is realistic and what you want to do.

Then you follow up on the work in the daily Scrum (*Scrum diario*), sometimes called a stand-up. It is a short meeting, from five to fifteen minutes depending on the size of the team. People stand because when they are seated they are more comfortable, while standing keeps them more active. The idea is to talk about three things: What did you do yesterday? What will you do today? Is anything blocking your progress? This helps the team see when someone is blocked and work out how to unblock them. It also lets everyone know where each person is in the work.

Next comes the sprint demo. The team has planned and kept in contact, and now it has something to show. It wants to hear what the rest of the team thinks of the design: Does it make sense? How can it be improved before release? Finally, to close the loop, there is the sprint retrospective (*retro del sprint*; captions: “reto del”), where the team reviews what worked and what needs to improve for the next sprint.

That is the same logic we use to design a product out in the world: it changes, improves, and evolves. The team applies that same process internally to discover how it can improve.

## Make room for the UX Writer on the team

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L07 · 04:54–06:45

798 · U02 · L07 · 04:54–06:45

It is essential to take part in these rituals, whatever your team calls them, because the UX Writer’s role needs to be considered part of the process. I know that is not always easy. Imagine joining a team that has never worked with a UX Writer. It is a complex and slow process, but you have to keep going.

A UX Writer who joins a company that did not have the role has two jobs. The regular job is designing with words alongside the team. The other is to evangelize—to explain to the rest of the team the benefits of having you there and having you be part of the process.

Be very clear that it is not about arriving, banging on the table, and saying, “Stop the presses! This is how we do things because I am the content expert.” That does not work. You are not the new sheriff in town. You also have to get to know the users and the team. Use the same methods: talk with your colleagues, understand what motivates and interests them, and learn what they are trying to achieve. Listen. Once you have gathered that information, you can say, “I understand that you care about these things and want to achieve these things. From my perspective as a UX Writer, this is how I can help you get what you need.” That helps you build relationships and closer ways of working with the rest of the team.

Here is a tip someone once gave me that has worked wonderfully: bring chocolate cookies during your first few days in the job. People do not expect them, and they nearly always make the team smile. They give you an opening to start collaborating. Remember it: chocolate cookies.

## Write in the design tool and collaborate

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L07 · 06:45–09:56

798 · U02 · L07 · 06:45–09:56

Starting work as a UX Writer without having used a design tool can feel intimidating or even frightening, because you are leaving your comfort zone. One of my best tips is to stop writing in word processors. There, you have no context: just a blank page and a cursor flashing at you while you start to write. You do not know where your words will sit in the design, how they will look with a button, whether you need one button or two, or whether you could split a title into two parts. You cannot see any of that when you write alone on a blank page.

If you write directly in design tools, it is easier for you and for the rest of your team to see these things. Learn the tools that help you with the design process and help you show the team how you can contribute value.

The design tool should depend on what your team needs. The important thing is that writing in a design tool makes collaboration easier. You do not have to be an expert; the goal is to learn little by little until you can at least create simple wireframes (*wireframes*).

Prepare the files for this. Talk with your product designer and prepare a space where you can work without breaking anything, where you can explore. Once you put words into context, you may see that the idea you had in your head does not work. Then you can try moving things around with the designer and say, “This does not fit for me. Why don’t we try this other option?” The designer can also say, “This concept or word feels off to me. Why don’t we try something else?” Neither person is trying to make the other person’s mistake; you are collaborating from a place where both understand the work.

Another thing to consider is version control (*control de versiones*). It keeps you from working in the main file and avoids the situation we have all seen: “Final, Final Final, really final this time.” Tools can let you create versions and later combine them. The point is to make collaboration easier. The tool depends on the team’s needs. If everyone is in the same place, you may need one kind of tool; if half your team is in another country, as mine is, you need a tool that gives you flexibility. There are many tools. Think about what you need to do with them.

## Group layers so the layout moves together

Anchor: 798 · U2 · L07 · 16:45–18:39

798 · U02 · L07 · 16:45–18:39

If you do not have the colors or options already set up, the same idea is simple: create a frame, press R to make another rectangle, and change its color to distinguish it. Use the text tool for the words.

I want to show you something useful: grouping. Why is grouping important? This is a simple screen, but imagine you have several and want to move them. If you select the frame by its frame symbol, you can move everything together. Often, though, you try to move something and it gets left behind. To avoid that, first press Ctrl+Z to return to where you were, then group the selection. In Figma, selected items turn blue. Make sure everything is selected, right-click, and choose “Group selection.” Now it all moves as one. You can also ungroup it.

Here is your group. You can change its name. Work with layers (*capas*), because the group includes the frame, rectangles, text, and everything else. I can call it “Respuesta.” When there are only three little objects, this is not a big problem. But imagine an app with many screens and elements. Grouping keeps things together and lets you search for what you need.
