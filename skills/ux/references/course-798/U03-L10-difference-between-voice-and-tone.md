# Lesson 10 · Difference between Voice and Tone

## Voice stays consistent; tone adapts

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L10 · 00:00–02:03

In this lesson, I want us to focus on a fundamental concept: voice and tone. People often say that a product or service needs a “tone of voice,” but these are two different concepts.

Voice has to be consistent; it is a little like personality. I, Mario, always have the same voice. Tone changes, because it has to adapt to the situation. I have the same voice when I speak to people I do not know, but my tone is different from the one I use with family or friends.

These changes should not depend on whatever you, as a UX writer, happen to come up with. They depend on the interaction the user is having with your app or website and on the point they have reached in the flow. That is where you adjust the tone. Voice stays consistent, but tone can cover a wide range: it might be friendly or approachable, formal, or reassuring, depending on what the interaction needs. Make changes gradually rather than jumping from one extreme to another. Think about how a person would speak: I cannot be speaking one way and then suddenly switch to a completely different way. If these changes are tied to the interaction and happen gradually, the experience feels more natural and consistent.

## Match the tone to the situation

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L10 · 02:03–04:22

For example, when a user has achieved what they came to do, the tone can be cheerful and congratulatory (captions: “conglomeratorio”). You might say, “Congratulations. Your order will be sent soon,” or “Congratulations, you have successfully booked your table.”

But imagine the user has a problem with a payment method. It would be jarring to read, “Congratulations, your credit card is not valid.” The message is about something serious, while the tone is very cheerful; it does not match what is happening, the interaction, or the context. Here, the message needs to be more serious and kind, and above all it needs to help. Instead of simply saying that the credit card is not valid and offering an “Accept” button, explain what happened and give an option. For example: “Your credit card has expired. Try another payment method.” The button can say “Try again.” That is more empathetic because it explains what happened and what the user can do.

The Nielsen Norman Group has a framework called the four dimensions of tone. They are basic guides for defining the tone of a product or service. The first is humor versus seriousness: you can be funny, but sometimes you need to be serious, depending on the situation. Another is formal versus casual and friendly. A person can use both, depending on where they are and the situation.

The third is respectful and reverent versus irreverent. Be careful: irreverence should be directed at the product or service, never at the user. Very few brands can do this successfully, so be cautious. The last dimension is enthusiastic versus informative.

## Map tone through the product journey

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L10 · 04:22–08:40

Now let’s see how you can use these dimensions in your final project. I have moved to a map of the steps a user of my taco-reservation app has to go through, from the welcome screen to confirming a reservation. It is linear, and this is the successful path (*happy path*). You can use this map to define and chart the tone at each interaction point.

In my example, the steps are: welcome; choose a taco restaurant; present options; the user does not find an option they like; choose an alternative; and confirm the reservation. I usually map tones at three levels: a more moderate one, the most cheerful or congratulatory one, and the one that tends to be more formal.

Let’s put ourselves in the user’s place. At the welcome, Marta is excited and wants to eat, so this interaction is casual and friendly. The tone should be warm enough to welcome her, but also cheerful. At the next step, choosing a taco restaurant, you can be more enthusiastic. You might say, “Hey, congratulations, you have a taqueria”—you had tacos in mind and now you have a taqueria. Great.

When you present the options, lower the tone a little and return to casual and friendly. You should use both enthusiasm and formality sparingly; otherwise they can be tiring or boring. In this step, the more casual tone helps explain how the system works and how Marta can make a reservation.

Marta searches and discovers that the restaurant she chose has no opening at the time or on the day she wants. You cannot be enthusiastic here, and a very friendly tone would also seem strange when the situation is negative for her. Explain what happened in a way that reassures her: there is no availability for that day and time.

When you offer an alternative, bring the tone up a little and present options: “There is nothing at that date and time, but I have these other times for that date,” or “If you want that time, I have these other dates.” Marta chooses something that works. If she cannot go at eight, perhaps she can go at eight-thirty. Once she makes that choice, raise the tone again—confetti, a school band—because you are confirming that the reservation is made.

Keep the product’s voice in mind, because it also sets limits on how far the tone can shift. Put your axes at the top and mark the main steps of the interaction. You can make the map as specific or general as you like, but if it is too general you will miss things, and if it is too detailed you will quickly feel overwhelmed. Find the most emblematic interactions in your app or website. Put them in order, set the three levels, use smiley faces or X marks—whatever is easiest—and map the tone. When you draw a line through it, you can see how the tone changes.

This is useful when you start writing microcopy. You can look at the map and remember that this interaction is about choosing a taqueria, where a little enthusiasm may fit. Ideally, add examples at the bottom to show what your product’s voice sounds like in a casual/friendly tone and in an enthusiastic tone. When new people join the team, instead of explaining everything from memory, you can show them the map and say, “This is how we do things here.” It is a very useful tool.

## Evolve a voice-and-tone guide

Anchor: 798 · U3 · L10 · 08:40–13:00

Now that you know the difference between voice and tone, let’s see how you can maintain and evolve a voice-and-tone guide (*guía de voz y tono*). We have talked about Andy Welfle and Michael J. Metts’s book *Writing Is Designing* (captions: “Andy Wellfie and Michael J. Metz”), especially its idea of being clear, concise, useful, and human. They also say that the voice and tone of a product is a tool that needs to evolve constantly.

It is an effort that continues over time. Technology changes quickly, and so do your audience and users. If your team has clearly defined the attributes that give your product or service its voice and tone, that helps you keep defining them, changing them, and evolving them. If the document or guide—or the way you manage voice and tone—does not evolve, you can fall into a trap. It has to keep changing.

Another tool is “This, but not this.” It seems simple, but it helps a lot when defining things. The tool comes from *Nicely Said* by Nicole Fenton and Kate Kiefer Lee. It works by creating a list of words that describe your product or service, then pairing each one with a word that describes what it is not. This second word helps the rest of the team understand each personality trait.

Let’s use Mailchimp as an example. Mailchimp is a newsletter service. It describes itself as fun but not childish, confident but not conceited (captions: “pero no en Grecia”), intelligent but not smug, informal but not careless, and expert but not bossy (captions: “pero no mandó”). Now apply this to my taco-reservation app. It is friendly but not on top of you all the time; flexible but not unfocused; fast but it does not neglect details; and informal, but it does not try to be funny.

Take this tool and apply it to the voice and tone for your client, personal brand, or hobby. These guides help you stay consistent. Over time, more and more screens appear. Without a place to check how the information is structured and what the voice and tone are, you start to lose consistency. A guide also saves time: you do not have to reinvent everything. When you are unsure how to communicate in an interaction, you can check the guide to remember the tone. It also helps teach new people joining the UX-writing team. A guide saves time for the people already there and helps new teammates learn.

Working with a product team means working on things that can scale, thinking in systems and in things you can reuse so you can be more productive and move faster. It is about working more intelligently, not harder. If you can make life easier for yourself, your team, and other people, document it. Create and evolve your voice-and-tone guide.
