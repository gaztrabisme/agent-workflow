# 798 — Introduction to UX Writing

Working notes from the course reading copy. Lesson anchors point to the matching section in `references/course-798/`.

## Method steps

1. **Choose a product or service and frame the problem.** Use a client, hobby, personal brand, or invented product; state what problem it solves and how the product solves it. For an initial “About us” description, first answer what the product does, what benefit it gives, what problem it solves, and what makes it different. (798 · U1 · L04 · 00:00–00:35; 798 · U1 · L04 · 00:35–01:11; 798 · U2 · L07 · 11:40–12:37; 798 · U2 · L07 · 12:37–13:07)
2. **Learn who will use it and what they need.** Talk with users and user researchers; ask user support what people understand, struggle with, and request; inspect relevant metrics and past attempts. Research competitors or similar products for information and language clues when direct knowledge is limited. Do not treat team assumptions as knowledge of users. (798 · U2 · L05 · 06:04–08:15; 798 · U2 · L06 · 01:30–03:08; 798 · U2 · L06 · 03:08–04:11; 798 · U2 · L06 · 04:11–05:52)
3. **Make a provisional user picture from the available evidence.** Put the person’s aspirations, motivations, goals and needs, and objections in a proto-persona; update it from research when available. (798 · U2 · L06 · 05:52–08:19)
4. **Work inside the product team and design process.** Bring content into design early, join the team’s planning and review rituals, and learn the team’s tools and constraints. Understand the user’s path into and out of each screen so the work serves the whole flow. (798 · U2 · L05 · 00:07–01:55; 798 · U2 · L05 · 09:05–11:31; 798 · U2 · L07 · 02:58–04:54; 798 · U2 · L07 · 04:54–06:45)
5. **Draft the interaction as a conversation.** Start from the user’s goal and write both sides of the exchange in chat form. Use the dialogue to find the main message and to check that the system’s questions, options, and next steps make sense. Then turn that draft into interface copy. (798 · U3 · L09 · 02:40–06:25)
6. **Map voice and tone across the important points in the flow.** Keep the product voice consistent; decide how tone changes with the user’s situation. Choose emblematic interactions, place them in order, set tone levels, and map the changes without making the map too general or too detailed. (798 · U3 · L10 · 00:00–02:03; 798 · U3 · L10 · 04:22–08:40)
7. **Shape the content for how people read and for the screen context.** Put key information in headings, subheadings, and opening words; simplify and split dense content where needed. Use real content early enough to see how words fit with layout, visuals, and actions. (798 · U3 · L08 · 03:42–06:38; 798 · U3 · L08 · 06:38–08:38; 798 · U3 · L08 · 10:20–12:04; 798 · U2 · L05 · 01:55–03:03)
8. **Write the messages for the interaction type.** Cover onboarding, empty states, notifications, errors, buttons, and other microcopy in the context where each appears; give each message the information and action appropriate to that moment. (798 · U4 · L11 · 01:14–03:22; 798 · U4 · L11 · 07:26–10:30; 798 · U4 · L12 · 00:00–03:43; 798 · U4 · L12 · 05:50–10:41; 798 · U4 · L12 · 10:41–14:02)
9. **Review accessibility and localization with design, development, and localization partners.** Check the order and wording screen-reader users receive, provide useful alternative text and labels, and give localization partners the flow and context for their work. (798 · U4 · L13 · 04:28–08:09; 798 · U4 · L13 · 08:09–11:23; 798 · U4 · L13 · 11:23–13:18; 798 · U4 · L13 · 13:18–14:20; 798 · U4 · L13 · 14:20–16:09)
10. **Refine with evidence and team feedback.** Compare variants with click-based A/B tests and use qualitative research to understand why results differ. Use available data to improve existing content and avoid repeating failed approaches. (798 · U2 · L05 · 06:04–08:15; 798 · U2 · L06 · 01:30–03:08)

## Principles and claims

- Words and visual design are experienced together; content belongs in experience design from the beginning. (798 · U2 · L05 · 00:07–01:55)
- Real copy can change the space and layout a wireframe needs; placeholder text cannot show how the finished screen will fit. (798 · U2 · L05 · 01:55–03:03)
- A UX Writer guides people through the product and should think about the whole flow, not one isolated screen. (798 · U2 · L05 · 05:12–06:04; 798 · U2 · L05 · 09:05–11:31)
- UX writing starts from the user’s needs while still accounting for business needs. (798 · U2 · L06 · 00:34–01:30)
- User research, support conversations, comparable products, and product data inform content; assumptions alone do not establish users’ needs. (798 · U2 · L05 · 06:04–08:15; 798 · U2 · L06 · 01:30–03:08; 798 · U2 · L06 · 03:08–04:11; 798 · U2 · L06 · 04:11–05:52)
- A proto-persona can make the intended user more concrete; the course’s unresearched example is an initial version, not research findings. (798 · U2 · L06 · 05:52–08:19)
- A product team is multidisciplinary and adapts to the project; regular collaboration helps the different perspectives fit together. (798 · U2 · L07 · 01:09–02:14; 798 · U2 · L07 · 02:14–02:58)
- Words are design material, like pixels; they need to make sense with visuals, code, animation, and interaction. (798 · U3 · L08 · 08:38–10:20)
- Readers’ eyes move through fixations and saccades; unclear text can prompt regressions and add cognitive load. (798 · U3 · L08 · 00:00–03:42)
- The teacher cites findings that people read 20–28% of a web page, cognitive load rises 11% per 100 words, and readers scan an F-shaped pattern. (798 · U3 · L08 · 03:42–06:38)
- Put the most relevant information in headings, subheadings, and the opening words; use concise, current content and break up long text. (798 · U3 · L08 · 03:42–06:38)
- Prefer simple, common words; making content accessible is not the same as lowering its level. (798 · U3 · L08 · 03:42–06:38)
- Microcopy should be clear, concise, useful, and human: avoid jargon, get to the point, guide the next action, and respond empathetically to context. (798 · U3 · L09 · 06:25–08:48)
- A digital product can be treated as a conversation between two entities using understandable language and sharing a goal. (798 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–02:40)
- Voice stays consistent; tone changes with the user’s interaction and situation, with gradual shifts between tones. (798 · U3 · L10 · 00:00–02:03)
- The four tone dimensions are humor/seriousness, formal/casual-friendly, respectful/irreverent, and enthusiastic/informative; irreverence should not target the user. (798 · U3 · L10 · 02:03–04:22)
- A tone guide and “This, but not this” descriptions help a team stay consistent, save time, and bring new teammates into the product’s writing style. (798 · U3 · L10 · 08:40–13:00)
- An onboarding should explain what the app is and what a user can do, the value it offers, and how to use it; deliver information at the right time and pace. (798 · U4 · L11 · 01:14–03:22)
- An empty state should orient people, explain what will appear after an action, and invite a relevant next step. (798 · U4 · L11 · 07:26–10:30)
- Notifications interrupt a person’s day; they should be relevant to context, explain the next action, and lead with the user’s benefit. (798 · U4 · L12 · 00:00–03:43)
- Error messages should explain the problem in context and offer recovery; do not blame the person. (798 · U4 · L12 · 05:50–10:41)
- Buttons should name the action that will actually happen, stay short, use verbs and active voice, and make alternatives predictable. (798 · U4 · L12 · 10:41–14:02)
- Screen-reader users move through content in blocks; provide required information before the action and coordinate the spoken order with development. (798 · U4 · L13 · 04:28–08:09)
- Alternative text should explain meaningful images and icon destinations; visible labels can help when icons are unclear. Text embedded in an image is not live text for a screen reader. (798 · U4 · L13 · 08:09–11:23)
- Localization means adapting content to other cultures and languages, not translating strings without context. (798 · U4 · L13 · 11:23–13:18)
- A shared glossary saves time and cost and lets teams check longer localized words in the wireframe. (798 · U4 · L13 · 13:18–14:20)
- A CopyDocs page can give localization partners project context, style notes, a contextual screenshot, and copy variants. (798 · U4 · L13 · 14:20–16:09)
- The Figma tone map shows six interaction points and places the “no availability” case at the formal level, with enthusiasm at restaurant choice and reservation confirmation. (798 · U3 · L10 · 04:22–08:40)
- **(frame)** In the three heat maps, the red and yellow attention areas are concentrated toward the upper and left portions of the example pages. (798 · U3 · L08 · 03:42–06:38)

## Checks the teacher uses

- **Whole-screen comprehension:** Can a person tell what the page is about and what is happening when visuals and text are considered together? Rendered-screen review helps locate missing or mismatched content; whether it is understandable needs user judgment. (798 · U2 · L05 · 00:07–01:55)
- **Fit with real content:** Put actual copy into the wireframe and inspect whether the title, body, and actions still fit, including longer localized wording. From a rendered artifact, inspect line wrapping, overflow, and available space; deciding whether the layout still communicates well needs judgment. (798 · U2 · L05 · 01:55–03:03; 798 · U4 · L12 · 10:41–14:02)
- **User grounding:** Check whether the draft reflects what users need, understand, and call things; consult research and support rather than relying on team assumptions. This requires research or qualitative judgment, not a screenshot alone. (798 · U2 · L06 · 01:30–03:08; 798 · U2 · L06 · 03:08–04:11)
- **Conversation draft:** Check that the message is short and clear, both sides understand what is happening, users know what they can and cannot do, and the next step is clear. Presence of turns and next steps can be inspected in the artifact; naturalness and clarity need judgment or role-play. (798 · U3 · L09 · 02:40–06:25)
- **Clear, concise, useful, human:** Check for technical terms and acronyms, unnecessary words, a useful next action, empathy, honesty, and context. Word count can be counted, but clarity, usefulness, and empathy need judgment and user understanding. (798 · U3 · L09 · 06:25–08:48)
- **Reading pattern:** Check that the important information appears in headings, subheadings, and opening words and that dense copy is split into pieces. Inspect the rendered hierarchy by eye; eye-tracking or heat maps can measure where readers look, as in the cited research. (798 · U3 · L08 · 03:42–06:38)
- **Onboarding:** Check for the three outcomes (what the app is/does, its value, how to use it), relevant information at the right pace, and a clear call to action. Count screens in the artifact; whether the content is relevant and understandable needs judgment. (798 · U4 · L11 · 01:14–03:22; 798 · U4 · L11 · 03:22–07:26)
- **Onboarding length:** The teacher’s rule of thumb is three to five screens at most. This can be checked by counting screens in the prototype. (798 · U4 · L11 · 10:30–11:05)
- **Empty state:** Check that the screen identifies the section, explains the current lack of content, gives context about what action will produce, and offers one clearly worded related button. These elements can be checked in the rendered screen; fit to the user’s situation needs judgment. (798 · U4 · L11 · 07:26–10:30)
- **Tone:** Check that the tone fits the interaction, changes gradually, and stays within the product’s voice. Map labels can be inspected in the artifact; appropriateness to the user’s situation needs judgment. (798 · U3 · L10 · 00:00–02:03; 798 · U3 · L10 · 02:03–04:22; 798 · U3 · L10 · 04:22–08:40)
- **Notification checklist:** Check expected/unexpected status, seriousness, brevity and clarity, the next action, relevance and context, user benefit at the start, and consistency with voice and tone. Most criteria need judgment; count the opening 35–45 characters and check whether the key information appears before truncation. (798 · U4 · L12 · 00:00–03:43)
- **Error recovery:** Check for context, explanation of what happened, and a recovery option; ensure the wording is direct, clear, kind, precise, constructive, plain, and non-blaming. The three parts can be checked for presence in the artifact; correctness, kindness, and usefulness need judgment. (798 · U4 · L12 · 05:50–10:41)
- **Button behavior:** Check that the label accurately describes the action, uses a short active verb, and that each option has a predictable result. Compare the label with the prototype or implemented behavior; the label length is countable, while clarity needs judgment. (798 · U4 · L12 · 10:41–14:02)
- **Screen-reader order and form help:** Check that the label, help text (including password requirements), and field are announced in that order before the user acts. This cannot be verified from a static visual alone; test with a screen reader and verify the navigation order with development. (798 · U4 · L13 · 04:28–08:09)
- **Alternative text and icons:** Check that meaningful images have concise alt text, icons communicate their destination, and text is live text rather than embedded only in an image. Presence can be inspected in the artifact/code; whether an icon’s destination is clear needs judgment. (798 · U4 · L13 · 08:09–11:23)
- **Localization handoff:** Check that strings come with the prior/next flow, screen context, style rationale, related research, and alternatives; put longer translated words into the wireframe to check fit. Presence and overflow can be inspected; cultural fit needs localization judgment. (798 · U4 · L13 · 11:23–13:18; 798 · U4 · L13 · 13:18–14:20; 798 · U4 · L13 · 14:20–16:09)
- **Evidence of performance:** Compare copy variants with click-based A/B tests; clicks can be measured, while qualitative research is used to understand why a version performed as it did. (798 · U2 · L06 · 01:30–03:08)

## Examples

- Jane Kirkman, Dan Harmon, and Carlos Vallarta are comedy influences because their work holds attention and moves between surprise and laughter. (798 · U1 · L02 · 00:00–01:08)
- Juan Rulfo’s local language, *Calvin and Hobbes*’ dry and acidic humor, and Gabriel García Márquez’s story worlds are writing influences the teacher connects to telling stories in UX. (798 · U1 · L02 · 01:08–02:17)
- Sarah Richards’s *Content Design* and the Readability Guidelines project are examples of bringing content into the process and grounding guidance in usability evidence. (798 · U1 · L02 · 03:14–03:58)
- A sports-news wireframe filled with real *Times* headlines shows how copy changes the space that placeholder text seemed to occupy. (798 · U2 · L05 · 01:55–03:03)
- A sign-up screen’s secondary action needs more space in a longer language, illustrating why the writer should help shape the button and supporting copy. (798 · U2 · L05 · 01:55–03:03)
- Marta López, the taco-app proto-persona, wants a pleasant place to eat, no queue, and a table at a convenient time; uncertainty and waiting are objections. (798 · U2 · L06 · 05:52–08:19)
- Shopify Polaris documents an inline error with content guidance: explain what went wrong, give a next step or one-click fix, keep it short, and avoid blame. (798 · U2 · L06 · 09:59–11:10)
- Intercom’s content designers focused on one large, important project at a time, with parity alongside product designers. (798 · U2 · L06 · 11:10–13:21)
- Slack’s “Boom. You’re all caught up” makes an empty inbox message playful while telling the user their unread messages are finished. (798 · U2 · L06 · 11:10–13:21)
- The taco reservation chat asks for a day and time, confirms the choice, and ends with a reservation confirmation. (798 · U3 · L09 · 02:40–06:25)
- The Google sign-in error changes from “An authentication error occurred” to “Incorrect password. Try again or recover your password,” adding context and recovery. (798 · U3 · L09 · 08:48–11:50)
- A cheerful “Congratulations!” above an expired-card error is replaced by an explanation and “Try again,” matching the tone to the problem. (798 · U3 · L10 · 02:03–04:22)
- Mailchimp’s “fun but not childish” and “confident but not conceited” examples show how “This, but not this” makes voice attributes more specific. (798 · U3 · L10 · 08:40–13:00)
- The taco reservation app is described as friendly but not overbearing, flexible but not unfocused, fast without neglecting details, and informal without trying to be funny. (798 · U3 · L10 · 08:40–13:00)
- The taco onboarding uses four successive screens to welcome, explain the reservation steps, and ask the user to start the reservation. (798 · U4 · L11 · 03:22–07:26)
- The “Your reservations” empty state says there is no table reserved yet and offers “Reserve a table.” (798 · U4 · L11 · 07:26–10:30)
- Two taco push notifications confirm a reservation or remind a person that an unfinished reservation can be continued later. (798 · U4 · L12 · 03:43–05:50)
- Three taco error examples cover a mistyped email, a reservation outside opening hours, and a lost internet connection. (798 · U4 · L12 · 05:50–10:41)

## Tasks

- **Unit 1 — Share your references:** post books, talks, interviews, or articles that inspire your UX writing. (**U1 unit, Tasks**)
- **Unit 1 — Write an “About us” text:** answer what the product does, what benefit it gives, what problem it solves, and what differentiates it; structure the answers into an introduction in Figma. (**U1 unit, Tasks**)
- **Unit 2 — Define problem and solution:** make two Figma frames, one for the problem the product/service solves and one for how the app solves it. (**U2 unit, Tasks**)
- **Unit 2 — Fill in the proto-persona canvas:** use available research about the people who will use the app. (**U2 unit, Tasks**)
- **Unit 3 — Adapt an error message:** revise “An authentication error occurred” to be clear, then concise, then useful. (**U3 unit, Tasks**)
- **Unit 3 — Make a “This, but not this” list:** write at least 10 paired concepts for the product, hobby, or personal brand. (**U3 unit, Tasks**)
- **Unit 3 — Design a conversation:** use that list to draft a WhatsApp- or Messenger-style welcome that briefly explains what a person can do with the product/service. (**U3 unit, Tasks**)
- **Unit 4 — Write interaction microcopy:** create an onboarding, one screen without content, three errors for different situations, one push notification, one app alert, and one account-creation screen, in Figma frames. (**U4 unit, Tasks**)

## Tool-specific steps

### Figma

- In a frame, create a text block with the text tool (`T`), enter an answer, and optionally change its color; structure the “About me” copy after answering the questions. (798 · U1 · L04 · 00:35–01:11; 798 · U1 · L04 · 01:11–01:51)
- Create a new page with the Pages plus button and name it; create frames for the product problem and solution. (798 · U2 · L07 · 11:40–12:37; 798 · U2 · L07 · 12:37–13:07)
- Create a frame with `F`; use `R` for a rectangle and drag to size/position it, using alignment guides; inspect dimensions when precision matters. (798 · U2 · L07 · 13:07–14:12; 798 · U2 · L07 · 14:12–15:29)
- Use `T` and click or drag to add text; copy a question or style when useful; group selected layers, name the group, and use the Layers panel to find objects. (798 · U2 · L07 · 15:29–16:45; 798 · U2 · L07 · 16:45–18:39; 798 · U2 · L07 · 18:39–19:56)
- Keep exploratory work in a mock/version rather than the main file; use the design tool and file setup that fit the team. (798 · U2 · L07 · 06:45–09:56)
- Draft a user–system chat using duplicated rectangles and text as a working template. (798 · U3 · L09 · 02:40–06:25)
- Build a tone map by ordering key interaction points, setting tone levels, and marking them with smiley faces or Xs. (798 · U3 · L10 · 04:22–08:40)
- Make onboarding screens from rectangles and text; turn groups into frames with **Create frame from selection**, then use **Prototype** to drag a connection from one screen to the next and preview with the play button. (798 · U4 · L11 · 03:22–07:26)
- Build notification examples with rectangles/squares and text to establish information order; they are wireframes, not production notification templates. (798 · U4 · L12 · 03:43–05:50)
- Preview longer localized words in the wireframe to find layout problems before localization restarts. (798 · U4 · L13 · 13:18–14:20)
