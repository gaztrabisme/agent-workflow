# 1490 — Digital Product Design with Lean and UX

## Method steps

The recap presents the course method as an iterative route through immersion, empathy, scope, information architecture, ideation, prototyping/testing, and UI design; the teacher says teams can move between stages and add exercises as they learn. The numbered steps below retain that order while detailing the practices covered in the lessons. (1490 · U8 · L32 · 00:00–01:26; 1490 · U8 · L32 · 01:26–02:23)

1. **Set up the work and align on the brief.** Gather the people involved and clarify their roles, the problem the project should solve, users, objectives, available resources and limits, budget, timing, and milestones. Revise an incomplete brief with the client/stakeholders before treating its requested solution or page structure as settled. Track tasks visibly in prioritized Kanban columns and estimate their effort. (1490 · U2 · L04 · 00:08–02:24; 1490 · U3 · L07 · 02:20–03:26; 1490 · U3 · L07 · 03:26–04:33; 1490 · U2 · L04 · 08:20–10:21)
2. **Research during discovery and as the work continues.** Start by empathizing with users, but also research while defining features, ideating, prototyping, and testing. Observe the problem, form hypotheses, experiment, and analyze evidence before accepting a requested solution. (1490 · U3 · L07 · 01:00–01:39; 1490 · U3 · L07 · 01:39–02:20)
   - Use desk research to collect existing studies, news, books, and essays; use a reference document to capture product screenshots, annotate useful functions and solutions, and end with conclusions and recommendations. Include non-competitors when they offer relevant ideas; limit the number of references and present conclusions rather than every page. (1490 · U3 · L08 · 00:33–01:45; 1490 · U3 · L08 · 01:45–03:14)
   - Use surveys for quantitative questions and interviews for needs, motivations, and explanations. For interviews, set an objective, prepare a short topic guide, ask simple neutral questions, probe with follow-ups such as the Five Whys, and record only project-relevant findings. (1490 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:25; 1490 · U3 · L09 · 02:20–04:02; 1490 · U3 · L09 · 04:02–05:48; 1490 · U3 · L10 · 00:00–00:37; 1490 · U3 · L10 · 00:37–01:15; 1490 · U3 · L10 · 01:15–02:11; 1490 · U3 · L10 · 03:32–04:35; 1490 · U3 · L10 · 04:35–05:18)
   - For contextual observation, define scope, identify users, plan sessions, prepare a guide, observe real tasks in context, take notes, then look for recurring patterns. (1490 · U3 · L11 · 00:00–01:57)
3. **Synthesize evidence and redefine the problem.** Diverge by collecting research and creating options, then converge by choosing and synthesizing evidence. Build an affinity diagram from one concept per note, group similar notes silently, then name groups together; retain source identity and stack repeated findings from different sources. Select the most important findings for a dated research poster that names categories, subcategories, data sources, and user evidence. Use the findings to rewrite the brief around the real problem. (1490 · U3 · L12 · 00:00–01:10; 1490 · U3 · L12 · 01:10–03:41; 1490 · U3 · L12 · 07:53–08:54; 1490 · U3 · L12 · 08:54–10:04; 1490 · U2 · L04 · 05:12–06:48)
4. **Build a working picture of the user.** Make a concise proto-persona from research patterns, with name/nickname, demographic information, behavior, and project-related needs and goals. The teacher allows an initial hypothesis before research results arrive, to be updated later. Use one persona as the subject of an empathy map; fill in what the person needs to do, sees, says, does, hears, thinks/feels, pains, and gains. (1490 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–01:04; 1490 · U4 · L14 · 02:34–04:44; 1490 · U4 · L14 · 07:28–08:17; 1490 · U4 · L15 · 00:00–01:22; 1490 · U4 · L15 · 01:22–01:51)
5. **Map a meaningful user journey and generate opportunities.** Choose a persona and a main action, set out about five to eight steps, and show satisfaction at each step. Mark roughly three or four critical points, frame each as a “How Might We?” question, then generate possible features and solutions without filtering them yet; vote on ideas if working as a group. Use references when the interactions are hard to imagine. (1490 · U4 · L16 · 04:05–05:46; 1490 · U4 · L16 · 05:46–07:24; 1490 · U4 · L16 · 12:51–13:57)
6. **Choose product scope and validate the value proposition.** Define the customer’s jobs, pains, and gains first; then match pains to pain relievers and gains to gain creators, and choose the minimum products/services that can meet the user need. Define an MVP as the smallest version that tests a hypothesis and maximizes validated learning with least effort; keep its core functions well designed. (1490 · U5 · L17 · 02:00–02:48; 1490 · U5 · L17 · 03:34–04:59; 1490 · U5 · L18 · 01:21–02:55; 1490 · U5 · L18 · 02:55–03:52; 1490 · U5 · L18 · 03:52–04:20; 1490 · U5 · L18 · 04:20–04:52; 1490 · U5 · L18 · 04:52–06:00)
   - Express needs as user stories (“As [persona], I want [function] to [goal]”) and Job Stories (“When [situation], I want [function] so I can [goal]”); combine them when the team wants both persona and situational context. Prioritize the functions with MoSCoW and/or the Functionality Matrix. (1490 · U5 · L19 · 00:00–01:27; 1490 · U5 · L19 · 01:27–02:18; 1490 · U5 · L19 · 02:18–02:55; 1490 · U5 · L20 · 00:40–01:57; 1490 · U5 · L20 · 01:57–03:01; 1490 · U5 · L20 · 03:01–03:59)
7. **Organize content and define routes.** Account for context, content, and users; organize and label content so it can be found. Co-design a hierarchical sitemap with stakeholders, then validate whether users can find items in the structure. Make a map before wireframes when content/navigation is extensive; for very simple navigation, a prototype may suffice. Define user flows for important actions, list entry points and landing pages, begin with the happy path, then add branches and include only key screens. If the project starts from scratch, make journeys before flows to discover feature ideas. (1490 · U6 · L21 · 02:45–03:21; 1490 · U6 · L21 · 03:21–04:20; 1490 · U6 · L22 · 01:12–02:20; 1490 · U6 · L22 · 03:48–04:14; 1490 · U6 · L22 · 04:14–04:45; 1490 · U6 · L22 · 04:45–05:28; 1490 · U6 · L23 · 06:28–07:19; 1490 · U6 · L23 · 07:19–08:22)
8. **Ideate before polishing.** Review the research and define the design challenge with the team. In a Design Studio, run Crazy 8s, present and vote on ideas, run a second round from promising ideas, vote again, then develop a selected sketch. In a single Crazy 8s round, fold paper into eight spaces, set five minutes, sketch one idea per space, stop at the timer, and present quickly. (1490 · U7 · L24 · 00:00–01:04; 1490 · U7 · L24 · 01:04–01:25; 1490 · U7 · L24 · 02:34–03:42)
9. **Sketch and wireframe at the fidelity the project needs.** Sketch screens by hand first; use references for familiar patterns. For desktop, the teacher commonly moves from hand sketch to low-fidelity wireframe and then design. For mobile, he generally uses hand sketch to high-fidelity wireframe and then design. Low fidelity uses boxes/lines for structure; high fidelity includes actual text and interaction details while withholding final visual styling. Validate wireframes before applying final UI design. (1490 · U7 · L25 · 00:00–01:00; 1490 · U7 · L25 · 01:00–02:58; 1490 · U7 · L25 · 06:03–06:59; 1490 · U7 · L25 · 06:59–08:20; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 03:13–04:26; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 04:26–05:16; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 05:16–06:30; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 06:30–07:13)
10. **Prototype and test the idea early.** Start with the lowest-fidelity useful prototype. Make only the main interactions needed to test the idea, prepare tasks and a short test guide, explain that the design is being evaluated rather than the participant, invite the participant to think aloud, stay neutral, and test the prototype and tasks in a pilot before sessions. Analyze task behavior and comments, change the solution, and test again with different people. Use high-fidelity wireframes for evaluating usability and interaction design. (1490 · U7 · L29 · 00:00–01:15; 1490 · U3 · L11 · 06:26–09:33; 1490 · U7 · L30 · 00:00–03:44; 1490 · U7 · L30 · 17:02–18:17)
11. **Apply the UI layer after structure is validated.** Add final colors, type, imagery, and visual styling after the wireframe/prototype structure has been approved; the teacher’s recap places UI design after prototyping and testing. (1490 · U7 · L25 · 01:00–02:58; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 03:13–04:26; 1490 · U8 · L32 · 00:00–01:26)
12. **Document the work as a case study.** Explain the context, problem, users, roles, scope and constraints, process and reasons for the exercises, results, conclusions, mistakes, blockers, pivots, and learning; show process before interface screens. (1490 · U8 · L31 · 00:51–02:31; 1490 · U8 · L31 · 02:31–03:07)

## Principles and claims

- Lean UX brings together Agile methods, Design Thinking, and Lean Startup; product design balances user value, business value, and development cost/difficulty. (1490 · U2 · L04 · 02:24–03:22)
- Agile work can move between specification, design, testing, and development in short iterations; learning can change specifications and move the team backward or forward. (1490 · U2 · L04 · 03:22–04:41)
- Lean seeks to add value while reducing waste; removing unused homepage or logo variants is not laziness or permission to make a mediocre product. (1490 · U2 · L04 · 04:41–05:12)
- Design Thinking keeps users at the center, studies their needs, and diverges to explore before converging on a solution; the detailed double-diamond frame marks “designing the right thing” before “designing things right.” (1490 · U2 · L04 · 05:12–06:48; 1490 · U2 · L04 · 05:12–06:48 (frame))
- Lean UX favors small multidisciplinary teams, outcomes over deliverables, eliminating work unrelated to the objective, small packages, continuous learning, making/prototyping early, learning before growth, permission to fail, and avoiding “stars” or gurus. (1490 · U2 · L04 · 06:48–08:20)
- Research is not confined to project kickoff; it can inform feature definition, ideation, prototyping, and tests. (1490 · U3 · L07 · 01:00–01:39)
- Secondary research uses existing published studies and material, is relatively cost-effective, and does not replace research with users. (1490 · U3 · L08 · 00:33–01:45)
- Quantitative research gives counts/statistics; qualitative research probes the quality and reasons behind what people do or feel. Survey numbers can reveal a problem without explaining its cause or solution. (1490 · U3 · L09 · 00:00–01:25; 1490 · U3 · L09 · 01:25–02:20)
- What users say can differ from what they do; interviews need neutral follow-up and interpretation rather than treating an answer as fact. (1490 · U3 · L10 · 02:11–03:11; 1490 · U3 · L10 · 03:11–03:32)
- Contextual observation studies people in their real environment to avoid designing from preconceptions. (1490 · U3 · L11 · 00:00–01:57)
- A usability test checks whether users can complete goals and where they meet obstacles; a user-experience test probes perceived value, emotional connection, motivation, missing value, and feelings. (1490 · U3 · L11 · 01:57–03:08)
- A persona is an archetype based on researched behavior patterns and is a recurring reference for early design decisions. (1490 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–01:04)
- An empathy map is a tool to visualize what the team believes one user thinks; it is not a data-collection method. (1490 · U4 · L15 · 00:00–01:22)
- A journey describes a person’s process and feelings over time; a flow describes routes and branches to finish a task and does not map feelings. (1490 · U4 · L16 · 00:00–01:17; 1490 · U6 · L23 · 07:19–08:22)
- A useful MVP tests whether a hypothesis is valid with minimal effort; it still needs its smallest set of functions to be designed well. (1490 · U5 · L17 · 02:00–02:48; 1490 · U5 · L17 · 03:34–04:59)
- A value proposition should be relevant, credible, and different; its customer value relates the product’s benefit to the sacrifice required to get it. (1490 · U5 · L18 · 00:00–01:21)
- MoSCoW results and Functionality Matrix scores are indicative and negotiable. (1490 · U5 · L20 · 03:01–03:59)
- Information architecture organizes, structures, and labels content so users can find and manage it; it accounts for context, content, and users. (1490 · U6 · L21 · 00:00–01:33; 1490 · U6 · L21 · 02:45–03:21)
- Information can be organized by location, alphabetical order, time, category, or hierarchy; IA uses organization, navigation, labeling, and search systems, with metadata such as titles, descriptions, and authors. (1490 · U6 · L21 · 01:33–02:45; 1490 · U6 · L21 · 03:21–04:20)
- Dan Brown’s eight IA principles shown in the lessons are objects, choice, disclosure, examples, front doors, multiple classification, focused navigation, and growth. (1490 · U6 · L21 · 07:41–09:09; 1490 · U6 · L21 · 09:09–10:38; 1490 · U6 · L21 · 10:38–12:29; 1490 · U6 · L21 · 12:29–12:55)
- Jakob Nielsen’s heuristics ask designers to show system status, use familiar real-world language, enable control/undo, follow consistency and standards, prevent errors, favor recognition over recall, support novices and experts, remove irrelevant content, give clear error recovery, and make help easy to find and useful. (1490 · U2 · L06 · 00:36–01:45; 1490 · U2 · L06 · 01:45–02:31; 1490 · U2 · L06 · 02:31–03:56; 1490 · U2 · L06 · 03:56–05:24)
- Error messages should use clear, simple language, state the problem precisely, and offer a constructive solution. (1490 · U2 · L06 · 02:31–03:56)
- Words in interaction design should be easy to understand and serve a clear function; visual elements should have a purpose and support the words. (1490 · U2 · L05 · 02:52–04:18)
- Design patterns reuse familiar solutions to recurring problems; users spend most of their time on other sites, so familiar conventions reduce relearning. (1490 · U7 · L27 · 00:00–01:53; 1490 · U7 · L27 · 01:53–02:39)
- The first screen or entry page matters, and links/navigation labels should tell people what content they lead to. (1490 · U6 · L21 · 10:38–12:29)
- A survey works best when focused on a few learning goals, around eight questions, clear introductions, balanced answer ranges, labels for numeric scales, and an “Other” option when possible. (1490 · U3 · L09 · 02:20–04:02; 1490 · U3 · L09 · 04:02–05:48; 1490 · U3 · L09 · 06:25–07:51)
- Prototypes may be storyboards, scenarios, films, improvised performance, paper screens, or digital screens, provided they let the team test an idea. (1490 · U4 · L13 · 00:00–01:25; 1490 · U7 · L29 · 00:00–01:15)
- High-fidelity wireframes can carry real content and interactions for testing, but adding their detail takes longer and can distract teams into styling the wireframe. (1490 · U7 · L26 · 03:13–04:26; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 04:26–05:16; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 06:30–07:13)
- Skeleton screens can illustrate interface structure, indicate loading, and show the place/size of an interface element within its page. (1490 · U7 · L26 · 08:12–09:28; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 09:28–10:04; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 10:04–11:05)

## Checks the teacher uses

- **Heuristic evaluation:** Have three to five expert evaluators inspect the interface against general usability rules; score each item, average the results, and add comments when sharing findings. The rendered evaluation can report an overall score out of 100 and category scores; deciding if a criterion is met still requires judgement about the site and its users. (1490 · U2 · L06 · 03:56–05:24; 1490 · U2 · L06 · 05:24–06:48; 1490 · U2 · L06 · 06:48–08:52; 1490 · U2 · L06 · 08:52–09:56)
- **Evaluation categories:** Check features/functionality, home/first screen, navigation, search, feedback, forms, errors, content/text, help, and performance; inspect whether the visible features serve user goals, whether frequent tasks are easy to find, and whether calls to action have clear labels and appear clickable. The presence of category fields, ratings, comments, and buttons/states can be counted or inspected in the rendered artifact; whether they work well is a judgement. (1490 · U2 · L06 · 05:24–06:48; 1490 · U2 · L06 · 06:48–08:52)
- **Error text:** Verify that an error names the problem plainly and provides a constructive next step, such as recovering a username or password. Presence of problem and remedy can be checked in the rendered text; clarity and tone need human judgement. (1490 · U2 · L06 · 02:31–03:56)
- **Survey quality:** Compare each question to the learning goal, target respondents, and what is already known; inspect that answer ranges are balanced and numbers labeled. Count goals/sticky notes and questions (the lesson limits the first quadrant to two or three notes and advises about eight questions); whether wording is neutral and understandable needs judgement. (1490 · U3 · L09 · 02:20–04:02; 1490 · U3 · L09 · 04:02–05:48; 1490 · U3 · L09 · 06:25–07:51)
- **Survey results:** Report response counts and analyze conclusions; the battery example compares selections for technical specs and benefit wording. Response proportions and sample size can be measured from the rendered results; reasons for preference cannot be inferred from those numbers alone. (1490 · U3 · L09 · 01:25–02:20)
- **Interview quality:** Keep the guide short, the language simple, the tone conversational, the interviewer neutral, and the session about 30 minutes; probe with “why” questions. Duration and number of guide topics can be measured; neutrality, empathy, and the usefulness of follow-up questions require judgement. (1490 · U3 · L10 · 01:15–02:11; 1490 · U3 · L10 · 03:32–04:35)
- **User-test outcomes:** Record task results for each participant: green means completed on the first try, yellow/orange means difficult, red means could not complete. Count each color or compute task success by task/person; interpreting why the problem occurred needs observation and judgement. (1490 · U3 · L11 · 09:33–10:48)
- **User-test behavior:** Watch what participants do, how long they hesitate or where they get lost, and give observed behavior more weight than their positive or negative opinion. Task completion/failed paths and interaction events can be counted; whether behavior signals confusion or a design flaw needs judgement. (1490 · U7 · L30 · 00:00–03:44; 1490 · U7 · L30 · 06:50–10:35)
- **Test setup:** Define objectives/type, prototype, about five tasks (three to seven), participant, place/materials, then pilot the complete test. Count tasks and check the guide/prototype are present; whether the tasks faithfully test the hypothesis requires judgement. (1490 · U3 · L11 · 06:26–09:33; 1490 · U3 · L11 · 10:48–13:02)
- **Affinity diagram:** Keep one concept per note, retain source cues if useful, silently group similar notes, and title groups together; check for repeated findings across sources. Note count, number of sources, and labeled groups are measurable in the board; similarity and theme names need collaborative judgement. (1490 · U3 · L12 · 01:10–03:41; 1490 · U3 · L12 · 05:37–07:53)
- **Research poster:** Include a descriptive title/date, categories/subcategories, user statements, and data sources/sample sizes; put key findings first, avoid jargon, and do not spend much time on decoration. Presence of required fields and position of key findings can be inspected; clarity and prioritization need judgement. (1490 · U3 · L12 · 08:54–10:04; 1490 · U3 · L12 · 10:04–11:01)
- **Persona usefulness:** For each feature or screen, ask whether the named person would use it and find it attractive or useful. The prompts can be included in a review record; answering them depends on research-backed judgement. (1490 · U4 · L14 · 00:00–01:04)
- **Empathy-map discipline:** Base it on one persona, work with the team when possible, and set a time limit. Persona presence and session duration can be checked; whether the map reflects what the person may think needs evidence and judgement. (1490 · U4 · L15 · 01:22–01:51)
- **Proto-journey:** Show persona, action, steps, and satisfaction changes; keep to five–eight steps and mark a small number of critical opportunities (three or four in the lesson, three–five in the unit task). Count steps and markers on the artifact; the emotional heights/lows and importance of opportunities need judgement. (1490 · U4 · L16 · 04:05–05:46; 1490 · U4 · L16 · 12:51–13:57; U04-unit.md)
- **Value Proposition Canvas:** Check that jobs, pains, and gains are connected to corresponding pain relievers, gain creators, and minimum products/services. The number and placement of notes can be counted; whether a proposed feature truly answers a user pain or gain needs judgement. (1490 · U5 · L18 · 01:21–02:55; 1490 · U5 · L18 · 02:55–03:52; 1490 · U5 · L18 · 03:52–04:20; 1490 · U5 · L18 · 04:20–04:52; 1490 · U5 · L18 · 04:52–06:00)
- **Feature priority:** Score user benefit, business benefit, and task complexity from 0 to 2; the displayed matrix calculates U + N − T and color-codes the result. The arithmetic, score, and color can be checked in the rendered spreadsheet; the inputs and final negotiation remain judgement calls. MoSCoW asks whether a feature is essential now, important soon, optional within resources, or not worth doing now. (1490 · U5 · L20 · 01:57–03:01; 1490 · U5 · L20 · 03:01–03:59; 1490 · U5 · L20 · 00:40–01:57; 1490 · U5 · L20 · 01:57–03:01 (frame))
- **Sitemap/findability:** Check that page/content hierarchy and navigation are represented, then ask users to find items within the map. Branches and labels are visible/countable; whether people expect or understand a label is judged through users’ attempts. (1490 · U6 · L22 · 00:00–00:50; 1490 · U6 · L22 · 00:50–01:12; 1490 · U6 · L22 · 03:48–04:14)
- **User flow:** List entry points, map the happy path first, add relevant branches, and include only key screens. The rendered artifact can be checked for entry paths/screens/branches; the number of steps needed and whether the route feels simple are assessed by tracing tasks and observing users. (1490 · U6 · L23 · 00:00–01:12; 1490 · U6 · L23 · 06:28–07:19)
- **Wireframe choice:** Check that the selected fidelity suits device and test purpose; low-fidelity is suited to rapid structure work and high-fidelity to mobile/content-rich user tests. Count screens/interaction details and inspect whether final colors/images have been withheld as intended; suitability and readability need judgement. (1490 · U7 · L25 · 00:00–01:00; 1490 · U7 · L25 · 01:00–02:58; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 02:18–03:13; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 03:13–04:26; 1490 · U7 · L26 · 04:26–05:16)
- **Interface words:** Read interface words for easy comprehension and a clear function; use familiar, non-technical language, concise microcopy, and avoid repeated content. A render can be audited for word/line counts and repeated strings; comprehension and voice require judgement with users. (1490 · U2 · L05 · 02:52–04:18; 1490 · U3 · L12 · 08:54–10:04)
- **Simplicity and focus:** Remove irrelevant content, offer a few choices at once, disclose further information when needed, and put the main action/content where users can find it. Element/option counts and visible hierarchy can be inspected in a rendered screen; relevance and ease-of-finding require judgement or user observation. (1490 · U2 · L06 · 02:31–03:56; 1490 · U6 · L21 · 09:09–10:38; 1490 · U6 · L21 · 10:38–12:29)

## Examples

- **Ed and Jim vs. Sue and Ann:** Ed makes detailed comps before validating; Sue aligns on needs and a brief, gets approval on visual direction, sitemap, and wireframes, then tests before final comps. (1490 · U2 · L04 · 00:08–02:24)
- **The product-design Triforce:** UX value, company value, and development cost/difficulty meet around the designer. (1490 · U2 · L04 · 02:24–03:22)
- **Double diamond:** The brief expands through user/competitor/company research, synthesis, redefined brief, idea generation, prototypes, and a tested solution. (1490 · U2 · L04 · 05:12–06:48)
- **Renfe.com heuristic review:** The ticket-search form is prominent, several CTA buttons are visible, and the sample site receives 61/100, classed as moderate; help is the weakest category in the displayed result. (1490 · U2 · L06 · 06:48–08:52; 1490 · U2 · L06 · 08:52–09:56)
- **Battery wording survey:** 78.1% of 32 respondents chose “Up to 10 hours of battery life” over the technical battery specification. (1490 · U3 · L09 · 01:25–02:20)
- **Reading-app reference study:** Goodreads offers genre-based recommendations, Kindle reading detection, social feeds, book/author pages, reviews and ratings; the teacher finds its dense hierarchy dated and noisy. (1490 · U3 · L08 · 04:04–05:54)
- **Cross-category reference borrowing:** Letterboxd’s movie diary/list and cover-browsing ideas, Readwise’s Kindle-highlight review, and BookBuddy’s loan tracking are considered for a reading app despite some products serving different domains. (1490 · U3 · L08 · 06:33–07:06; 1490 · U3 · L08 · 07:06–07:33; 1490 · U3 · L08 · 09:13–09:37)
