## What the owner's work shows

### Palette

- The 2021 Space Race poster uses a dark teal-green star field, charcoal and mauve-grey ground, copper-pink frame lines, and a cream moon and title. The moon and title are the lightest anchors against the dark field (Space Race poster, single-page composition).
- The 2024 submission deck uses a deep indigo field, yellow panels with heavy dark outlines, and bright pink, cyan, violet and green pixel accents (submission deck, slides 1, 8–9, 11–14). The later paired illustrations introduce saturated green and pale mint that do not always connect to the indigo side (slides 10, 13, 15, 17, 19, 21, 23–27).
- Across these two works the palette changes with the chosen world. Working hypothesis: preserve a strong value hierarchy and a small set of linked colors inside each piece, rather than impose one fixed personal palette (poster, full page; deck, slides 1 and 10–27).

### Type

- The poster pairs a wide geometric capital title with smaller sans-serif labels in the HUD, keeping the event title dominant (Space Race poster, single-page composition).
- The deck sets opening and section headings in a pixel display face; the content panels use a rounded geometric sans, with Poppins noted for body copy in the first read (submission deck, slides 1, 7–9, 14). The title lettering is part of the game-world framing, while dense copy is sometimes given more space than it can comfortably use (slide 14).
- Working producer cue: keep display lettering for short titles and transitions; set explanatory copy in a readable sans-serif at a generous size (deck, slides 7–9 and 14). The specific typeface names and fallbacks in tokens are implementation choices, not a verified font inventory.

### Layout and grid habits

- The poster is portrait, centered and close to symmetrical: title at the bottom, moon high on the right, astronaut at lower center, and a geometric frame around the scene (Space Race poster, single-page composition).
- The deck repeats a full-bleed indigo 16:9 field. It uses framed dialogue or content panels, short title cards, diagrams and charts, and later a two-part image-plus-copy layout (submission deck, slides 1–7, 8–9, 10–27).
- The deck's most legible rhythm alternates a setup, an artifact or explanation, and a transition card; the repeated panel is a strong module, but text-heavy panels can become crowded (slides 4–9 and 12–20). No measured underlying grid is visible in the supplied work.
- Working producer cue: make one element dominant, then group details around it; keep negative space intentional, and check that real artifacts remain readable at presentation size (poster, full page; deck, slides 3–6 and 14). This is a synthesis from the examples, not a claim that the owner uses a fixed grid.

### Imagery and illustration

- The poster combines a photobashed rear-view astronaut, a cropped moon, star field, reticle and invented mission-data panel. The viewer reads as a participant looking toward the moon (Space Race poster, single-page composition).
- The deck commits to an 8-bit platform-game setting through pixel clouds, stars, floor tiles, balloons, a sword, controller, medal and trophy (submission deck, slides 1, 8–9, 11–12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22).
- Later deck slides pair AI-generated, polished green token illustrations with adjacent explanatory copy. The image/copy division is clear, though those green images bring a separate palette from the indigo field (submission deck, slides 10, 13, 15, 17, 19, 21, 23–27).
- Working producer cue: choose props that belong to the piece's metaphor, and use interface frames as part of that world. Either treat a contrasting illustration as a deliberate second register or connect it to the surrounding palette (poster, full page; deck, slides 1, 8–9 and 10–27).

### Tone of voice

- The poster's HUD uses invented mission facts, giving a straight-faced science-fiction frame a dry comic edge (Space Race poster, data panel in the single-page composition).
- The deck opens with a direct greeting and uses playful request-like transition cards before the next content section (submission deck, slides 1, 7 and 22). The voice reads conversational and lightly playful; content slides shift to a more instructional register (slides 8–9 and 14).
- Working producer cue: let a small deadpan detail or direct transition carry the playfulness; keep the main explanation plain and specific (poster, data panel; deck, slides 1, 7–9 and 22).

### Motion and pacing

- The supplied poster is static. The PDF is a static export, so it does not establish animation, transition, duration or easing preferences.
- The deck does use narrative time skips and reset cards to signal progression (submission deck, slides 5–7 and 22). This shows sequence-level pacing in the edit, not a motion style.
- Motion values in tokens are chosen starting points only. For a future video, make movement serve the message, carry continuity between scenes, and let reading time set each hold; confirm actual timing with the owner.

## Optional direction from course 2078

These are techniques the owner could choose to borrow. They are course material, not evidence that the owner already uses Art Deco as a signature.

- Bring image, typography and ornament into one visual language while keeping the result contemporary (2078 · U1 · L03 · 00:42–01:26; 2078 · U1 · L03 · 01:26–02:08).
- Limit colors and use bold shapes and strong lines to make a poster read from a distance (2078 · U2 · L04 · 02:14–03:00; 2078 · U2 · L04 · 03:00–04:05).
- Build custom display lettering to match the illustration's stroke weight, then test geometric, cut-out letterforms (2078 · U4 · L13 · 01:19–02:34; 2078 · U4 · L13 · 04:55–08:43).
- Use a symmetrical frame as an organizing structure, then let selected ornaments echo the figure's gesture; the course also edits patterns away from the headline and focal action when they interfere (2078 · U4 · L14 · 02:31–04:56; 2078 · U4 · L14 · 10:10–12:31; 2078 · U4 · L15 · 04:53–07:36).
- Treat the same artwork as a system that can adapt to portrait, narrow and wide formats while protecting its focal figure and title (2078 · U4 · L16 · 01:50–06:22).

## Producer guidance — working hypothesis

- Start with the idea and audience, then choose a visual world for that piece. Make the props, title treatment, palette and framing belong to that world.
- Establish one clear focal element and a readable path to the evidence or payoff.
- Use interface-like frames when they help organize the story. Avoid letting framing or ornament compete with the actual artifact.
- Keep text concise on slides. When the evidence is dense, split it into steps, show the real artifact, or give it a separate page.
- Preserve the ability to change worlds across projects. The possible signature is the way the world is built, not a requirement to repeat the same surface style.
- Where a company or client identity applies, follow it first. Use this profile for choices the governing identity leaves open.
